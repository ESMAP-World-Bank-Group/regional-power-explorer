Fragile and conflict-affected state — military coup July 2023. Niger experienced a military coup on 26 July 2023 when the Presidential Guard (CNSP, led by General Abdourahamane Tiani) ousted elected President Mohamed Bazoum. ECOWAS imposed sanctions, Nigeria suspended electricity exports (previously supplying ~60% of Niger's grid), and France expelled its diplomats in response to the military authorities' demand. Niger subsequently joined the Alliance of Sahel States (AES) with Mali and Burkina Faso. As of June 2026, the military authorities remains in power, Western aid has been suspended or significantly reduced, and the electricity crisis triggered by the Nigerian supply cut has severely damaged economic activity and humanitarian conditions, particularly in Niamey. Sector data post-coup carries high uncertainty. Standard development financing and sector reform approaches are not applicable under the current circumstances.

1. Energy context

Niger has the lowest electrification rate in the WAPP zone — and one of the lowest in the world — at approximately 20% nationally (urban ~72%, rural ~2%). Before the July 2023 coup, Niger depended on Nigeria for approximately 60–70% of its total electricity supply through a 132 kV interconnection (Kainji–Gaya). This extreme import dependency, combined with a fragile domestic generation base of ~370 MW, left Niger entirely vulnerable to the ECOWAS/Nigeria sanctions that immediately followed the coup: Nigeria suspended electricity exports within weeks, causing immediate and severe load-shedding in Niamey (extended to 20+ hours/day) that persisted through 2023–2024.

Niger is also one of the world's largest uranium producers (SOMAIR, COMINAK mines, historically operated by Orano/France). Following the coup, France suspended uranium imports from Niger, and Orano was expelled from its mining concessions in 2024. China's CNPC operates the Agadem oil field and a 20,000 bbl/day refinery, with an associated power plant. The post-coup energy situation is a humanitarian emergency layered on an already-depleted sector.

All fuel is imported by road through Nigeria — a supply chain vulnerable to both the Nigeria relationship and security conditions. Post-coup, fuel import logistics became more difficult under ECOWAS sanctions and road blockages, though informal fuel imports continued.
  • Coal (~18% of domestic generation): A small Chinese-built coal-fired plant (~35 MW) near Niamey and the SORAZ refinery power plant (co-generation, ~30 MW) provide a minor coal share. Coal is trucked from the Anou Araren mine (northern Niger).
  • Gas (~5%): Some associated gas from the CNPC Agadem oil field powers the oil refinery. Limited gas-to-power assets exist outside this captive industrial use.
  • Solar (~2%): Several small solar projects (Agadez 10 MWp, Dosso 5 MW, and others) have been commissioned. The Kandadji Dam hydropower component (~130 MW) was under active construction pre-coup, targeting completion in 2026.

3. Sector organisation

NIGELEC — Société Nigerienne d'Electricité Under Military authorities Control

NIGELEC is the state-owned vertically-integrated electricity utility. Under the post-coup CNSP government, NIGELEC operates under military administration and emergency management following the Nigerian supply suspension. Its pre-coup financial position was already critically weak — chronic underpayment by the state, uncollectable receivables from government agencies, tariffs below cost recovery, and technical losses exceeding 30%. The post-coup crisis has made NIGELEC's situation functionally non-viable without emergency state support and international assistance, much of which was suspended following the coup.

CNPC / SORAZ — Oil and associated power Chinese State Enterprise

China's CNPC operates the Agadem oil block (20,000 bbl/day refinery at Zinder, SORAZ), with an associated power plant providing electricity to the refinery complex. China has maintained relations with the military authorities and provided some diplomatic and economic support, making the CNPC enclave a continued operational reality in an otherwise disrupted sector. The Agadem–Benin crude oil export pipeline (1,950 km) was inaugurated in 2024 under the military authorities, representing a major infrastructure completion that changed Niger's export economics.

4. Key issues and challenges

  • Nigerian electricity suspension — a catastrophic dependency materialised: Niger's pre-coup 60–70% dependence on Nigerian electricity via 132 kV interconnection was always the sector's most acute vulnerability. When ECOWAS sanctions triggered the Nigerian supply cut in July 2023, Niamey experienced 20+ hours of daily load-shedding within weeks. Hospitals, water pumping stations, and markets were severely disrupted. This event represents one of the most vivid demonstrations in WAPP history of what hyper-import dependence means in practice when the political relationship with the exporting country breaks down.
  • Kandadji dam — the domestic supply lifeline: The Kandadji hydropower project (130 MW, on the Niger River, 180 km northwest of Niamey) was begun in 2011 and construction accelerated in 2017–2021 with World Bank and IsDB support. The project is critical: 130 MW would replace a significant portion of the lost Nigerian imports. Construction status under the coup government is uncertain — Gezhouba (Chinese contractor) may have continued work, but the coup-related withdrawal of World Bank financing (loan suspended) creates a financial gap. Completion is the military authorities' stated energy priority.
  • Electrification — the lowest in WAPP: With only 20% national electrification (2% rural), Niger's energy poverty is extreme. There are 24 million people without electricity access. The pre-coup trajectory — supported by World Bank/AfDB off-grid solar and rural mini-grid programmes — has been halted by the suspension of international development financing. The humanitarian cost of this halt is compounded by the post-coup supply crisis.
  • Uranium geopolitics: Niger supplies ~7% of global uranium production. Pre-coup, France (via Orano) was the dominant partner. Post-coup, Orano's mining concessions were cancelled, creating a potential windfall for alternative partners (Russia, China). The uranium sector's energy consumption (mines require significant power) is relevant to NIGELEC's revenue: losing uranium mine customers reduces the utility's most creditworthy load.

5. Five key takeaways

1
Niger's July 2023 coup and the subsequent Nigerian electricity supply suspension is the most dramatic realisation of energy import vulnerability in WAPP history: within weeks, Niamey went from 60% Nigerian supply to 20+ hours of daily load-shedding — a textbook crisis of strategic import dependence in a low-resilience system.
2
With 20% electrification nationally and 2% rural, Niger has the deepest energy poverty problem in the WAPP zone — and the post-coup suspension of World Bank and AfDB off-grid solar programmes has stalled the only realistic path to closing this gap in the near term.
3
Kandadji dam (130 MW) is the single most important energy infrastructure project for Niger's medium-term resilience: it could replace a large share of the lost Nigerian imports and has been under construction for a decade — but World Bank financing suspension and political isolation are creating a serious completion risk.
4
Niger's geopolitical pivot — AES alliance, expulsion of French Orano uranium partnership, Chinese and Russian engagement — is reshaping the energy sector's financial and technical partnerships. The Agadem–Benin crude export pipeline inaugurated under the military authorities represents the one major energy infrastructure success of the post-coup period, diversifying the country's export geography beyond Nigeria.
5
Niger's electricity sector cannot recover under the current political conditions — the combination of military authorities governance, ECOWAS sanctions (even as they soften), Western development financing suspension, and Nigerian supply uncertainty creates a compounding crisis that only a political transition and restoration of international partnerships can structurally resolve.
