Generation Mix & Capacity

Algeria operates approximately 27 GW of installed generation capacity, making it one of Africa's largest electricity systems. Over 98% of generation is gas-fired, supplied from Algeria's vast domestic natural gas reserves — the world's ninth-largest and Africa's second-largest. Renewable capacity stood at approximately 446 MW at end-2025 (mostly solar PV), with ~2.6 GW under construction. Algeria's energy abundance has historically suppressed the urgency of renewables development, but a strategic shift is underway: Sonelgaz announced $4.94 billion in electricity investment for 2025, a 56% increase over 2024, with the first 3,200 MW solar phase expected operational by 2026.

Gas Abundance & the Transition Dilemma

Algeria's gas sector dominates its energy identity. Sonatrach — the national oil and gas company — is the world's 11th largest gas producer and Algeria's largest company by far. Gas revenues fund ~60% of the state budget. This creates a structural paradox for the electricity transition:

  • Algeria has subsidised domestic electricity at near-zero cost for decades — tariffs of $0.01–0.03/kWh among the lowest globally. Electricity is effectively priced as a social entitlement, not a commodity.
  • The opportunity cost of burning domestic gas for electricity — rather than exporting it to Europe at $8–15/MMBtu — is enormous. Each GWh generated from gas domestically forgoes ~$2–5M in potential LNG export revenue.
  • Solar deployment displaces domestic gas consumption, freeing more gas for lucrative European export — making Algeria's renewable energy programme simultaneously a decarbonisation initiative and a gas monetisation strategy.
  • Algeria supplies ~11% of Europe's gas imports (through Medgaz and TransMed pipelines to Spain and Italy). The 2022 European gas crisis elevated Algeria's strategic importance; the revenue windfall has funded Sonelgaz's expanded investment programme.
Algeria's revised renewable target — 15 GW by 2035 (down from 22 GW by 2030) — reflects the tension between ambition and execution speed. The 6,000 MW programmed for 2023–2027 has been slow to materialize; tender processes are often lengthy and opaque. Yet the strategic logic is sound: Algeria has the best solar resource in the world, a vast domestic electricity market to decarbonise, and a gas export revenue incentive that perfectly aligns with solar deployment. The question is institutional, not economic: whether Sonelgaz can execute at the procurement and installation rates required.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Algeria's gas subsidy regime is the primary barrier to renewable energy deployment — not technology, not resource, not finance. When electricity costs consumers effectively nothing (tariffs below $0.02/kWh), the avoided-cost case for solar self-generation is non-existent. Only state-driven utility-scale deployment by Sonelgaz can drive the transition under these conditions. Tariff reform — politically difficult after decades of cheap electricity as a social compact — is the pre-condition for a self-sustaining renewable energy market.
2
The opportunity cost argument for Algerian solar is uniquely compelling globally. No other country combines world-class solar irradiance, a massive domestic electricity market served almost entirely by high-value exportable gas, and a government that is explicitly aware of the gas-for-export logic. Every GWh from solar is a unit of gas freed for European LNG markets at $8–15/MMBtu. At scale, Algeria's solar programme is essentially a $2–5B/year implicit subsidy recapture — the strongest commercial case for solar outside competitive electricity markets.
3
Algeria's 3,200 MW solar Phase 1 (expected 2026) will be the first real test of Sonelgaz's large-scale procurement and execution capability. Previous renewables tenders have been slow and plagued by delays. The DZD 656B 2025 investment budget signals genuine commitment, but Algeria's track record of launching ambitious RE programmes that miss targets (22 GW by 2030 was announced in 2015) requires healthy skepticism. Phase 1 delivery will determine whether Algeria's solar ambitions are credible.
4
Algeria's strategic pivot toward becoming a European green hydrogen exporter faces a 10-year development horizon. Multiple MoUs with European energy companies (notably Italy's ENI and Germany's RWE) have been signed for Saharan green hydrogen production. But electrolyser costs, pipeline conversion for hydrogen blending, and European port infrastructure for ammonia import are all still being developed. 2035 is a realistic first-delivery date for meaningful volumes; 2030 hydrogen export targets are aspirational.
5
Algeria's electricity sector is strategically underappreciated in regional energy discussions. With 27 GW of capacity, potential for hundreds of GW of solar, the EU's largest gas supplier relationship outside Russia, and a 45 million person domestic market, Algeria is the sleeping giant of North African energy. The combination of gas revenues funding a solar buildout that then frees more gas for export creates a self-reinforcing growth loop that could make Algeria one of the world's largest clean energy producers within two decades — if institutional reform allows it.
