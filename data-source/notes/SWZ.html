1. Energy context

Eswatini (formerly Swaziland) is a small landlocked constitutional monarchy (1.2 million population, 17,364 km²) enclosed within South Africa and Mozambique. Its electricity sector reflects its geographic situation: EEC (Eswatini Electricity Company) imports approximately 50% of total consumption from Eskom (South Africa), with the remainder generated domestically from a mix of hydro, sugarcane bagasse bioenergy, and some coal and solar. Eswatini's most distinctive energy characteristic is its substantial sugarcane bioenergy sector: the sugar industry — anchored by Royal Swazi Sugar Corporation, Illovo Sugar, and RCL Foods — generates significant electricity from bagasse (sugar cane waste), making Eswatini one of SAPP's few members with a meaningful industrial bioenergy base.

Like Botswana, Lesotho, and Namibia, Eswatini is heavily exposed to Eskom's load shedding crisis: ZAF Stage 6 load shedding flows directly into Eswatini supply curtailments. The EEC has been investing in solar IPPs and demand-side management to reduce this exposure, but the structural dependency on South African imports is difficult to eliminate quickly in a country with limited land for large-scale renewables and a small electricity market (~1.7 TWh total demand).

  • Hydro (~45% of domestic generation): Maguga Dam (24 MW, on Komati River, shared infrastructure with South Africa) and Mnjoli Dam (20 MW, Mbuluzi River), plus smaller run-of-river stations. Total hydro capacity ~65 MW. Hydro output is seasonal and drought-sensitive.
  • Sugarcane bioenergy / bagasse (~24% of domestic generation): The sugar mills at Simunye (Big Bend) and Mhlume operate co-generation plants burning bagasse during the crushing season (April–November). The Simunye Power Station (45 MW) and Mhlume sugar mill generate electricity from the sugar processing co-product. Bagasse power is seasonal: high output during crushing season, near-zero in off-season. This creates seasonal system management challenges for EEC (importing more from Eskom in the off-season, exporting back in peak crushing season).
  • Coal (~2% of domestic generation): Small coal-fired generating sets. Eswatini's coal resources are limited; coal generation is a residual category from older plant.
  • Solar (~3% and growing): Rooftop solar and small IPP projects (commercial/industrial self-generation) are growing. EEC has developed a net metering framework, and commercial rooftop solar is the fastest-growing segment.

3. Sector organisation

EEC — Eswatini Electricity Company State Utility

EEC (formerly SEC — Swaziland Electricity Company) is the state-owned vertically-integrated utility responsible for bulk power supply, transmission, and distribution in Eswatini. It manages the Eskom bilateral import arrangement (the largest single source of Eswatini's electricity), the domestic hydro and bioenergy portfolio, and the growing solar IPP PPA portfolio. EEC is relatively stable financially by SAPP standards — Eswatini's tariff regime allows closer-to-cost-recovery pricing than many regional peers — but it faces the same structural challenge as Botswana and Namibia: over-reliance on an Eskom import that is increasingly unreliable.

4. Key issues and challenges

  • Eskom dependency — mirroring South Africa's crisis: Eswatini's electricity supply is directly coupled to Eskom's operational performance. Every Stage 6+ load shedding event in South Africa means reduced volumes from Eswatini's largest supplier, with no short-term alternative of equivalent scale. EEC has been investing in solar IPPs and battery storage to provide some buffer, but the fundamental exposure to Eskom remains a structural constraint for the next several years until domestic renewable capacity is substantially expanded.
  • Bagasse seasonality — an opportunity and a grid management challenge: The sugarcane bioenergy sector provides relatively cheap and renewable electricity during crushing season — but goes to zero in the off-season. This creates a pronounced seasonal import pattern: Eswatini is nearly self-sufficient (or even a net exporter) in April–November, then switches to heavy Eskom imports in December–March. Managing this swing with grid stability requires EEC to coordinate with SAPP's regional market mechanisms and potentially invest in storage to smooth the seasonal transition.
  • Solar expansion potential: Eswatini's highlands have good solar insolation (5–5.5 kWh/m²/day). The country's small size and high existing electrification (~68%) mean that a focused utility-scale solar programme of 100–200 MW could substantially reduce import dependence. Several solar IPP projects are in the EEC procurement pipeline. Rooftop commercial solar is growing through private investment. The main constraint is EEC's offtake capacity (its small demand base limits how much IPP capacity it can absorb) and the absence of large-scale storage.

5. Five key takeaways

1
Eswatini's most distinctive electricity sector feature is its sugarcane bioenergy base — the sugar industry generates meaningful renewable electricity from bagasse, making Eswatini a rare SAPP example of agricultural-industrial energy integration. This is a model for other sugar-producing SAPP members (Malawi, Zambia, Zimbabwe) that generate bagasse but do not systematically convert it to power.
2
Like Botswana, Lesotho, and Namibia, Eswatini is a passive casualty of Eskom's load shedding crisis — importing from a supplier whose output is determined by South Africa's political economy of coal plant maintenance and Eskom debt, which Eswatini has no leverage to influence.
3
The bagasse seasonal swing (high domestic generation April–November, near-zero December–March) is Eswatini's most distinctive grid management challenge — it creates a bi-annual import surge in the crushing off-season that is predictable but commercially expensive and requires active SAPP market management rather than simple bilateral contracts.
4
EEC's financial stability (relative to most SAPP peers) and Eswatini's rule of law make it a credible PPA counterparty for private solar developers — a comparative advantage over Zimbabwe, Malawi, and other SAPP members where utility credit risk blocks IPP investment.
5
Eswatini's absolute electricity demand (~1.7 TWh/year) is small enough that a relatively modest renewable programme (100–150 MW solar) could meaningfully reduce its Eskom dependency — making it one of the few SAPP members where energy independence is achievable through achievable near-term investment rather than requiring decade-scale infrastructure transformation.
