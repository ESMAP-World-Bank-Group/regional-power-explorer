1. Energy context

The Gambia is mainland Africa's smallest country by area — a narrow strip of land surrounding the Gambia River, enclosed on three sides by Senegal. Its electricity sector is correspondingly small: a single utility (NAWEC — National Water and Electricity Company) managing approximately 157 MW of mostly diesel/HFO generation for roughly 63% of the population. The sector is acutely vulnerable to global HFO price shocks, which translate directly into NAWEC's fuel costs and the country's import bill.

The arrival of OMVG (Organisation pour la Mise en Valeur du fleuve Gambie) hydropower from Guinea's Kaléta dam in November 2023 marked a transformational moment for the Gambia's electricity sector: for the first time, the country has access to bulk renewable electricity from a regional grid, potentially reducing HFO dependence and lowering generation costs. The addition in 2024 of a 23 MWp solar plant with 8 MWh battery storage further marks a genuine energy transition underway, from a diesel monoculture toward a diversified supply mix.

  • HFO / diesel (historically ~100% of generation): NAWEC operates HFO sets at the Kotu Power Station and Brikama plant, plus a Karpowership floating HFO/LNG unit (~36 MW). All fuel is imported, making NAWEC's fuel bill the dominant operational cost (~60% of total expenditure). HFO price volatility in 2021–2022 severely stressed NAWEC's finances and led to payment arrears. The Karpowership contract terms have been renegotiated multiple times.
  • Solar — new addition (2024): A 23 MWp solar PV plant with 8 MWh battery storage was commissioned in 2024 (World Bank/ECREEE-supported). This is the most significant renewable addition to the Gambia's capacity and will supply Banjul and the Greater Banjul Area with ~30 GWh/year — reducing diesel displacement meaningfully in peak solar hours.
  • OMVG hydro (from November 2023): The Kaléta-to-Gambia 225 kV OMVG transmission line reached limited operational status in November 2023, allowing Guinea's Kaléta hydro power to enter the Gambia for the first time. Initial contracted capacity is ~40–60 MW, with ramp-up as OMVG commercial arrangements are finalised. This is potentially transformational: Guinea's hydro at Kaléta and Souapiti (690 MW combined) is abundant and cheap relative to NAWEC's diesel costs.

3. Sector organisation

NAWEC — National Water and Electricity Company Vertically Integrated SOE

NAWEC is a multi-utility state enterprise responsible for electricity generation, transmission, and distribution, as well as water supply — an integrated utility model that is rare in the region. NAWEC's electricity operations have been chronically loss-making: HFO import costs, tariffs that have not kept pace with costs, low industrial load, and government customer arrears create a structural deficit. Government budget transfers are NAWEC's primary financial backstop. NAWEC has been the subject of multiple World Bank Electricity Restoration and Modernisation Project (ERMP) programmes targeting technical rehabilitation and institutional reform.

Karpowership / NAWEC — Floating Power Unit IPP

Karpowership's floating HFO/LNG power unit (Karadeniz, ~36 MW) has operated in the Gambia under successive contracts since ~2018, providing a significant share of peak capacity. Karpowership contracts are expensive relative to NAWEC's tariff base and have been a recurring fiscal concern. As OMVG hydro and solar scale up, NAWEC will seek to reduce its Karpowership contracted hours or renegotiate on more favourable terms — but terminating the contract early may involve break fees.

4. Key issues and challenges

  • OMVG ramp-up — potential, but commercially complex: The arrival of OMVG power in November 2023 is a landmark, but the actual volume and price at which The Gambia can purchase Guinea hydro depends on OMVG commercial agreements (still being negotiated as of 2024), substation and grid upgrades on the Gambia side, and the financial capacity of NAWEC to pay for bulk power (even at lower prices than diesel). The transition from "OMVG power physically available" to "OMVG power financially contracted and reliably delivered" is a multi-year process.
  • HFO price exposure: As long as NAWEC's dispatch relies heavily on diesel/HFO, global oil price shocks (2021–2022: +50% oil price) translate directly into NAWEC's fiscal crisis. The government absorbs the shortfall through budget transfers, but this creates political economy tensions in a country with very limited fiscal space. OMVG hydro and solar displacement of diesel is the structural fix; the pace of displacement is the key policy variable.
  • Urban-rural access gap: Greater Banjul Area (GBA) has ~69% electrification; rural areas are at ~28%. NAWEC's grid does not extend beyond the western urban corridor in any meaningful way. Rural electrification requires off-grid solar (solar lanterns, mini-grids) funded through PURA, the Public Utilities Regulatory Authority, and NGO programmes. The Gambia's small size means that a unified mini-grid programme could realistically achieve near-universal access in 5–10 years if adequately funded.
  • Tariff reform: NAWEC's electricity tariff (GMD 13.85/kWh ≈ USD 0.24) is one of the higher tariffs in WAPP, reflecting diesel generation costs, yet still insufficient to achieve full cost recovery. PURA has approved periodic tariff adjustments, but these lag cost increases. With OMVG hydro reducing generation costs, there is an opportunity to maintain tariffs at current levels while improving NAWEC's operating margin — a rare positive feedback loop if the transition is managed well.

5. Five key takeaways

1
The Gambia is WAPP's smallest and most isolated electricity market — entirely diesel-dependent until November 2023 — making the OMVG Kaléta interconnection arguably the most transformative single infrastructure event in the country's electricity history since independence.
2
The combination of OMVG hydro (low-cost clean energy at ~40–60 MW) and the new 23 MWp solar plant (2024) means the Gambia could, by 2025–2026, be sourcing 40–50% of its electricity from clean sources — a complete reversal from the 100% diesel baseline of 2022 that no other WAPP member is achieving at comparable speed.
3
NAWEC's integrated water-and-electricity model is administratively convenient for a small country but creates cross-subsidy and accountability complications: water and electricity performance are bundled in a single balance sheet, making reform harder and comparative benchmarking against electricity-only utilities misleading.
4
The Gambia's rural electrification gap (28% rural access) is a solvable problem given the country's small geographic scale — distributed solar mini-grids covering the entire country would require modest capital compared to comparable access programmes in geographically larger WAPP members, and could be completed within 5–7 years with coordinated donor financing.
5
Karpowership is the clearest short-term exit target for NAWEC's cost structure: replacing Karpowership's 36 MW of expensive HFO generation with an equivalent volume of OMVG hydro or additional solar would save NAWEC an estimated USD 10–15M/year — funding that could be redirected to rural access, grid rehabilitation, or tariff relief.
