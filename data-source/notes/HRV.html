Generation Mix & Sector Overview

Croatia's electricity system is among the Western Balkans' cleanest, combining Dinaric Alps hydropower (~45% of generation), onshore wind along the Dalmatian coast (~14%), and a 50% share of the Krško Nuclear Power Plant in Slovenia (~10% of Croatian generation). The country has no coal in its power system and gas provides peak and backup generation (~20%). Solar has grown rapidly since 2022 but from a small base. The dominant utility is HEP (Hrvatska elektroprivreda) — a fully state-owned vertically integrated company operating hydro, thermal, and distribution assets. The TSO is HOPS (Hrvatski operator prijenosnog sustava). Croatia's long Adriatic coastline, Dinaric ridge wind resource, and Mediterranean solar irradiance give it strong RE fundamentals — though it remains a net electricity importer in most years due to seasonal hydro variability.

HEP: state utility with full vertical integrationHrvatska elektroprivreda operates generation (hydro, thermal), transmission (via HOPS subsidiary), and distribution; ~2,000 MW hydro; 50% Krško nuclear; gas CCGT and CHP; 2.3 million electricity customers; revenues ~€3B; expanding solar and wind assets. HEP is Croatia's largest company. State ownership (100%) creates political pricing pressures — Croatia capped electricity tariffs during 2022-23 energy crisis, affecting HEP revenues.
Dinaric hydro cascadeThe Dinaric Alps' karst rivers (Cetina, Krka, Neretva, Zrmanja, Dobra, Mrežnica, Korana, Una) feed Croatia's hydro fleet. Largest plants: Orlovac (237 MW, Cetina), Zakučac (486 MW, Cetina — Croatia's largest), HE Peruča (60 MW), TE-TO Zagreb CHP (440 MW gas+steam). Total hydro ~2,100 MW. Hydro variability is significant: wet years produce 8+ TWh, dry years 5 TWh — driving import volatility. Pumped storage development is planned at Đale on the Cetina (300 MW).
Dalmatian wind: among Mediterranean's bestThe Velebit ridge and Dalmatian coast experience the Bora (NE cold wind) and Maestral (summer NW sea breeze) at sustained high speeds. Wind farms on Velebit and inland Dalmatia have capacity factors of 35-40%. Croatia has ~900 MW onshore wind; pipeline of ~2 GW in various planning stages. Adriatic offshore wind (bottom-fixed in northern Adriatic shallows, floating in deeper south) being studied for post-2030 development.
Krško 2 co-investment (50% Croatia)Croatia approved co-investment in Krško 2 (1150 MW new unit, shared with Slovenia) in 2024. HEP's 50% share (~€4-5B) is Croatia's largest ever energy investment commitment. Financing under development: HEP equity + green bonds + EU fund access. Croatia's NECP 2023-2030 shows Krško 2 as central to its post-2030 clean baseload strategy. HEP's financial capacity is somewhat stronger than GEN energija (Slovenia) — Croatia may carry proportionally more of the early capital burden.
Gas import dependency and Krk LNGCroatia opened the Krk Floating Storage and Regasification Unit (FSRU) in January 2021 — becoming the Adriatic's first LNG import terminal. Capacity: 2.9 BCM/year (expandable to 6.1 BCM). The Krk LNG serves not only Croatia but also Hungary, Slovenia, Austria, and indirectly supplies the Southern Gas Corridor to the Balkans. This gives Croatia significant strategic leverage as a regional gas hub — and has become more valuable since Russian gas supply disruptions post-2022.

The Adriatic Energy Hub Opportunity

Croatia's geographic position — at the intersection of Central Europe and the Western Balkans, with Adriatic coast and LNG terminal access — creates an emerging regional energy hub role that extends beyond electricity:

  • Krk LNG: Southern European gas hub: The Krk FSRU processes LNG from the US, Qatar, and Norway for Croatian domestic use and for pipeline delivery to Hungary, Slovenia, and Austria via the JANAF pipeline system. Croatia has announced plans to expand Krk's capacity and develop an onshore LNG terminal, positioning Adriatic Croatia as the primary gas import gateway for Central and Southeastern Europe — a role Russia's TurkStream pipeline previously filled for part of this market.
  • Western Balkans grid connector: Croatia's HOPS grid interconnects with Slovenia, Hungary, Bosnia & Herzegovina, and Serbia. Croatia is an important transit country for Balkan power flows and plays a balancing role for Bosnia's thermal-heavy system (which exports cheap coal power at night). As Bosnia and Serbia decarbonise, Croatia's hydro flexibility will be increasingly valuable for regional balancing.
  • Adriatic offshore wind: The northern Adriatic (Gulf of Quarnero, Zadar coast) has water depths of 30-80m — suitable for conventional monopile offshore wind. Southern Adriatic depths exceed 200m, requiring floating technology. Croatia has included 1.5 GW of Adriatic offshore in its 2030 NECP — early-stage planning, with no auction completed as of mid-2026.
  • Solar: Mediterranean irradiance advantage: Croatia's Adriatic coast receives 1,400-1,600 kWh/m²/year of solar irradiance — among the highest in the EU. Utility-scale solar is growing rapidly in Dalmatia and Slavonia. Combined with wind, this gives Croatia a RE profile that is less seasonal than Nordic countries — generating meaningfully in winter months when hydro is typically low.
Croatia's energy story in the 2020s is about transformation: from a hydro-and-gas system with Russian energy exposure, to a diversified clean energy hub with LNG import capacity, growing wind and solar, and a major nuclear co-investment commitment. HEP's parallel management of the Krk LNG strategic asset and the Krško 2 co-investment makes it one of the most strategically positioned utilities in the Western Balkans — provided Croatia's government maintains consistent energy policy through the construction decade of the 2030s.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Croatia's Krk LNG terminal — opened in 2021, before Russia's Ukraine invasion made it strategically critical — is now the most important new energy infrastructure built in Southeastern Europe in a decade, providing gas supply security to six countries and directly reducing Russian energy leverage over the Western Balkans. The Krk FSRU was controversial at construction (environmental groups objected; LNG was seen as locking in fossil gas); it became indisputably valuable within 18 months of opening. The terminal's expansion to 6.1 BCM and potential conversion to hydrogen-capable infrastructure makes it a long-duration strategic asset. Croatia's early commitment to LNG import infrastructure is a case study in energy security investment that appeared premature and proved prescient.
2
Croatia's Dinaric hydro cascade — producing 6-8 TWh in normal years from karst rivers that drain the Alps into the Adriatic — is structurally irreplaceable as baseload and balancing capacity for a country with limited alternative storage options, making hydro preservation (not just maintenance, but watershed protection) the most important long-term energy policy priority. Karst hydro is sensitive to precipitation patterns and groundwater recharge; Mediterranean climate warming reduces winter snowpack and summer rainfall — directly threatening hydro output volumes. Zakučac's 486 MW on the Cetina is Croatia's largest generator; its output variability drives Croatia's import/export position more than any other single factor. Climate adaptation planning for Croatian hydro is underinvested relative to its strategic importance.
3
Croatia's decision to co-invest in Krško 2 with Slovenia is the right strategic call for a country with Croatia's resource endowment — but HEP's financing capacity will be tested by simultaneously managing Krk LNG expansion, Krško 2 equity commitment, solar/wind deployment, and grid upgrade requirements across a single decade. HEP's revenue base (~€3B/year) and state ownership give it access to EU and EIB financing — but committing €4-5B to Krško 2 while also investing €1-2B in domestic RE and grid will require substantial debt leverage. Credit rating management, green bond issuance timing, and EU grant maximisation (CEF, InvestEU, RRF) are the critical success factors for HEP's capital programme through 2035.
4
The Dalmatian coast wind resource — Bora-driven, high-capacity-factor, and historically underexploited — represents Croatia's largest single untapped low-carbon generation opportunity, with a realistic pipeline of 3-5 GW onshore + offshore that could transform Croatia from a net electricity importer to a significant exporter within a decade. Croatia's current wind capacity (~900 MW) is small relative to its resource. If the 2 GW onshore pipeline clears planning (sensitive landscapes in Dalmatia complicate permitting) and Adriatic offshore develops from 2030, Croatia could generate 15-20 TWh of wind annually — doubling its current total generation. This would make Croatia a net electricity exporter to Bosnia, Serbia, and Hungary, generating significant export revenue through HOPS interconnections.
5
Croatia's electricity system serves a distinctive demand profile — winter peak from heating, summer peak from Adriatic coastal tourism — that requires flexible generation capacity precisely when hydro output is most variable, making the wind-solar-nuclear combination of the 2030s a structurally better fit for Croatian demand than the current hydro-gas system. Summer tourism demand (Adriatic coast, Istria, Dalmatian islands) spikes in July-August — when solar production is highest. Winter heating demand peaks in January-February — when hydro may be low and nuclear (Krško) provides firmness. Wind fills the spring/autumn shoulder periods when solar and hydro are both transitioning. The Krško 2 + solar + Dalmatian wind portfolio, if fully built by 2038, would match Croatian demand seasonality better than any alternative technology combination.
