Generation Mix & Sector Overview

Malta is the EU's smallest member state by area (316 km²) and one of its most densely populated (~550,000 people, 1,750/km²). Its electricity system is equally constrained: a 200 MW HVAC submarine interconnector to Sicily provides 25-30% of Malta's supply (importing Italian electricity, which includes some RE); domestically, the Delimara power station complex (combined-cycle gas turbine, operated by ElectroGas Malta under a 2015 concession) generates ~65% of Maltese electricity from LNG. Solar PV has grown rapidly (~200+ MW) given excellent Mediterranean irradiance — but Malta's land scarcity limits further ground-mounted deployment, pushing solar onto rooftops, parking canopies, and eventually floating offshore installations. Enemalta — the national utility — remains state-owned but is 33% owned by SGCC (State Grid Corporation of China), an unusual arrangement for an EU utility. The TSO function is managed by Enemalta's grid division. Malta's NECP targets 42% RE by 2030 — highly ambitious given land constraints.

Enemalta + SGCC 33% stake: unusual EU ownershipEnemalta plc; Maltese government holds 67%; Shanghai Electric Power (a subsidiary of China's State Grid Corp SGCC) holds 33% since 2014. SGCC acquisition gave Enemalta access to Chinese financing and technology — controversial in EU context (Chinese state actor in EU critical infrastructure). Renewables mandate for SGCC stake: commitments to invest in Maltese RE. No evidence of security issues to date but EU security regulators monitoring.
Delimara power station (ElectroGas concession)Enemalta contracted ElectroGas Malta (private consortium) to build and operate a 205 MW CCGT + 50 MW OCGT complex at Delimara (SE Malta, 2017) for 18 years. Fuel: LNG supplied via FSRU moored offshore. Malta's energy policy shifted from oil to gas in 2015 via this concession — reducing CO₂ intensity by ~40%. The concession terms (capacity payments, fuel pass-through) have been politically controversial; Maltese Audit Office scrutinised the contract structure. Concession expires ~2035.
Malta-Sicily interconnector (200 MW HVAC)Two 200 MW HVAC submarine cables connect Malta to Sicily (95 km); operated since 2015. Provide ~25-30% of Malta's electricity supply, typically importing Italian gas/RE mix. Cables provide both supply security (if Delimara trips) and market access (Malta participates in the Italian electricity market via the interconnectors). Upgrade to HVDC and/or additional capacity (second interconnector 300 MW, Gozo-Sicily) is planned under EU PCI status — commissioning ~2028-30.
Solar: land-constrained but growingMalta receives ~1,800 kWh/m²/year of solar irradiance — comparable to Cyprus and superior to Spain. Solar has grown to ~200+ MW (2024) and continues to expand rapidly. Constraint: land. Malta at 316 km² with 1,750 persons/km² has very limited land for utility-scale solar farms. Government programme: mandatory solar on new buildings; agrivoltaics over parking; floating solar on reservoirs; coastal floating offshore feasibility study. Target: 1+ GW solar by 2030 — requires creative space utilisation.
Wind: insufficient resource, offshore studyMalta's wind resource is poor by European standards — average wind speeds 4-5 m/s, with seasonal Sirocco (SE, hot/dry from Africa) and Tramontane (N, winter) episodes. No onshore wind farms have been built; land constraints make them impractical regardless. Offshore floating wind (deep Mediterranean waters, 200m+ within 20 km) is being studied — principally for post-2030. Without offshore wind, Malta's 42% RE target is extremely challenging: solar alone (constrained by land) cannot reach 42%.

The Small Island Energy Dilemma at Its Most Extreme

Malta's electricity situation encapsulates every small island energy challenge at maximum intensity — land scarcity, isolation, fossil dependency, high cost, and ambitious RE targets — compressed into Europe's smallest geography:

  • Land scarcity mathematics: To reach 42% RE domestically from solar alone (~2.6 TWh/year at current demand), Malta would need ~1.5 GW of solar PV. At typical ground-mounted density (1 MW/hectare), this requires 1,500 hectares — equivalent to ~5% of Malta's total land area. This is physically implausible given urban density. Rooftop solar (Malta has ~180,000 buildings), agrivoltaics, floating solar on reservoirs and coastal waters, and building-integrated PV are all necessary to approach this target. No single solution is sufficient.
  • Interconnection as the RE strategy: If Malta imports 30-40% of its electricity from Sicily, and that Sicilian electricity is increasingly from RE (Italian solar booming; wind growing), then Malta's effective RE share rises without adding a single domestic RE installation. The second Sicily interconnector (300 MW, Gozo-Sicily, planned) doubles import capacity — and doubles Malta's access to Italian RE. This "virtual RE" via interconnection is Malta's most practical path to 42% RE: buying clean Italian electricity rather than generating all RE domestically.
  • Gas concession timing: The ElectroGas concession expires ~2035. The decision about what replaces Delimara CCGT is Malta's most consequential upcoming energy policy choice. Options: extend the gas concession, build new gas capacity, develop offshore floating wind, install grid-scale batteries + maximal solar, or increase Sicily interconnection to near-100% import dependence. Each option has different security, cost, and climate implications. The decision must be taken by ~2028-30 to ensure construction/procurement lead times are met.
  • Floating offshore wind: Malta's territorial waters extend to 25 nautical miles; the Mediterranean immediately south and west of Malta is 200-500m deep — only accessible via floating wind technology. Malta has included floating offshore wind in its NECP as a post-2030 option; feasibility studies are being conducted. Mediterranean floating wind capacity factors (~30-35%, less than Atlantic) make costs higher than northern European projects — but Malta has no alternative large-scale RE resource.
Malta's energy challenge is a microcosm of the SIDS (Small Island Developing States) problem in EU form: high fossil dependency, land-constrained RE, island isolation, and expensive electricity — but with EU funding access and a Sicily cable that partially solves the isolation problem. Malta is essentially testing whether a micro-island can decarbonise through a combination of maximal rooftop solar, submarine interconnection, and demand response alone — without utility-scale RE generation. The answer, if it works for Malta, has direct relevance for Pacific and Caribbean SIDS that lack even Malta's Sicily cable lifeline.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Malta's 42% RE target by 2030 is the EU's most ambitious RE commitment relative to current starting position and physical constraints — achievable only through a combination of aggressive rooftop solar, imported Italian RE via expanded interconnection, floating solar, agrivoltaics, and demand response, with no single pathway sufficient on its own. If Malta reaches 42% RE, it will have done so through the most creative and multi-modal energy transition in EU history — developing RE policy solutions (agrivoltaics at scale, floating solar, mandatory building-integrated PV) that larger countries with land abundance haven't needed to prioritise. Malta's energy transition is a policy innovation laboratory by necessity.
2
SGCC's 33% ownership of Enemalta — a Chinese state actor in a EU critical infrastructure company — is a security governance anomaly that has attracted increasing EU and NATO scrutiny, and Malta's ability to maintain EU energy regulatory alignment (ACER, ENTSO-E, NIS2) while having a Chinese state partner is being tested as EU-China strategic competition intensifies. The 2014 acquisition predated EU FDI screening regulations. Under current rules (EU FDI Screening Regulation 2019), the same transaction would face significant scrutiny. Malta has maintained that SGCC's stake is commercially passive and that Enemalta's critical infrastructure security is managed by the Maltese state. As EU-China tensions rise (Taiwan, technology controls), the political sustainability of this ownership structure is diminishing.
3
The ElectroGas concession — a private LNG-to-electricity contract that runs to ~2035 — is Malta's single most important energy security arrangement, but its contractual structure (capacity payments, fuel pass-through) insulates the operator from efficiency incentives in ways that the Maltese Audit Office has flagged as poor value for taxpayers. Capacity markets and long-term concessions are a standard tool for island energy security — you pay for backup capacity regardless of whether you use it. But ElectroGas's specific terms (the exact capacity payment level, fuel procurement responsibility, and profit margin) have been questioned. The concession's 2035 expiry is an opportunity to redesign Malta's gas backup arrangements with better competitive tension and efficiency incentives.
4
Malta's decision to build the second Sicily interconnector (300 MW, Gozo-Sicily) is the most leverage-efficient energy investment available to the island: each MW of interconnector capacity provides more energy security, RE access, and price reduction per euro invested than any domestic generation asset that Malta could build. For a 550,000-person island with no large RE resource, interconnection is not supplementary to the energy strategy — it IS the energy strategy. A 500 MW total interconnection capacity (after the second cable) would allow Malta to meet 50%+ of annual demand from Sicily imports, effectively plugging Malta into Italy's rapidly decarbonising grid at a fraction of the cost of building equivalent domestic capacity.
5
Malta's energy story has direct relevance for World Bank SIDS clients in the Caribbean and Pacific: it demonstrates that a small island can partially decarbonise through interconnection and rooftop solar even without offshore wind or large utility-scale RE — but also that without interconnection, even excellent solar resources (Malta's irradiance) cannot drive deep decarbonisation on an isolated gas-dependent grid. The Caribbean SIDS (Haiti, Jamaica, Trinidad, Eastern Caribbean states) face identical structural constraints with worse fiscal capacity and no Sicily cable equivalent. Malta's policy experiments in mandatory building solar, agrivoltaics regulation, and floating solar feasibility — conducted with EU funding — are directly transferable as technical assistance for ESMAP's SIDS energy work, adjusted for local context.
