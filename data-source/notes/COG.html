Generation Mix & Capacity

The Republic of Congo (Congo-Brazzaville) has approximately 830 MW of installed generation capacity, split between gas-fired thermal plants around Pointe-Noire and hydroelectric plants serving Brazzaville and the centre of the country. The dominant asset is the CEC (Centrale Electrique du Congo) — an 450 MW gas plant at Pointe-Noire, 80% state-owned and 20% Eni — which alone represents over 55% of national capacity. Congo has the Congo River basin's vast hydropower potential at its doorstep but has developed barely 5% of it.

The Two-Grid Problem

Congo-Brazzaville operates two completely separate, unconnected electricity grids — a fundamental structural constraint on the sector:

  • Northern Grid (Brazzaville & centre): Powered by Imboulou, Moukoukoulou, and Djoué hydro plants plus Brazzaville gas peakers. Serves the capital and interior towns. Heavily hydro-dependent and vulnerable to seasonal river level fluctuations.
  • Southern Grid (Pointe-Noire & coast): Dominated by the CEC gas plant; serves the economic capital, the Congo-Ocean railway corridor, and coastal towns. Separate from the northern grid entirely.

There is no transmission backbone connecting Brazzaville (north) and Pointe-Noire (south) — despite these being Congo's two largest cities. Building this link is a strategic priority in the government's Vision 2030 plan, but financing (~$500M+) has not been secured.

Congo has a paradox at the heart of its electricity sector: it sits adjacent to the Congo River — the world's second-largest by discharge and an extraordinary hydropower resource — yet only 5% of its 3.9 GW national hydro potential has been developed, and its two grids cannot even exchange power with each other. The gap between resource and delivery is institutional and financial, not technical.

Market Structure & Regulation

  • SNE (Société Nationale d'Electricité): State utility; historically responsible for generation, transmission, and distribution; now operates alongside CEC for generation and increasingly relies on private/PPP structures for new investment.
  • CEC (Centrale Electrique du Congo): The 450 MW Pointe-Noire gas plant; 80% SNE/state, 20% Eni; the country's most commercially significant generation asset.
  • ANRE: National regulatory authority; limited capacity; tariff-setting constrained by political economy of subsidised electricity.
  • China engagement: The proposed Sounda Gorge HPP involves China Overseas and is premised on Chinese sovereign financing — continuing Congo's pattern of large infrastructure financed by Chinese state capital.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
The Brazzaville–Pointe-Noire transmission link is the single most important energy infrastructure investment Congo can make. Connecting the two grids would allow CEC gas capacity to balance hydro droughts in the north, and Imboulou/Moukoukoulou hydro to reduce gas burn in the south — improving both resilience and economics for the entire system.
2
Congo has vast hydro potential that is almost entirely undeveloped. 3.9 GW technically exploitable, 5% built — the Kouilou River basin (including the Sounda site) represents a transformative opportunity. If Sounda (600–800 MW) is built and the grid interconnector constructed, Congo could become a significant CAPP pool exporter.
3
The CEC gas plant is Congo's most reliable electricity infrastructure and a model for gas-to-power. At 450 MW it provides more capacity than all of Congo's hydro plants combined. Its Eni partnership demonstrates the viability of private co-investment in Congolese power generation — a template that other projects could follow.
4
Congo's 2030 target to double capacity to 1.5 GW is ambitious but technically achievable. The 70% gas / 30% hydro split envisaged means primarily expanding CEC-type gas projects in the south and incremental hydro in the north. The binding constraint is financing and project preparation capacity — both of which require institutional reform at SNE and ANRE.
5
Rural electrification is lagging severely. With ~50% national electrification and the rural rate far lower, Congo-Brazzaville has a significant energy access gap despite oil and gas revenues. Solar mini-grids for rural communities — as deployed in other oil-exporting Central African states — could address this gap faster and cheaper than grid extension in most areas.
