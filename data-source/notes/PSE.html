Structural Constraints of Occupation

Palestinian electricity is uniquely shaped by the political economy of occupation. The Oslo Accords (1993) and the Paris Protocol (1994) created an electricity governance framework in which:

  • Israel controls the frequencies, voltages, and technical standards of Palestinian electricity infrastructure — effectively preventing Palestinians from connecting to non-Israeli grids without Israeli approval.
  • Area C (60% of the West Bank, under full Israeli civil and military control) requires Israeli permits for any electricity infrastructure construction — permits systematically denied or delayed for Palestinian projects, particularly solar and transmission lines.
  • The PA's $750M+ debt to the IEC has been used as leverage — Israel periodically deducts electricity debt from customs revenues it collects and transfers to the PA, directly affecting the PA budget and fiscal sovereignty.
  • Gaza's blockade (since 2007, tightened after 2023) has prevented fuel imports, equipment imports, and repair materials from reaching GEDCO — keeping the Gaza Power Plant operating at minimal capacity even before total war-related destruction.
Palestine's electricity sector cannot be meaningfully discussed outside the context of occupation. The system is not merely impoverished or mismanaged — it is structurally dependent by design on Israeli infrastructure and subject to Israeli control at every level, from frequency standards to import permissions to debt management. Every technical recommendation — more solar, better distribution, new generation — runs into the same constraint: Palestinian authorities cannot build the infrastructure they need without Israeli approval, and that approval is systematically withheld in ways that serve Israel's strategic interests.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Gaza's electricity infrastructure requires complete reconstruction — estimated at $2B+ — before any normal development conversation is possible. With 70% of GEDCO's grid destroyed, 5,000 km of cable gone, and 2,200 transformers destroyed, Gaza's electricity system cannot be repaired incrementally. It must be rebuilt from scratch under whatever political arrangement follows the current war. The reconstruction design — centralised vs. distributed, Israeli-compatible vs. independent — will be shaped by the political settlement, not technical preference.
2
Solar energy offers West Bank Palestinians the only viable path toward electricity independence from Israel — but Area C restrictions severely constrain deployment. Solar projects in Area A and B (Palestinian civil control) can be developed more freely; projects in Area C require Israeli permits. Since most of the West Bank's best solar land is in Area C, and most transmission corridors cross Area C, Palestinian solar development is structurally constrained even where technically feasible. IFC's West Bank solar programmes operate in a narrow permissible space.
3
The Palestinian Authority's electricity debt to IEC is a tool of financial coercion that undermines Palestinian fiscal sovereignty. The $750M+ debt accumulates because JDECO and other distribution companies cannot collect all their revenues from consumers (many of whom are also in financial distress) and from Palestinian municipalities that refuse to pay. Israel deducts from customs clearance revenue — reducing the PA budget directly. This debt cycle keeps Palestinian electricity authorities financially dependent on Israeli institutional goodwill.
4
Post-war Gaza's energy reconstruction will be the largest humanitarian energy project since Afghanistan — and requires an entirely different governance framework from West Bank development. Gaza's post-war electricity system will need to be designed for maximum resilience against future attacks — distributed solar + battery systems at the community level, hardened substations, redundant supply routes. A centralised system dependent on any single infrastructure (power plant, supply line, substation) can be instantly disrupted. Distributed resilience is not an ideological preference but a physical security imperative.
5
Palestine's energy situation is one of the most extreme cases globally of electricity being used as a coercive instrument of political control. Restricting electricity is cheaper, less visible, and more deniable than kinetic violence — yet more pervasive in its effects on civilian life. The systematic use of electricity control (fuel blockade, supply line cuts, permit denials for infrastructure) as part of a broader strategy of pressure on Palestinian civilian populations represents a category of infrastructure warfare that international humanitarian law is only beginning to adequately address.
