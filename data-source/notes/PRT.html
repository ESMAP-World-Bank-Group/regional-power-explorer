Generation Mix & Sector Overview

Portugal has one of the most advanced renewable electricity systems in Europe — generating 65.8% of electricity from renewable sources in 2024, led by hydro (30%), wind (21%), and the fast-growing solar fleet (14%). The country has no nuclear power and has pursued a renewables-first strategy for two decades, driven by its excellent Atlantic wind resources, Douro and Tagus hydropower, and some of Europe's best solar irradiance south of Lisbon. Portugal participates in the MIBEL Iberian electricity market with Spain and exports significant volumes in high-hydro or high-wind years. The national utility is EDP (Energias de Portugal) — now a global renewables company with assets in Brazil, the US, and across Europe.

Atlantic Wind and Deep-Water Offshore

Portugal's most distinctive energy asset is its Atlantic coastline — one of Europe's windiest, with annual average wind speeds exceeding 8–9 m/s off the north coast. This creates both opportunities and challenges:

  • Onshore wind maturity: Portugal has ~5.8 GW of onshore wind — among the highest per-capita in Europe. Galicia-border regions and inland Serra da Estrela ridges host dense wind farms. The onshore resource is largely tapped; growth is now offshore.
  • Deep water challenge: Unlike the UK, Denmark, and Germany (shallow North Sea), Portugal's Atlantic shelf drops steeply to 200m+ within 50 km of shore. Conventional bottom-fixed monopile or jacket foundations are economically viable only within a narrow coastal strip. Floating offshore wind is the only scalable technology for Portuguese offshore — making Portugal both a pioneer and a higher-cost deployer than Northern European neighbours.
  • Aguçadoura floating wind (200 MW): First large-scale floating offshore project in development; EDP + Principle Power; planning consent proceeding; would be the world's largest floating wind farm on commissioning (~2028-30); critical demonstration project for Portuguese and global floating wind industry.
  • Hydro variability: Portugal's heavy reliance on hydro (30%) creates significant year-to-year generation variability linked to Atlantic rainfall patterns. Drought years (2022 was severe) can cut hydro output 40-50%, requiring emergency gas generation or imports from Spain. Pumped storage (Alqueva, Alto Lindoso) partially mitigates this by storing water from wet seasons.
Portugal represents one of the clearest examples of a medium-sized European economy that committed to renewables early, built a globally competitive utility (EDP), and is now positioned to benefit from the hydrogen export opportunity. The challenge is scale: Portugal's domestic electricity system (50–60 TWh demand) is too small to justify the billions in offshore and electrolysis infrastructure alone. The H2Med pipeline and the export revenue from green hydrogen to Germany and France are what make the economics work — which makes the pipeline a strategic imperative, not just an infrastructure choice.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Portugal's 65.8% renewable electricity in 2024 — achieved without nuclear, in a medium-income economy — is proof that the energy transition is not limited to rich, industrialised Northern European states. Portugal's renewable journey began in the 1990s with hydro and onshore wind, long before solar costs collapsed. The country now has three mature RE technologies (hydro, wind, solar) and is pioneering a fourth (floating offshore). This multi-technology, phased development approach — building capability sequentially rather than trying to do everything simultaneously — is a model for emerging market energy transitions.
2
EDP's transformation from a domestic utility to one of the world's top renewable energy companies — while remaining majority state-linked — is the most successful European energy utility transformation of the past two decades. EDP had to internationalise (Brazil, US, Spain) to find growth markets when the Portuguese domestic market was too small. Its renewable pivot preceded most European peers by 5-10 years. The result: EDP Renováveis operates in 28 countries, has a market cap comparable to large European utilities 5× its size, and is now a credible global decarbonisation partner. Portugal's energy policy success is partly EDP's corporate success.
3
Portugal's floating offshore wind pioneer status — through Windfloat Atlantic — positions it to capture disproportionate technology and industrial value from a market that could exceed 300 GW globally by 2050. Floating wind is the technology that unlocks offshore wind for 80% of the world's coastlines (where water is too deep for conventional foundations). Portugal, Japan, Norway, and the UK are the primary floating wind pioneers. Portuguese expertise in Atlantic deep-water operations, mooring systems, and umbilical cables — developed through oil and gas in the 1970s-90s — creates a genuine technological head start. Exporting floating wind technology is as valuable as the electricity it generates.
4
Portugal's hydro-dependent renewable system is its primary resilience risk — and the increasing frequency of Mediterranean drought events under climate change makes this risk grow over time. A 40% reduction in hydro output (as in 2022) cannot be compensated by onshore wind alone. Gas import reliance during droughts is expensive and politically difficult for a country positioning as a green energy leader. The solution — more pumped storage, more solar, more demand flexibility — is known but requires sustained investment. Climate-driven variability in Portugal's hydro resource is a slowly-building systemic risk that needs explicit planning.
5
Sines port's transformation from coal terminal to hydrogen export hub is Portugal's most strategically important infrastructure investment for the next decade — and its success depends on German and French industrial decarbonisation demand materialising at scale by 2030. Sines already handles LNG imports and has infrastructure for bulk liquid and gas handling. The pivot to hydrogen export requires €1–2B of upgrade investment for electrolysis, liquefaction or ammonia conversion, and pipeline connectivity. If H2Med is built and European industrial hydrogen demand develops as projected, Portugal captures significant export revenue from its RE surplus. If either the pipeline or the demand fails to materialise on schedule, the Sines investment risks stranding.
