Generation Mix & Capacity

Nepal has 3,422 MW of installed electricity capacity as of March 2025, of which an extraordinary 95% (3,256 MW) is hydropower. The country has transformed over the past decade from chronic load-shedding (up to 18 hours/day in 2015) to a net electricity exporter, as a wave of run-of-river and storage hydropower plants came online. The milestone of becoming a net exporter to India — earning NPR 17.5 billion from electricity exports in FY2024-25 — marks the most consequential shift in Nepal's energy economy in its history.

NEA & Market Structure

  • NEA (Nepal Electricity Authority): Vertically integrated state utility; generation + transmission + distribution; the key institution in Nepal's power sector; improving financial position as export revenues grow.
  • IPPs: Private hydropower IPPs have driven most capacity additions in the 2010s and 2020s; they sell power to NEA under PPAs; sector increasingly relies on private developers for new projects while NEA manages the grid.
  • IPPAN: Independent Power Producers Association of Nepal; represents a large and growing IPP community.
  • Interconnections: Multiple 220 kV and 132 kV cross-border lines to India (PGCIL–NEA); capacity being expanded under the Power Trade Agreement. A new 400 kV cross-border line is under development to increase export capacity.
  • Bangladesh link: Nepal now exports ~40 MW to Bangladesh via India's grid — the first South Asian tri-country electricity trade arrangement.
Nepal's transition from 18-hour daily load shedding in 2015 to net electricity exporter in 2025 is one of Asia's most dramatic energy transformation stories. The key was a wave of run-of-river hydropower projects by private IPPs, many financed by the IFC and development banks, that added ~2,000 MW in roughly eight years. Nepal's 42,000 MW theoretical hydro potential — less than 10% developed — means this is only the beginning of what could become a major regional clean energy export economy.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Nepal's electricity export to India is transforming its geopolitical and economic relationship with its southern neighbour. For decades, Nepal imported electricity from India in the dry season and was a net electricity debtor. Now Nepal earns NPR 17.5 billion annually from exports and has approval to export 1,010 MW. This shift — from dependency to partnership — strengthens Nepal's negotiating position across bilateral relations beyond energy.
2
The seasonal mismatch between Nepal's run-of-river hydro and India's demand pattern is the central technical challenge. Nepal's rivers peak in the monsoon (June–September) when India's demand is partly met by its own hydro. Nepal's dry-season (November–May) output is lower — exactly when Nepal needs to import from India. Storage hydropower (multi-year reservoirs like Upper Arun, Dudhkoshi, Budhigandaki) would shift Nepal's generation profile toward dry-season export, which commands premium prices. Nepal's pipeline of storage hydro is critical to maximising export value.
3
The 10,000 MW by 2029 target is achievable only if transmission infrastructure keeps pace with generation. Nepal is adding ~500–800 MW/year in new hydro capacity; the bottleneck is increasingly cross-border transmission capacity to India, not the hydro projects themselves. The planned 400 kV cross-border line and transmission corridor upgrades are critical path items; delays will strand generation capacity that Nepal has invested to build.
4
Upper Tamakoshi's landslide vulnerability is a warning about Nepal's infrastructure risk. The September 2024 landslide that knocked Nepal's largest power plant (456 MW) offline for 88 days caused a significant supply shortfall and economic loss. As climate change increases extreme rainfall events in the Himalayas, hydropower plants in steep river gorges face escalating geological risk. Disaster resilience — in plant design, access road redundancy, and rapid repair capacity — must be incorporated into all future projects.
5
Nepal's emergence as a power exporter creates a new model for Himalayan small states. With Bhutan already exporting ~70% of its hydro production to India, and Nepal now joining as a net exporter, the Himalayan corridor from Bhutan to western Nepal is becoming the renewable energy backbone of South Asian electricity trade. If Pakistan ultimately connects via India, this corridor could evolve into a South Asian Green Energy Belt — replacing the region's coal dependency with low-cost Himalayan hydro.
