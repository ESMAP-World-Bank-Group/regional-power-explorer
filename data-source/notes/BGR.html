Generation Mix (2024 estimates)

Bulgaria's generation profile is anchored by two nuclear units at Kozloduy and a large lignite complex in the Maritza East basin, supplemented by hydro and rapidly growing renewables. The country generates far more power than it consumes domestically, exporting 8–12 TWh per year across five border interconnections.

Market Structure & Regulation

Bulgaria joined the EU in 2007 and operates within the ENTSO-E Continental Europe synchronous area. The power sector is partially liberalised: a wholesale day-ahead market via IBEX (Independent Bulgarian Energy Exchange, launched 2016) coexists with regulated tariffs for households and certain industrials.

  • Regulator: EWRC (Energy and Water Regulatory Commission) — sets network tariffs, approves generation licences, oversees retail.
  • TSO: ESO EAD (Electroenergien Sistemen Operator) — state-owned; operates transmission, manages balancing, interfaces with ENTSO-E.
  • DSOs: EVN Bulgaria (Austrian), CEZ (Czech, largely acquired by Électricité de France subsidiaries), and Elektrohold (private).
  • Wholesale market: IBEX day-ahead and intraday; Bulgaria participates in the EU single day-ahead coupling (SDAC).
  • Vertically integrated legacy: National Electricity Company (NEK EAD) remains dominant in hydro, wholesale import/export, and as single buyer for regulated consumers.
Bulgaria's electricity prices have historically ranked among the lowest in the EU — a consequence of cheap Maritza lignite and fully amortised nuclear assets at Kozloduy. EU carbon pricing (ETS) is now rapidly eroding this advantage and making all coal plant uneconomical.

Nuclear — Kozloduy & the Belene Question

Kozloduy NPP

Kozloduy Nuclear Power Plant, located on the Danube in north-western Bulgaria, is the country's single largest source of electricity. Two operational units — Unit 5 and Unit 6 (each ~1 000 MW VVER-1000, Soviet design) — operate under Kozloduy NPP EAD, a state-owned company. Four older VVER-440 units (Units 1–4) were closed by 2006 as a condition of EU accession, removing ~1 760 MW from the system.

  • Unit 5 licence: expires 2027 — life extension application under review by nuclear regulator BNRA.
  • Unit 6 licence: expires 2030 — life extension also being pursued.
  • Unit 7 (new build): 1 000 MW AP1000 reactor with Westinghouse Electric Company. Framework agreement signed; FID expected circa 2026. Would lock in western technology and cement nuclear at >40% of generation.
The Unit 7 AP1000 decision is the single most consequential energy investment Bulgaria faces. It represents a geopolitical as much as an economic choice — moving firmly from Russian reactor technology toward a US-designed platform, with implications for Bulgaria–Russia relations and long-term fuel supply chains.

Belene NPP — the unresolved question

The Belene site, also on the Danube ~100 km east of Kozloduy, has been intermittently developed since 1981. Two VVER-1000 reactors were ordered from Russia's Atomstroyexport; the contract was cancelled in 2012 after political reversals, triggering a €600 million arbitration award paid by Bulgaria to Atomstroyexport. Pre-fabricated reactor vessel components remain on-site.

  • Revival proposals resurfaced in 2018–2020 but were blocked by parliament.
  • Approximately €2 billion of sunk costs (site, components, legal costs) make the project politically impossible to formally abandon.
  • Post-2022, any Russian involvement is politically toxic; western investor interest (including a brief EDF/Framatome approach) has not materialised into a viable consortium.
  • Status as of 2026: formally suspended, but kept alive by political factions and energy security arguments around growing demand.

Coal & the Just Transition Challenge

The Maritza East lignite complex in the Stara Zagora region is the backbone of Bulgaria's thermal generation but its future is acutely threatened by EU carbon pricing. The three main plants together represent the largest source of CO₂ emissions in Bulgaria.

The complex directly employs approximately 15 000 people and indirectly sustains much of Stara Zagora's regional economy. Bulgaria has submitted a Just Transition Territorial Plan under the EU Just Transition Mechanism (JTM), but critical questions around retraining, alternative economic activity, and a binding coal phase-out date remain unresolved.

Under EU ETS Phase 4, carbon prices above €60–80/t make Bulgarian lignite uncompetitive against gas and increasingly against renewables. All three Maritza plants are economically marginal or loss-making at current carbon prices. The question is no longer whether they close, but when and at what social cost.

Renewables — Accelerating Growth

Solar PV and wind have grown rapidly since 2020, driven by falling technology costs, EU taxonomy alignment, and the push to reduce coal dependence. Bulgaria's renewable potential is significant: the south has high solar irradiance and the northern Danubian plain offers good wind resources.

Gas & Energy Security after 2022

Bulgaria was among the EU member states most dependent on Russian natural gas: before 2022, Gazprom supplied over 90% of Bulgaria's gas via the TurkStream pipeline and the Ukrainian transit route. In April 2022, Gazprom cut supplies to Bulgaria after the government refused to pay in roubles — making Bulgaria one of the first EU states to be cut off.

  • Immediate response: Emergency imports via Greece (Alexandroupolis LNG terminal, IGB interconnector) and reverse flow from Romania.
  • TurkStream continuation: Despite the cutoff, Bulgaria remains a transit corridor for Russian gas to Serbia and Hungary via the TurkStream/BalkanStream pipeline — a politically awkward dependency that has continued under successive governments.
  • IGB interconnector: The Interconnector Greece–Bulgaria (3 bcm/yr capacity) opened in October 2022, providing diversification via Azerbaijani gas (TAP/TANAP) and LNG regasification in Greece.
  • Alexandroupolis FSRU: The floating LNG terminal in northern Greece, operational from 2024, provides an additional regional supply source accessible via IGB.
  • Power sector impact: Gas-fired generation (~900 MW) plays a balancing and backup role. Gas price volatility in 2021–2023 reduced dispatch and highlighted the value of nuclear baseload and storage hydro.

Cross-Border Interconnections

Bulgaria's grid position — between the Balkans, Türkiye, and the Romanian hub — gives it strategic trading value. All interconnections operate within the ENTSO-E Continental Europe synchronous area. Exports flow primarily to Greece and Türkiye; imports are rare except during hydro drought years.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Kozloduy Unit 7 (AP1000/Westinghouse) is Bulgaria's most consequential energy decision. It would cement nuclear at >40% of generation, lock in western technology, and mark a definitive break from Soviet-era reactor platforms — with major implications for Bulgaria–Russia relations and regional energy security.
2
The Maritza coal complex is Bulgaria's most politically difficult energy problem. Cheap lignite power for decades, now a stranded asset and Just Transition liability: ~15 000 direct jobs, an entire regional economy, and no credible managed exit plan. EU carbon pricing is already making the economics untenable.
3
Bulgaria is Southeast Europe's structural electricity exporter — its grid position between Romania, Greece, and Türkiye gives it durable trading value. Growing renewables will reinforce rather than diminish this role, provided grid integration investment keeps pace.
4
The 2022 gas crisis exposed Bulgaria's acute vulnerability to Gazprom. The April 2022 supply cut — the first in the EU — revealed how limited Bulgaria's diversification options were. The IGB interconnector and Alexandroupolis FSRU have improved the picture, but TurkStream transit dependency and political ambiguity persist.
5
The Belene question will not go away. With ~€2 billion in sunk costs, Russian arbitration compensation already paid, pre-fabricated reactor components on-site, and rising power demand projections, Belene remains a live political issue regardless of official suspension — and will resurface with each election cycle.
