1. Energy context

Sierra Leone has one of the smallest and most fragile electricity systems in the WAPP zone, a legacy of the brutal civil war (1991–2002) that destroyed much of the country's infrastructure. The Freetown grid — essentially the only grid of substance — relies on the Bumbuna hydropower plant (50 MW, 160 km northeast of Freetown), several diesel thermal plants operated by EDSA/EGTC, and occasional backup from the CLSG interconnection with Côte d'Ivoire. With an electrification rate of approximately 30%, Sierra Leone's energy poverty remains extreme: most rural areas and even much of Freetown's peri-urban sprawl have no grid access.

The sector is at a pivotal moment: Bumbuna II (143 MW, Joule Africa/MIGA-supported), a second stage of the Bumbuna Falls hydro complex on the Seli River, is nearing completion and would more than triple Sierra Leone's existing hydro capacity. Additionally, the CLSG interconnection corridor (Côte d'Ivoire–Liberia–Sierra Leone–Guinea) would bring Ivorian power to Freetown when the Sierra Leone section is complete. These two projects together could transform Sierra Leone's electricity landscape — assuming they can be financed, completed, and operated effectively by EDSA/EGTC.

  • Hydro (~45% of domestic generation): Bumbuna Falls hydropower plant (50 MW, commissioned 2009, rehabilitated with World Bank support) is Sierra Leone's primary generation asset, located on the Seli River in Tonkolili District. Bumbuna is a seasonal plant — output is high during the rainy season (May–October) and can drop to near-zero in the dry season (January–April), requiring full diesel backup. The dam also plays a water supply and irrigation role.
  • Diesel/HFO (~55% of domestic generation): EGTC (Electricity Generation and Transmission Company) operates several diesel/HFO sets in Freetown: Blackhall Road (60 MW), Charlotte Street, and Wellington plants, many of which are aging, poorly maintained, and fuel-hungry. Diesel import costs are a major drain on the sector's finances. Emergency rental units (Aggreko) have been deployed multiple times.
  • Solar (minor): Small rooftop and off-grid solar programmes in provincial towns (NPA networks) and several NGO-funded rural solar mini-grids. Utility-scale solar remains minimal but is under development as part of new IPP frameworks.

3. Sector organisation

EDSA — Electricity Distribution and Supply Authority Distribution (Freetown)

EDSA is the state entity managing electricity distribution and retail supply in the Western Area (Freetown and environs). EDSA has approximately 100,000 customers in its service area — a small fraction of Freetown's 1.2 million population. Its collection efficiency is low (~60%), technical and commercial losses are high (~40%), and it is financially dependent on government subsidies. EDSA's pre-pay metering rollout (underway since 2018) has improved collection performance in areas covered but has not yet reached most customers.

EGTC — Electricity Generation and Transmission Company Generation + Transmission

EGTC manages electricity generation (including Bumbuna and the Freetown thermal plants) and the 66/33 kV transmission network. Separated from EDSA as part of a vertical unbundling attempt to create clearer accountability and attract private investment. EGTC has been the implementing vehicle for the Bumbuna II project (under a build-own-transfer-type arrangement with Joule Africa). EGTC's financial position is also stressed — fuel payments for Freetown thermal plants consume most of its revenue.

NPA — National Power Authority Rural (outside Freetown)

The National Power Authority manages electricity supply outside the Western Area — essentially independent mini-grid operations in Bo, Kenema, Makeni, and smaller towns. NPA operations are very small-scale: most provincial towns receive only a few hours of electricity per day from ageing diesel units. NPA is chronically under-resourced and fuel payments are routinely in arrears. Rural electrification beyond NPA towns is served by NGO-funded solar lanterns and mini-grids.

4. Key issues and challenges

  • Bumbuna hydrology and dry season vulnerability: Bumbuna I (50 MW) is a seasonal plant that provides near-zero output in the January–April dry season. Bumbuna II (143 MW) will have similar seasonality. This means that even with 193 MW of hydro installed, Sierra Leone will still require substantial diesel backup capacity in the dry season — reducing (but not eliminating) the fuel cost burden. A reservoir regulation strategy for both dams together could extend seasonal output, but this requires coordinated operational management that EGTC currently lacks capacity for.
  • CLSG section — the blocking gap: Sierra Leone is both the beneficiary and the bottleneck of the CLSG ring: it needs the interconnection to receive CIV power and improve supply, but its section of the 225 kV line (linking from Liberia at Ganta to Freetown) is the last to be completed. AfDB financing is in place, construction is underway, but delays in procurement, community compensation, and RoW clearance have repeatedly pushed back the timeline. When complete, CLSG could supply Freetown with 50–100 MW of CIV hydro power at prices likely below EDSA's current diesel cost.
  • Post-conflict institutional weakness: Sierra Leone's electricity sector never fully recovered from the 1991–2002 civil war — EDSA and NPA inherited a devastated infrastructure and have operated in a resource-constrained environment ever since. Technical staff capacity remains below adequate levels, and institutional reform (the EDSA/EGTC unbundling, pre-pay metering, regulatory establishment) has proceeded slowly against this backdrop. International donor patience with Sierra Leone's reform pace is a recurrent challenge.
  • Mining sector demand: Iron ore mining (Marampa, Tonkolili by SL Mining) was a potential large-scale electricity customer before operations were mothballed (2015 commodity price crash, COVID-19, market disruptions). If iron ore mining restarts at scale, it would create a major industrial load that could dramatically change the economics of grid investment and Bumbuna II/CLSG utilisation.

5. Five key takeaways

1
Sierra Leone has one of the smallest and most fragile electricity systems in the WAPP zone — 0.21 TWh of domestic generation for a population of 8+ million — a direct legacy of the 1991–2002 civil war that destroyed grid infrastructure and has never been fully restored despite 20+ years of peace.
2
Bumbuna II (143 MW) is the single most transformational project available to Sierra Leone's electricity sector — tripling hydro capacity and potentially eliminating most diesel dependence in the rainy season — but its dry-season output limitation means diesel backup will remain essential year-round.
3
Sierra Leone is the blocking node in the CLSG ring: the CIV–Liberia–Guinea 225 kV line is partially operational, but the Sierra Leone section's delays prevent the full ring closure that would let cheaper CIV hydro power flow to Freetown — making SLE's CLSG section completion the most urgent transmission priority in WAPP's western sub-region.
4
EDSA's pre-pay metering rollout is one of the few reform success stories in a sector otherwise characterised by stagnation — pre-pay has improved collection efficiency and reduced commercial losses in covered areas, demonstrating that governance improvements can work even in a resource-constrained environment if implementation is sustained.
5
Iron ore mining restart (Marampa/Tonkolili) could be the exogenous demand shock that transforms Sierra Leone's electricity economics from a public welfare problem to a commercially attractive market — one large creditworthy industrial customer changes the entire investment case for Bumbuna II, CLSG, and private generation.
