1. Energy context

Armenia is a landlocked country of 3 million people with two closed borders — Türkiye and Azerbaijan — since the early 1990s. 78.6% of total primary energy supply was imported in 2020, dominated by Russian natural gas (59.6%) via a single corridor through Georgia. Armenia is the only nuclear power producer in the South Caucasus — a key strategic differentiator.

The electricity sector is the one segment where Armenia achieves effective autonomy: it is a net exporter, operates a diversified nuclear-hydro-gas mix, and has carried out a substantial market reform since the mid-1990s — moving from a near-bankrupt Soviet-era monopoly to a partially privatised market.

2.3 Grid and interconnections

The HV grid (220–500 kV) is operated by HVEN CJSC (state). Network losses ~11.7% (2023). The grid has two notable topological features: the backbone runs North–South connecting Metsamor (west), the Hrazdan cascade (north-central) and the Vorotan cascade (south), with the main load centre in Yerevan in the middle. The system is asynchronously connected to both Georgia and Iran — i.e. not synchronised to either the ENTSO-E grid or the Iranian grid — which limits the speed and volume of cross-border power exchange and requires dedicated back-to-back conversion capacity. The eastern half of the ring (Azerbaijan border) is physically de-energised. Cross-border links:

2.4 Cross-border trade arrangements

🇮🇷
Iran — gas-for-electricity barter Structural

In place since around 2004 and periodically renewed. Iran delivers approximately 365 million m³ of natural gas per year; Armenia returns 3 kWh of electricity per m³ received — around 1.1 TWh per year, 88% of total exports. No cash changes hands; pure in-kind exchange managed by HVEN (Armenia) and TAVANIR (Iran). The implicit gas price works out to approximately $165–180 per thousand m³ — roughly 10–15% below the Russian Gazprom rate, providing a meaningful economic hedge. The gas feeds the Yerevan CCGT and Hrazdan Unit 5, whose output is re-exported to Iran in a closed circuit. The third 400 kV line, once completed, will double the volume capacity to ~1 200 MW.

🇬🇪
Georgia — seasonal bilateral trade Opportunistic

Bidirectional flows governed by annual contracts between HVEN and ESCO (Georgia's market operator), not a long-term structural agreement. Armenia exports in winter when gas and nuclear capacity runs above domestic demand, and imports Georgian hydro in summer. Net annual volumes are modest — around 0.15 TWh — at negotiated prices broadly in the $30–45/MWh range depending on season. No long-term pricing formula.

3. Sector organisation and actors

3.1 Market structure

Vertically separated but fully regulated. No spot market, no retail competition. PSRC sets all tariffs. HVEN operates as single buyer — procuring from generators under annually approved quota contracts at regulated prices, then dispatching to ENA for distribution.

4. Key issues and dynamics

4.1 Metsamor — the nuclear succession question

  • VVER-440 V230, commissioned 1980. One of the oldest operating NPPs globally. Life extended to 2036 via $65M Rosatom contract (Dec 2024). Currently in maintenance shutdown Apr–Sep 2026 — the main operational risk of the year.
  • New NPP: dedicated company created early 2025. PM Pashinyan confirmed SMR decision Oct 2025 — simultaneous talks with US, France, Russia, China, South Korea. Supplier choice = geopolitical litmus test between Moscow, Washington and Paris.

4.2 Renewables acceleration

  • Target: 66% renewables by 2036 (from 7% in 2012). Requires 2 185 MW new capacity.
  • Solar PV at ~15% of 2025 generation (~800 MW), feed-in tariff driven. Wind near-zero despite 4 900 MW potential.
  • World Bank least-cost plan (2024): 2 630 MW PV + 375 MW wind + 580 MW hydro + 130 MW storage + 600 MW NPP by 2033. Total ~$13bn.

4.3 ENA nationalisation — 2025 governance shock

  • June 2025: Tashir owner Karapetyan arrested. July 3: Parliament votes. November 2025: PSRC revokes ENA licence.
  • ~1M subscribers, 5 TWh/yr, 47–54bn AMD investment programme under temporary administrator. Final structure unclear — main governance risk for 2025–2026.

4.4 Supply security

  • Gas corridor risk: single Russia–Georgia pipeline. Any disruption → reliance on hydro + nuclear only.
  • Nuclear dependency: 5-month shutdown requires compensating via imports.
  • Infrastructure vulnerability: Sep 2022 Azerbaijani strike on the Armenia–Iran HV line as precedent.
  • Mitigants: ~3 200 MW installed capacity vs ~1 700 MW peak demand; solar growth reducing thermal exposure.
A normalisation with Türkiye — whose peak load exceeds 58 GW — would be the single most transformative development for Armenia's long-term energy security. It remains a geopolitical question, not a technical one.

5. Five things to take away

1
Metsamor is irreplaceable short-term. Its Apr–Sep 2026 maintenance shutdown is the main operational risk of the year. Any extension becomes a supply crisis.
2
The Iran barter is Armenia's most valuable bilateral energy arrangement — cheaper gas, Gazprom hedge, strategic depth. The third 400 kV line will cement it.
3
ENA's nationalisation resolves a political problem but opens an operational transition of uncertain duration — the main governance risk for 2025–2026.
4
The new NPP supplier decision is the decade's defining energy choice — and a geopolitical litmus test between Moscow, Washington and Paris.
5
Solar growth is real, but the 66% target by 2036 requires ~$13bn, a functional DSO, and wind development that has not yet started. All three are uncertain.
