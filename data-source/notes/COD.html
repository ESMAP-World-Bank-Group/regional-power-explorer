1. Energy context

The Democratic Republic of Congo presents one of the most dramatic paradoxes in global energy: it sits above the Congo River basin — home to an estimated 100 GW of untapped hydroelectric potential, roughly 40% of the total hydro potential on the African continent — yet only 22.1% of its 100+ million people have access to electricity. An estimated 79.6 million Congolese lack electricity, the largest unelectrified population in Africa and one of the largest in the world.

The physical infrastructure that does exist is concentrated on the Inga hydropower complex on the Lower Congo River: Inga I (351 MW nameplate) commissioned in 1972 and Inga II (1,424 MW nameplate) commissioned in 1982. Together they represent the backbone of DRC's electricity supply. But decades of under-investment, war, and institutional failure have reduced their effective output to roughly 40–60% of nameplate capacity. SNEL, the state utility, operates with USD 3 billion in debt, commercial losses of ~46%, and retail tariffs of ~USD 0.06/kWh that cannot cover operating costs.

The promise of Grand Inga — up to 40,000+ MW across multiple phases, which would be the largest hydropower complex on earth — has loomed over DRC's development narrative for decades. Most recently, China Three Gorges (the world's largest hydropower company) withdrew from Grand Inga in January 2025 after a six-year agreement collapsed over financing, equity, and power allocation disputes. The World Bank approved a USD 250 million concept note for Phase 1 rehabilitation in June 2025, reopening an IFI-led path — narrower in scope but potentially more credible than the Grand Inga mega-project.

Electricity tariff crisis: SNEL charges approximately USD 0.06/kWh — below the regional average and well below the true cost of supply (~USD 0.12–0.15/kWh). This pricing, coupled with ~46% technical and commercial losses and poor billing collection rates (~55–60%), makes SNEL's financial position untenable without continuous state transfers. Total SNEL debt is estimated at USD 3 billion. Any rehabilitation or expansion financing requires a tariff reform pathway, which has been politically blocked. The World Bank's 2021 USD 500 million Inga rehabilitation loan included tariff reform conditionality with partial implementation only.

2.4 Grand Inga — the long-stalled megaproject

Grand Inga — China Three Gorges withdrawal (January 2025) Stalled

The Grand Inga project — phased development of up to 40,000+ MW on the Lower Congo River — has been the single most-discussed energy project in Africa for 30 years. Inga III (Phase 1, 11,000 MW) was to be the entry point. A 2018 agreement with China Three Gorges (CTG) and the Chinese government was intended to deliver Inga III by the late 2020s. The agreement collapsed in January 2025, officially due to disagreements over equity stakes, power pricing, South African offtake commitments (Eskom could no longer guarantee the off-take required to make the project bankable), and the DRC government's demands for local content and project control.

CTG's withdrawal leaves a USD 14–80 billion project (cost estimates vary enormously by scope) without a lead developer. Attempts to attract alternative Chinese consortia, Australian investors, and US developers have not materialised into binding commitments. The project requires a single creditworthy anchor off-taker for financing to close — currently lacking.

World Bank Phase 1 rehabilitation — USD 250M concept approved (June 2025) In preparation

Following CTG's withdrawal, the World Bank approved a USD 250 million Phase 1 concept note in June 2025 focused on rehabilitating Inga I and II (restoring ~500 MW of effective capacity) rather than building new greenfield capacity. This approach is more modest in scope but also more credible: it addresses deferred maintenance, replaces aging turbines, and builds toward restoring nameplate capacity at existing dams. It builds on the earlier 2021 USD 500 million Inga rehabilitation programme. The phased IFI-led approach signals a strategic pivot from the Grand Inga mega-project to incremental rehabilitation — less transformational, but far more likely to actually happen.

3. Sector organisation and actors

3.1 Market structure

DRC's electricity sector is dominated by SNEL (Société nationale d'électricité), a fully vertically integrated state monopoly handling generation, transmission, and distribution. SNEL was restructured nominally under the 2014 Electricity Act, which opened the sector to IPPs and created the framework for an independent regulator (ARE), but implementation has been slow. ARE is nominally operational but remains under-resourced and politically subordinate.

In practice, the DRC electricity sector operates as a fragmented multi-tier system: SNEL serves Kinshasa and provincial capitals via long-distance HVDC/AC transmission; provincial utilities (REGs) serve smaller cities under SNEL sub-concession agreements; industrial and mining companies self-generate; and informal diesel micro-grids serve peri-urban areas outside SNEL reach. Grid access to rural DRC (78% of population) is effectively zero.

4. Key issues and dynamics

4.1 The Inga paradox — 100 GW potential, chronic blackouts

  • The Congo River carries more water than any river on earth except the Amazon, and its near-constant flow (no seasonal monsoon dependency) makes it the world's premier site for large-scale hydropower. The total exploitable potential is estimated at 100 GW — enough to power the entire African continent. Yet today, the actually operational output of the Inga complex is roughly 900–1,100 MW effective — less than 2% of potential.
  • The gap between potential and reality is entirely attributable to institutional failure: SNEL's financial bankruptcy, deferred maintenance since the 1990s, political patronage in utility management, and the inability to create a creditworthy off-take framework for new investment. The physical resource is world-class; the governance challenge is profound.

4.2 SNEL's financial crisis

  • SNEL's ~USD 3 billion debt (largely to the state, suppliers, and Chinese construction loans) represents an overhang that precludes normal commercial financing. The utility's annual revenues (~USD 400–500 million at current tariffs and billing rates) fall far short of cost recovery (~USD 700–800 million). The gap is filled by state transfers that crowd out other public investment.
  • The 2021 World Bank USD 500 million operation included a partial tariff reform — raising industrial tariffs toward cost-reflective levels while keeping household tariffs subsidised — but political resistance limited implementation. Full reform is a prerequisite for any large-scale financing of Inga rehabilitation or expansion.
  • High system losses (~46% including technical losses and non-payment) are both a cause and symptom of the financial crisis: low tariffs reduce incentives to invest in loss reduction; deferred maintenance increases technical losses; poor billing systems allow non-payment to continue.

4.3 Mining sector — the demand anchor for Grand Inga

  • DRC is the world's largest cobalt producer (~73% of global supply) and a major copper producer. The Katanga/Lualaba mining belt is the primary industrial electricity consumer — and the only plausible anchor off-taker for a major expansion at Inga. Without a bankable long-term industrial PPA, Grand Inga cannot reach financial close.
  • Kamoa-Kakula (Ivanhoe Mines, the world's second-largest copper mine by resource and fastest-growing by output) is increasingly self-sufficient in electricity: it rehabilitated Mwadingusha hydro (150 MW, 2024) under a direct agreement with SNEL, bypassing the unreliable grid. This trend — major miners self-generating — reduces the commercial incentive for Inga investment and signals the absence of trust in SNEL's reliability.
  • South Africa's Eskom was the original anchor off-taker envisioned for Grand Inga III — able to absorb ~2,500 MW via a dedicated HVDC line through Zambia and Zimbabwe to South Africa. Eskom's own financial crisis and political opposition within South Africa effectively removed it as a credible counterparty by 2022, materially contributing to CTG's January 2025 withdrawal.

4.4 Rural electrification — the 1.1% challenge

  • Rural electrification in DRC stands at approximately 1.1% (World Bank 2023) — among the lowest rates on earth. With ~70 million rural Congolese and a national grid that barely reaches provincial capitals, the grid extension model is functionally inapplicable for the vast majority of the country for the next several decades.
  • Mini-grids and off-grid solar are the only credible near-term access vector. Power Africa, the World Bank, and multiple bilateral donors are funding mini-grid pilots in the Kivu region, Kasai, and along the eastern border. Progress is real but orders of magnitude below the scale needed.

5. Five things to take away

1
DRC holds roughly 40% of Africa's total hydroelectric potential (~100 GW) on the Congo River — enough to power the entire continent. Yet only 22% of Congolese have electricity and the two operational Inga dams run at 40–60% of nameplate capacity. This is not a resource problem; it is an institutional, financial, and governance failure of extraordinary scale.
2
Grand Inga — after decades of false starts and the January 2025 withdrawal by China Three Gorges — is essentially in a reset phase. The World Bank's June 2025 Phase 1 rehabilitation concept (USD 250 million) represents a more modest but more realistic path: restore the existing dams rather than build 40 GW of new capacity. This shift in strategy matters for how donors, financiers, and regional buyers should calibrate their expectations.
3
SNEL's USD 3 billion debt, 46% system losses, and ~USD 0.06/kWh retail tariff form a vicious cycle that no donor can sustainably finance around. Any meaningful improvement at Inga — whether rehabilitation or expansion — requires tariff reform and institutional restructuring as prerequisites. Political economy has blocked this for 25+ years; the IMF and World Bank continue to attach conditionality that sees partial implementation at best.
4
The mining sector (Kamoa-Kakula, Glencore Katanga) is the economically dominant electricity consumer — and is increasingly self-generating. Ivanhoe's rehabilitation of Mwadingusha (150 MW) outside SNEL's system is a rational response to SNEL's unreliability but also a signal: even the most creditworthy potential anchor off-taker no longer trusts the utility. Without a credible industrial off-taker, Grand Inga cannot be financed at any scale.
5
Rural electrification in DRC at 1.1% requires off-grid and mini-grid solutions — grid extension is not economically viable for the ~70 million rural Congolese on any relevant planning horizon. The eastern Kivu region has the most active mini-grid development; the Ruzizi III project (EGL, AfDB/EIB, 147 MW) is the highest-impact near-term project for the Great Lakes sub-region. For the rest of the country, off-grid solar is the only scalable path.
