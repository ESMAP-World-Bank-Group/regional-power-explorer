Generation Mix & Capacity

Equatorial Guinea has approximately 349 MW of installed capacity across two geographically separated grids — Bioko Island (capital Malabo) and the mainland region (Bata). Gas-fired plants — burning associated gas from the Gulf of Guinea offshore fields — account for roughly 67% of generation (~0.98 TWh/yr), with hydropower at 33% (~0.48 TWh/yr) and negligible oil. The Djibloho HPP (120 MW) — commissioned in 2012 on the Wele River in the mainland interior — was the country's biggest clean energy addition; the larger Sendje HPP (200 MW) downstream is now under construction.

The Bioko–Mainland Grid Split

Equatorial Guinea's electricity system is divided into two structurally separate grids with different supply profiles:

  • Bioko Island grid (Malabo): Powered primarily by gas plants fed by associated offshore gas; relatively reliable supply in the capital; benefits from proximity to the LNG complex at Punta Europa.
  • Mainland grid (Bata region): More dependent on hydro (Djibloho, and eventually Sendje); also has gas backup; mainland electrification rate significantly lower than Bioko; significant rural population without grid access.

Bioko Island and the mainland are physically separated by 20+ km of ocean, making a grid interconnection between them extremely costly. Each operates independently; shortfalls on one cannot be balanced from the other.

Equatorial Guinea's oil-funded economy has not translated into comprehensive electricity access. Despite being one of sub-Saharan Africa's highest-income countries per capita (from oil), the national electrification rate sits at ~67% and rural mainland access remains far lower. The same governance patterns that have concentrated oil wealth in a narrow elite have limited investment in public infrastructure, including electricity.

Market Structure & Key Actors

  • SEGESA (Sociedad de Electricidad de Guinea Ecuatorial): State national utility; operates through three subsidiaries — SEGESA Generation, SEGESA Transmission, SEGESA Commercial. Monopoly on all power sector activities.
  • Ministry of Mines and Hydrocarbons: Oversees both the oil & gas sector and energy policy; electricity sector governance is closely tied to hydrocarbon policy.
  • GEPetrol: National oil company; expanded role following ExxonMobil's 2024 exit from the Zafiro field; gas supply for power generation is partly dependent on associated gas from GEPetrol-operated or joint-venture fields.
  • International oil companies: Marathon and Chevron remain the primary offshore operators producing associated gas used in Bioko Island power generation.

Sendje HPP — The Sector-Defining Project

The Sendje Hydroelectric Project (200 MW) on the Wele River downstream of Djibloho is the most important near-term energy investment in Equatorial Guinea. When complete, it will:

  • Nearly double the country's hydropower capacity (from 120 MW to ~320 MW hydro)
  • Shift the mainland grid from gas-dominant to hydro-dominant generation — significantly reducing fuel costs and import dependency
  • Improve supply reliability in the Bata region and create capacity for expanded rural electrification
  • Reduce the sector's vulnerability to gas supply disruptions from aging offshore infrastructure

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Sendje HPP (200 MW) is the single most transformative energy project Equatorial Guinea has underway. When completed, it doubles hydro capacity and could shift the generation mix to majority-renewable — a significant step for a country whose power sector has been historically locked into gas. Commissioning timeline and construction progress are the key metrics to watch.
2
The ExxonMobil exit (2024) creates uncertainty about long-term gas supply for Bioko power generation. Associated gas from offshore fields is the fuel source for Bioko's gas plants; as IOC positions change — ExxonMobil out, GEPetrol expanded — the reliability and cost of gas supply to SEGESA's Bioko plants will evolve. This is particularly relevant as offshore fields mature and gas volumes decline.
3
The Bioko–mainland separation is a structural constraint that no affordable technology can overcome. The 20+ km ocean gap means both islands/regions must be self-sufficient in generation. Any supply crisis on one cannot be relieved by the other. This argues for diverse, resilient generation portfolios on each side — not a single dominant source.
4
Equatorial Guinea's electrification gap is primarily political, not technical. At ~67% national access in a high-income-per-capita country, the gap is a reflection of governance priorities rather than resource constraints. Oil revenues are sufficient to fund universal access; the question is whether political will exists to prioritise it.
5
SEGESA's vertically integrated monopoly limits private investment in the sector. Without IPP frameworks, competitive generation procurement, or regulatory independence, Equatorial Guinea cannot attract the private capital needed to diversify beyond gas and hydro into solar and storage — technologies that would be well-suited to both Bioko and the mainland given their equatorial solar resource.
