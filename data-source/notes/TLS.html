Generation Mix & Sector Overview

Timor-Leste (East Timor) differs fundamentally from the Pacific atolls in this briefing series: it is a mountainous Southeast Asian half-island of 14,874 km² (sharing the island of Timor with Indonesia's West Timor), with 1.3 million people, significant topographic relief (Mt. Ramelau, 2,963 m), rivers, potential hydro resources, and a Petroleum Fund accumulated from offshore oil and gas production. Its electricity sector challenges are nonetheless serious: a colonial and post-conflict infrastructure legacy, chronic generation plant unreliability, reliance on expensive diesel, a utility (EDTL) with significant governance and technical capacity constraints, and a looming fiscal crisis as the Petroleum Fund — the source of nearly all government revenue — depletes following the exhaustion of the Bayu-Undan gas field.

The Electricidade de Timor-Leste (EDTL), the state electricity utility established at independence in 2002, operates the national grid from the Hera Power Station (Dili vicinity, approximately 130 MW HFO/diesel installed capacity, effective ~80 MW in 2024 due to plant aging and fuel supply), an aging transmission network connecting Dili to district capitals, and a series of district-level diesel generators in areas not yet connected to the national grid. Total installed capacity (all sources): approximately 600 MW nominal — but this is highly misleading. Approximately 250-350 MW of this is "installed" in the sense of having nameplate capacity on registered plant; effective available capacity has ranged from 80-150 MW due to plant outages, fuel shortages, and transmission constraints. Dili (population ~300,000) experiences approximately 4-12 hours of load shedding daily depending on season and plant availability.

Data quality caveat: EDTL's generation and capacity statistics are not reliably reported in a consistent international format. Installed capacity figures (variously reported as 300-600 MW) conflate nameplate rating of aging, partially functional plant with effective available capacity. Access rate (~80%) reflects electrified households rather than hours of supply — many "connected" households in district towns receive 8-12 hours/day. Bayu-Undan depletion and the Petroleum Fund fiscal cliffThe Bayu-Undan gas and condensate field (operated by ConocoPhillips then Santos, Timor Sea) began production in 2004 and was the source of virtually all Timor-Leste government revenue until approximately 2022-2023, when the field was effectively depleted. At peak (2010-2015), Bayu-Undan revenues of US$1.5-2B/year funded 95%+ of the government budget, accumulated in the Timor-Leste Petroleum Fund (peak ~US$17B in 2012). The Petroleum Fund is constitutionally protected — withdrawals above the "Estimated Sustainable Income" (ESI, ~4% of fund value annually, ~US$560M at current fund level) require parliamentary approval. With the fund at approximately US$14B in 2024 and declining as withdrawals exceed investment returns, Timor-Leste faces a decade-level fiscal adjustment: by approximately 2035, the Petroleum Fund will be reduced to the point where ESI withdrawals can no longer fund current government spending levels. Every year of delay in developing Greater Sunrise — or in building domestic non-oil tax revenue — shortens the adjustment window.
Greater Sunrise: the contested development pathwayThe Greater Sunrise gas field (Timor Sea, approximately 150 km south of Timor-Leste), operated by Woodside (Australia), contains approximately 7 trillion cubic feet of gas — one of the largest undeveloped gas fields in the Asia-Pacific region. The development pathway has been contested for 20+ years: Australia and Woodside prefer a floating LNG (FLNG) or pipeline to Darwin, Australia (shorter distance, lower cost); Timor-Leste insists on a pipeline to the Timorese south coast for onshore LNG processing at Tasi Mane (a planned south coast industrial hub). The core issue is not just commercial: Timorese development of Tasi Mane would create domestic industrial employment, onshore gas processing capacity, and potentially domestic gas supply for power generation — transforming Timor-Leste's energy and economic development trajectory. The CMATS (Certain Maritime Arrangements) treaty that delayed boundary determination was cancelled by Timor-Leste in 2017; the ICJ-mediated boundary treaty was signed in 2018, giving Timor-Leste a larger Sunrise share (~70%). But Woodside FID (final investment decision) remains pending as of 2026 — the commercial gap between Timorese and Woodside development visions has not been bridged.
EDTL: post-conflict utility rebuilding under fiscal pressureEDTL was established from scratch in 2002 — Indonesian withdrawal from East Timor in 1999 was accompanied by destruction of approximately 70% of Timor-Leste's infrastructure. The Portuguese colonial grid (pre-1975) was modest; the Indonesian-era grid was partially expanded; the 1999 destruction set electricity access back to near zero in many areas. EDTL has achieved ~80% electrification in 22 years — a significant accomplishment for a post-conflict startup utility. But EDTL's institutional capacity remains weak: high staff turnover, limited technical training, aging plant with inadequate O&M budgets, and subsidy dependence on the Petroleum Fund. Commercial tariffs: approximately US$0.10-0.15/kWh for residential — well below cost-reflective rates (~US$0.25-0.35/kWh) — with the subsidy gap funded by Petroleum Fund transfers that are unsustainable as the Fund depletes. EDTL's financial position does not allow it to fund generation investment independently.
RE potential: hydro, solar, and Tasi Mane gas-to-powerTimor-Leste has genuine RE potential that remains largely undeveloped: hydro (Ira Lalaro 90 MW potential on the Laclo River — ADB pre-feasibility completed; several smaller run-of-river sites), solar (8-9 kWh/m²/day horizontal irradiation — excellent); wind (south coast and highlands show potential, limited assessment). Current RE installed: approximately 10-20 MW (scattered solar rooftop, small hydro). The 30% RE by 2030 target requires approximately 150 MW of additional RE — primarily Ira Lalaro hydro (if financed) plus distributed solar. ADB's Timor-Leste Energy Programme (approved 2022, US$120M) includes Dili distribution rehabilitation and rural electrification; a proposed hydro component is under preparation. The Greater Sunrise gas pipeline option would additionally enable domestic gas-to-power (replacing diesel) at Hera and Dili — potentially 100-200 MW of gas generation at significantly lower fuel cost than current HFO.

Energy Transition in a Post-Conflict, Pre-Sunrise Context

Timor-Leste's energy transition is constrained by three simultaneous pressures: (1) an ageing, unreliable grid that requires maintenance investment before new capacity is useful; (2) a declining Petroleum Fund that limits the fiscal space for any large infrastructure investment; and (3) the unresolved Greater Sunrise development pathway, which is the single largest factor shaping whether Timor-Leste can afford both its social spending and its energy investment. The EDTL governance and capacity deficit sits underneath all three: no amount of RE potential or Petroleum Fund capital produces reliable electricity if EDTL cannot operate and maintain the infrastructure that donors build.

Timor-Leste is the Pacific SIDS batch's outlier: not a coral atoll facing sea-level rise, but a mountainous half-island with genuine hydro, solar, and gas potential, whose energy sector challenge is primarily about fiscal sustainability, utility governance, and resolving the contested Greater Sunrise development pathway that will determine whether Timor-Leste escapes Petroleum Fund dependency before the Fund runs out. The energy question and the fiscal question are inseparable — and both are downstream of Greater Sunrise.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Greater Sunrise's development pathway — whether a floating LNG to Australia or a pipeline to Timor-Leste's south coast — is the single most consequential energy and economic policy decision Timor-Leste will make in this decade, because a Timorese pipeline enables domestic gas-to-power generation that could transform EDTL's fuel cost structure, while an Australian FLNG option captures gas revenues without enabling domestic energy transition — and Timor-Leste's negotiating position is stronger than its fiscal desperation implies, because Woodside cannot develop Sunrise without Timorese consent. Woodside's 70% minority share in Greater Sunrise (Timor-Leste holds the balance via TIMOR GAP, the national oil company) means Woodside cannot proceed without Timorese agreement on the development concept. Timor-Leste's leverage is real — but time-limited: as the Petroleum Fund depletes, fiscal pressure may eventually force Timor-Leste to accept less favourable terms to unlock any revenue. The optimal negotiating strategy is to press for a pipeline-to-Timor concept now, while the Petroleum Fund still provides fiscal headroom, rather than accepting FLNG terms under fiscal duress in 2030-2035.
2
EDTL's institutional capacity — not generation capacity — is the binding constraint on Timor-Leste's energy transition, and ADB's and WB's Timor-Leste energy programmes must make sustained EDTL utility reform (governance, commercial systems, O&M capability, technical training, tariff structure) the primary deliverable rather than treating plant and grid construction as the deliverable and utility capacity as a secondary project component. The pattern in post-conflict SIDS electricity (Timor-Leste, Solomon Islands pre-Tina River) is that donors build infrastructure that the utility cannot maintain: diesel generators installed in 2005-2015 are now aging and partially non-functional because O&M budgets were not established; solar installations from 2015-2020 are degrading because EDTL has no systematic panel cleaning, inverter maintenance, or battery replacement programme. Every new generation project in Timor-Leste should include a 5-year funded O&M programme, a technical training curriculum for EDTL staff, and a commercial management review — not as afterthoughts but as primary deliverables with their own budget lines.
3
The Ira Lalaro hydro project (90 MW, Laclo River) is Timor-Leste's highest-value energy investment prospect — capable of replacing approximately 60-70% of Dili's current diesel generation with firm renewable energy at a cost per kWh competitive with EDTL's current diesel tariff — but requires ADB financing of approximately US$300-400M and a transmission line to Dili, and the project should be brought to financing readiness as ADB's single largest Timor-Leste investment priority before Petroleum Fund depletion removes the fiscal collateral that makes sovereign debt financing possible. Ira Lalaro has seasonal variability (rainy season flow vs. dry season low flow) but the Laclo River catchment is large enough to provide meaningful firm capacity year-round with reservoir storage. At 90 MW, it would approximately double EDTL's reliable generation capacity at a fuel cost of zero — transforming both electricity reliability and EDTL's per-unit cost. The ADB pre-feasibility is complete; the missing step is an ADB board-level commitment to bring Ira Lalaro to full feasibility and financing within 2025-2027, before the Petroleum Fund fiscal position changes the sovereign credit context.
4
Timor-Leste's electricity tariff (approximately US$0.10-0.15/kWh residential, well below cost-reflective rates) is a Petroleum Fund subsidy that will become fiscally unsustainable as the Fund depletes, and the government must design and implement a tariff reform pathway now — with low-income exemptions and a phased 5-year adjustment — rather than waiting for fiscal crisis to force an abrupt tariff shock that destroys household welfare and political stability simultaneously. The history of forced tariff adjustments in post-conflict states (Liberia, Sierra Leone) shows that abrupt electricity price increases during fiscal stress are politically destabilising and disproportionately harm urban poor populations who have the least alternative (no generator backup, no wood fuel alternative in urban Dili). A planned, gradual tariff adjustment to cost-reflective rates over 5 years (2025-2030), with a targeted low-income exemption for the first 50 kWh/month, is both fairer and more politically sustainable than the fiscal-crisis-forced tariff shock that awaits if no adjustment is made before the Petroleum Fund depletes.
5
The 2018 Timor Sea boundary treaty between Timor-Leste and Australia — resolving a 20-year dispute through ICJ mediation and giving Timor-Leste a 70% share of Greater Sunrise revenues — is one of the most significant recent achievements of international law in the energy resource domain, and Timor-Leste's insistence on pursuing its full legal rights (including cancellation of CMATS in 2017, which had suspended boundary determination) demonstrates that small, newly independent states can successfully assert resource rights against powerful neighbours when they have credible legal standing and sustained domestic political will — a lesson relevant to Pacific maritime boundary disputes more broadly. Australia's original position (proposed joint development zone rather than a full boundary, which would have given Timor-Leste a smaller Sunrise share) was eventually conceded when Timor-Leste initiated ICJ proceedings in 2013 and CMATS was cancelled in 2017. The resulting 2018 boundary treaty is a precedent for small island states asserting UNCLOS rights against larger neighbours — directly relevant to Pacific nations navigating boundary disputes with Australia, New Zealand, and the US. ESMAP and WB should document the Timor Sea case as a maritime energy rights reference in Pacific energy policy discussions.
