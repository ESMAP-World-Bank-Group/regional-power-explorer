Generation Mix & Sector Overview

Grenada's electricity system (~110 MW, serving ~120,000 people on the main island of Grenada plus the smaller islands of Carriacou and Petite Martinique) is operated by GRENLEC (Grenada Electricity Services Ltd.), a utility with an unusually complicated ownership history. GRENLEC was privatised in 1994 to WRB Enterprises (a US-based holding company), which managed the utility profitably under a long-term concession. In 2019, the Grenadian government exercised a contractual right to repurchase a majority stake — acquiring 51% of GRENLEC, with WRB retaining 49%. The dispute over the repurchase price and terms generated protracted Caribbean Court of Justice (CCJ) litigation that was resolved in 2022-2023. The practical result: GRENLEC is now majority state-owned but retains WRB management involvement and operates under a regulatory framework designed for the private concession era. The electricity mix in 2024 is approximately 82% diesel/HFO (Grenada's main plants at Beausejour, St. George's, and Grand Anse), ~12% solar (utility-scale projects at Grand Anse and Mt. Gay, plus significant hotel rooftop), and ~4% wind (northeast coast). The 50% RE by 2030 target requires approximately tripling the current RE fleet — a challenging but not impossible pace given Grenada's solar irradiance (~1,900 kWh/m²/year) and emerging wind resource.

GRENLEC privatisation, re-nationalisation, and CCJ: a cautionary taleGRENLEC's 1994 privatisation to WRB Enterprises delivered a genuinely improved service — WRB invested in new generation capacity, reduced technical losses, and maintained reliable supply with limited subsidies. The 2019 majority repurchase by the Grenadian government was motivated by a desire to capture GRENLEC's profitability for the state and to accelerate RE transition under direct government control. The repurchase mechanism (contractual buyback right at a formula price) generated a valuation dispute — WRB argued the formula undervalued their stake; the CCJ sided substantially with WRB in a 2023 ruling requiring additional compensation. The total cost of the dispute (additional buyout compensation + legal fees) exceeded US$50M — significantly more than expected. Lesson: poorly designed privatisation exit clauses are more expensive than clearly specified ones, and the government now manages a utility with less fiscal flexibility due to the litigation cost.
Hurricane Ivan 2004: the pre-Maria benchmarkHurricane Ivan (Category 3 at Grenada landfall, 240 km/h, September 2004) destroyed approximately 90% of Grenada's building stock — comparable in per-capita impact to Maria in Dominica three years later. GRENLEC's generation and distribution infrastructure was severely damaged; power restoration took several months island-wide. Ivan caused total economic damage of ~US$900M (~200% of GDP). The Ivan experience preceded Dominica's Maria by 13 years — but produced less "build back better" ambition: Grenada's post-Ivan grid rebuild used conventional overhead lines and standard thermal generation rather than the underground cabling and RE-first approach that Dominica later adopted post-Maria. Lesson: the "build back better" instinct is partly a function of leadership response and available finance frameworks, not just storm severity.
Carriacou and Petite Martinique: outer island diesel challengeCarriacou (~7,000 people, ~34 km²) and Petite Martinique (~900 people) are Grenada's outer islands, ~40 km northeast of the main island. Both are served by isolated GRENLEC diesel mini-grids — small, expensive, and vulnerable. Carriacou experiences frequent power outages due to aging diesel equipment and fuel delivery logistics (small vessel from Grenada, weather-dependent). Solar + battery hybrid for Carriacou (IDB-financed pilot, ~500 kW solar + 600 kWh BESS, operational 2023) has reduced diesel consumption by ~40% and improved reliability significantly. Petite Martinique: entirely diesel, no RE to date. These outer islands — each with ~1-10 MW mini-grids — are the highest-priority, highest-impact RE targets in Grenada's portfolio on a per-unit-investment basis.
Geothermal potential: Kick 'em Jenny and the volcanic arcGrenada sits on the Lesser Antilles volcanic arc, and while its primary volcanic risk is the underwater Kick 'em Jenny volcano (25 km north of Grenada, active submarine volcano), surface geothermal manifestations exist in the interior. Grenada is less endowed geothermally than Dominica or St. Kitts-Nevis — surface exploration has not found the high-temperature resource needed for commercial power generation. A 2018 IDB geothermal assessment found "possible but not confirmed" resources at depths of 2-3 km. Grenada's RE pathway is solar-first, with wind second and geothermal speculative rather than the other way around.
50% RE 2030: solar pathwayGrenada's 50% RE target by 2030 (from ~18% in 2024) requires approximately 35-40 MW of additional solar on a 344 km² island — roughly 1 MW per km² of available flat/low-slope land (modest by Caribbean solar standards). GRENLEC's solar procurement programme: Grand Anse Solar Farm Phase 2 (10 MW, under construction 2025); additional 25 MW RFP issued 2024. Net metering: adopted 2019, with ~3-4 MW of customer-installed rooftop solar by 2024. Hotel sector solar: Sandals LaSource, Spice Island Beach Resort, and several boutique hotels have installed rooftop solar, collectively ~2-3 MW. Battery storage requirement: GRENLEC estimates ~20-30 MW BESS needed by 2027 to maintain grid stability at 50% RE penetration. IDB and CDB financing confirmed for BESS component. Wind development (northeast corridor, Mt. Granby area): 5-10 MW additional turbines planned 2025-2027.

The GRENLEC Ownership Question: Implications for RE

Grenada's re-nationalisation of GRENLEC majority control raises important questions about the relationship between utility ownership structure and RE transition pace:

  • Government control advantages: The Grenadian government can now direct GRENLEC's capital allocation toward RE projects without needing to persuade a private shareholder of the return on investment. Tariff adjustments for RE cost recovery are politically easier when the government controls the board.
  • Government control disadvantages: GRENLEC must now fund capital expenditure partly from the government budget — which competes with health, education, and infrastructure priorities. WRB's access to commercial capital markets (at lower borrowing cost than the Grenada sovereign) is no longer available for GRENLEC projects. The litigation cost has absorbed capital that could have funded solar installation.
  • The WRB legacy: WRB Enterprises retains 49% and remains technically involved in management. The split ownership (51% state, 49% private) creates board dynamics where RE investment decisions require consensus — potentially slowing the decision-making that purely state-owned utilities can accelerate (or purely private utilities, where board authority is clearer).
Grenada is in many ways the most "normal" of the OECS micro-states from an energy perspective: solar-first RE strategy, improving grid mix (18% RE in 2024 vs. ~5% in 2018), conventional thermal backup, modest geothermal potential, and a 50% RE target that is ambitious but achievable without exotic technology. What distinguishes Grenada is its utility governance complexity — the GRENLEC nationalisation dispute consumed management attention and fiscal resources that could have been directed at RE. The lesson is that ownership structure transitions in small utility systems should be designed to minimise disruption to the operational and investment agenda, not maximise political signalling.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Grenada's GRENLEC re-nationalisation — motivated by reasonable policy goals (RE acceleration, capturing utility profit for the state) but executed with insufficient attention to the litigation and fiscal costs of the concession buyout — demonstrates that utility ownership transitions in SIDS carry hidden costs that can consume a significant fraction of the fiscal resources that motivated the transition in the first place. The ~US$50M+ dispute cost would have funded approximately 25 MW of utility-scale solar — roughly half of what GRENLEC needs to reach the 50% RE target. The policy goal (state control of RE transition pace) could have been achieved through enhanced regulatory authority over the private utility at a fraction of the cost. Grenada's experience is a cautionary reference for any OECS government considering utility re-nationalisation as an RE policy instrument.
2
Grenada's solar deployment trajectory (from ~5% RE in 2018 to ~18% in 2024 despite the GRENLEC governance disruption) demonstrates that solar PV rollout in the Eastern Caribbean is resilient to utility ownership uncertainty — private developers and hotel operators installed solar regardless of the GRENLEC dispute because the economics (high diesel tariffs making solar commercially attractive) drove deployment independent of utility policy. This finding — that distributed solar deployment can outpace utility procurement in high-tariff markets — has important implications for RE policy design: enabling net metering and removing interconnection barriers may generate more rapid solar deployment than utility-led procurement, at least in the first 20-25% of RE penetration. Beyond that threshold, utility-led procurement with battery storage becomes essential for grid stability.
3
Carriacou and Petite Martinique's diesel mini-grids — now the highest per-unit-cost electricity supply in the Grenadian system — are the clearest case for solar + battery hybrid replacement in Grenada, and the Carriacou solar hybrid pilot's demonstrated 40% diesel displacement validates the technology for rapid replication on Petite Martinique and on outer islands across the OECS. The Carriacou hybrid pilot (IDB-financed, ~US$3M capital cost) is generating diesel savings of approximately US$200,000/year — a 15-year simple payback that becomes economically superior to ongoing diesel purchases by year 4-5 when lifecycle costs are compared. Petite Martinique's ~900 residents have a strong equity claim on equivalent service quality improvements. GRENLEC's ability to replicate Carriacou at Petite Martinique and maintain both systems depends on technical capacity that is currently thin at GRENLEC's outer island operations level.
4
Grenada's tourism economy — "The Spice Isle" brand attracting upscale eco-tourism and yacht charter visitors — creates a natural alignment between tourism sector sustainability credentials and electricity decarbonisation, because international tour operators and yacht charter clients increasingly require green energy certifications that Grenada can only provide credibly with a substantially cleaner grid. The tourism-RE alignment is commercially exploitable: GRENLEC could develop a "Green Energy Tariff" for hotels and charter operators willing to pay a premium for certified RE electricity, creating a cross-subsidy that accelerates RE investment while capturing tourism sector willingness-to-pay for sustainability credentials. No OECS utility has implemented a functioning green tariff of this type; Grenada's tourism profile makes it the natural candidate to pilot the model.
5
Grenada's relatively modest 50% RE target by 2030 — compared to Barbados, Dominica, Antigua, and St. Kitts committing to 100% — reflects realistic assessment of GRENLEC's implementation capacity post-nationalisation dispute, and may ultimately produce better outcomes than more ambitious targets that strain institutional capacity and result in missed deadlines that erode policy credibility. Target credibility matters for RE investment signals: developers designing projects to a 50% target that is reliably met will invest more confidently than in a 100% target that is repeatedly deferred. Grenada's 50% by 2030 commitment, supported by specific solar procurement already underway (Grand Anse Phase 2, net metering programme, BESS procurement), has higher probability of being achieved on schedule than the 100% targets of neighbours whose implementation plans are less concrete. The lesson: specificity and implementability of RE targets matters more than their ambition level for actual investment mobilisation.
