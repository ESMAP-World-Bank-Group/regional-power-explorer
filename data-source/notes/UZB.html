1. Energy context

Uzbekistan is Central Asia's most populous country and its dominant electricity producer — generating over half of all power flowing across the CAPS regional grid. With 37 million people, an economy expanding at 5–6% per year, and a young, rapidly urbanising population, its electricity market is under structural pressure: demand reached ~85 TWh in 2024, nearly double the 2010 level. The system inherited from the Soviet era is gas-heavy, capital-starved, and priced well below cost recovery — but it is changing faster than any comparable market in the region.

Since 2017, President Mirziyoyev's reform programme has transformed the sector's institutional architecture: Uzbekenergo was unbundled into three functional entities, a new energy market regulator was established, competitive IPP tenders attracted over $13 billion in renewable investment, and tiered retail tariffs were introduced for the first time. The strategic ambition — repeatedly stated in presidential decrees and energy plans — is to reposition Uzbekistan as the green energy hub of Central Asia, exporting renewable electricity westward via the Caspian Sea to European markets by 2030.

  • Gas (56% cap / 84% gen): ~71 TWh in 2024, up from 60 TWh in 2023 — a sign of demand growth outpacing renewable additions in absolute terms. Most plants are Soviet-era (~1960s–1980s), operating at below-nameplate efficiency. Modernisation is underway: ACWA Power's 1,500 MW combined-cycle contract is one of several upgrades planned.
  • Coal (8% cap / 1% gen): A limited and structurally declining share. Coal capacity is concentrated in the Angren area (Tashkent region); its generation share is falling and carries no significant political weight.
  • Hydro (9% cap / 8% gen): ~77 hydropower plants, predominantly run-of-river on the Chirchiq, Naryn and Amu Darya tributaries. Output is seasonally variable — substantial spring snowmelt surplus, constrained in winter. Total capacity ~2.3 GW; limited further expansion potential.
  • Solar PV (16% cap / 5% gen): Near-zero in 2021, ~4 GW by end-2025. The IPP tender programme has made solar the fastest-growing segment. Competitive tender prices — as low as $0.026/kWh in early rounds — set a regional benchmark. Generation share will rise sharply as newer plants complete their first full operating year.
  • Wind (8% cap / 2% gen): ~2 GW installed, largely in the Karakalpakstan steppe where wind resources are among the strongest in Central Asia. Several large projects remain under construction. Part of the same IPP tender programme as solar.
The gap between renewable capacity share (~32%) and generation share (~18%) reflects the recent vintage of most solar and wind additions. As these plants accumulate full operating years, and as the ageing gas fleet loses availability, the generation mix will rebalance significantly through the late 2020s — if grid flexibility infrastructure keeps pace.

Demand growth is structural: a young population, rapid urbanisation, rising appliance ownership, and an expanding industrial base push consumption up ~4–5%/year. Government forecasts put demand at 117 TWh by 2030 and 137 TWh by 2035 — a near-doubling of today's system in ten years. The 2024 tiered tariff reform produced an immediate, measurable response: a 10.6% drop in consumption in the eight months following implementation, saving 1.3 billion kWh without any new supply-side investment. Demand response exists in Uzbekistan; it had simply never been priced before.

Retail tariffs (2025): Tiered residential structure introduced May 2024 — UZS 650/kWh for the first 200 kWh/month (~$0.052), UZS 900/kWh for 201–500 kWh (~$0.072), UZS 1,100/kWh above 500 kWh (~$0.088). Industrial tariff ~$0.074/kWh. These represent large increases from prior levels but remain well below cost-reflective pricing, leaving a structural fiscal gap in the sector.

2.3 Grid and interconnections

The high-voltage grid (500 kV and 220 kV) is operated by the National Electric Grids of Uzbekistan (NEGU). A defining feature: Tashkent hosts the Coordinating Dispatch Center (CDC) Energiya — the operational control room for the entire Central Asian Power System. This makes Uzbekistan not just the largest producer but the physical and institutional backbone of the regional grid. System losses run at approximately 10–12%.

Unlike ENTSO-E's synchronised Continental European grid, CAPS operates as a loosely-coupled parallel pool: member grids are physically interconnected but cross-border trade is governed by bilateral annual and seasonal energy balance agreements, not real-time market clearing. There is no regional TSO with binding authority over cross-border dispatch — a gap the World Bank's REMIT programme (approved March 2026, $1 billion) explicitly aims to close.

2.4 Cross-border trade arrangements

Caspian Green Corridor — the continent-scale bet In development

In May 2024, Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan and Azerbaijan signed an MOU to develop a submarine electricity transmission cable across the Caspian Sea — routing Central Asian renewable electricity westward through Azerbaijan, Georgia and to the European Union. The political ambition: 10–15 billion kWh of Central Asian green power exported to Europe annually by 2030. The engineering challenge is formidable (the Caspian has no prior subsea power cable), the geopolitics are complex (Iran's Caspian border claims, Russia's reaction), and no final investment decision has been taken — but the multilateral momentum is real. The World Bank's $1 billion REMIT programme, approved March 2026, treats regional electricity market integration as a prerequisite for the corridor to function.

Afghanistan — CASA-1000 export corridor Active / limited

Uzbekistan has historically exported modest volumes to northern Afghanistan via 220 kV lines. The Central Asia–South Asia electricity project (CASA-1000) aims to formalise and scale these flows, routing Central Asian hydro and renewable surplus to Afghanistan and Pakistan. The project has been repeatedly delayed by political instability and financing gaps but remains the flagship of the Central Asian regional electricity cooperation narrative.

CAPS seasonal balancing — the structural role Ongoing

Within CAPS, Uzbekistan's seasonal pattern is consistent: summer solar and hydro surplus flows north to Kazakhstan and east to Kyrgyzstan; winter demand peaks create import needs from Turkmenistan and Kazakhstan. The absence of a regional spot market — real-time prices, transparent capacity allocation — means these flows are handled through intergovernmental protocols, an inefficient mechanism for a grid that will carry increasing volumes of variable renewable energy.

3. Sector organisation and actors

3.1 Market structure

Until 2019, Uzbekenergo was one of the last fully vertically integrated Soviet-model electricity monopolies in the former USSR — controlling generation, transmission, distribution and retail under a single state entity. The 2019 unbundling, driven by ADB and World Bank conditionality, created three separate JSCs: JSC Thermal Power Plants (generation), JSC National Electric Grids of Uzbekistan (transmission/TSO), and JSC Regional Electric Power Networks (distribution, 14 regional entities). A fourth entity, Uzenergosotish JSC, was established as single buyer and wholesale market operator.

The reform architecture remains incomplete. A new energy market regulator — the Agency for Development and Regulation of the Energy Market — was created only in 2023. A liberalised wholesale spot market is planned for 2026. Distribution privatisation is under active tender, with concession contracts expected in 2025–2026. The transition from Soviet-model monopoly to functioning competitive market is real but unfinished — single-buyer pooling, below-cost retail tariffs, and state-controlled dispatch still define the operational reality.

4. Key issues and dynamics

4.1 The renewable revolution — 27 GW by 2030

  • Target: 27 GW of renewable capacity and a 54% renewable generation share by 2030. From near-zero in 2021 to ~6 GW installed by end-2025 — among the fastest renewable build-outs globally in per-capita terms.
  • The IPP tender model has been the key unlock: competitive auctions, dollar-denominated PPAs backed by sovereign guarantees, and offtake via Uzenergosotish. Early Masdar solar bids at $0.026/kWh set a regional price record in 2022 and established Uzbekistan as a credible frontier market for international solar developers.
  • Three companies — Masdar (UAE), ACWA Power (Saudi Arabia), and China Three Gorges/Gezhouba (China) — account for 77% of installed utility-scale solar and wind capacity and 100% of planned storage. This concentration, while efficient, creates strategic dependency on a small cluster of state-linked foreign investors.
  • Beyond 2025, the binding constraint is no longer appetite or price: it is grid flexibility. Absorbing high shares of variable solar and wind requires storage, dispatchable backup, and transmission reinforcement. Battery storage tendering is in early preparation; no equivalent to Türkiye's DEKA programme exists yet.
The renewable build is credible and well-financed. The risk is not ambition — it is grid infrastructure and market design. A 54%-renewable grid operating on Soviet-era dispatch protocols and a single-buyer wholesale structure is an engineering and institutional challenge of the first order.

4.2 The nuclear pivot — Rosatom's new template

  • In late 2025, Uzbekistan and Rosatom agreed a revised nuclear plan pairing two VVER-1000 reactors (1 GW each) with two RITM-200N small modular reactors (190 MW each) — a configuration providing both baseload and flexible output. Construction site: Farish district, Jizzakh region. Excavation began December 2025.
  • At full output: 16–17 TWh/year, covering approximately 15% of Uzbekistan's current electricity demand. The SMR units are explicitly positioned to buffer solar variability — a technically interesting integration of conventional nuclear with next-generation flexibility technology.
  • Uzbekistan is the first country to pursue Rosatom's RITM-200N SMR alongside conventional VVER units in a single programme. The geopolitical parallel with Türkiye's Akkuyu is direct: the project deepens the Russian energy relationship at a moment when Tashkent is actively courting Gulf and Western capital for renewables. Both tracks coexist under a deliberate multi-vector foreign energy policy.

4.3 Supply constraints and seasonal shortages

  • Despite rapid capacity additions, Uzbekistan regularly fails to meet demand — particularly in winter (peak heating demand, low solar hours, gas supply volatility) and summer (peak cooling load, urban grid overload). Tashkent experienced widespread blackouts in July 2025, affecting hospitals and businesses during a high-demand period.
  • The underlying tension: gas-fired capacity is the backbone of dispatch, but gas supply to power plants is increasingly contested by residential heating demand and export revenue pressures. A single disruption — a 6 million m³/day shortfall in early 2025 — was enough to trigger rationing across multiple oblasts.
  • Progress is measurable: scheduled curtailments fell from 4 billion kWh (2013) to 357 million kWh (2024) — a ten-fold improvement in a decade. But the system remains supply-constrained at seasonal peaks, and the shift toward a higher-solar mix will sharpen the winter adequacy problem unless storage or dispatchable capacity is built in parallel.

4.4 Market reform and tariff normalisation

  • The creation of a liberalised wholesale market by 2026 is the sector's most consequential pending reform. The current single-buyer model insulates both generators and distribution companies from price signals — efficient for a monopoly grid, incompatible with a high-IPP, high-variable-renewables future. Moving to a spot market requires cost-reflective tariffs, transmission pricing, and an independent balancing mechanism — all still works-in-progress.
  • Tariff normalisation — the quiet reform that matters most — has clearly begun. The 2024 tiered residential structure produced measurable demand response immediately. Cost-reflective tariffs are still years away, but the direction is set and the political commitment appears durable.
  • Distribution privatisation is the other pending piece: 14 regional networks are to be transferred to private operators under concession contracts (tenders active 2025–2026), targeting a 50% reduction in distribution losses currently running at 10–12%.

5. Five things to take away

1
Gas is still the system — 84% of generation in 2024. The IPP programme is reshaping the capacity mix rapidly, but the dispatch order keeps gas central until storage and grid flexibility infrastructure catches up with the solar build.
2
The 27 GW renewable target by 2030 is ambitious and largely credible: over $13 billion in projects are contracted or in development. Uzbekistan has become Central Asia's most attractive renewable IPP market — with Gulf and Chinese capital competing for PPAs that would not have existed five years ago.
3
Nuclear is coming. Rosatom's 2×VVER-1000 + 2×RITM-200N SMR plan broke ground in December 2025. At 16–17 TWh/year, it would add the equivalent of one-fifth of today's total demand — and the SMR units are explicitly designed to provide grid flexibility for a high-solar system.
4
The Caspian Green Corridor — a submarine cable linking Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan and Azerbaijan for green power exports to Europe — is the region's most geopolitically ambitious energy project. The $1 billion World Bank REMIT programme (March 2026) is the first major multilateral investment in the regional market architecture that would make it viable.
5
Tariff reform is the quiet structural shift that matters most. Years of below-cost pricing suppressed investment and masked demand signals. The 2024 tiered tariff delivered 1.3 billion kWh in savings immediately — the clearest proof yet that Uzbek consumers respond to price. The path to a self-sustaining sector runs through cost-reflective tariffs, and that path has now started.
