Generation Mix & Capacity

Morocco has approximately 12 GW of installed electricity capacity, of which 5.5 GW (45.4%) is renewable as of end-2025: 2.1 GW hydro, 2.4 GW wind, and ~961 MW solar. The remaining ~6.5 GW is predominantly coal (Jorf Lasfar and Safi), gas, and oil. Morocco is simultaneously one of Africa's most ambitious renewable energy developers and one of its largest coal importers — a tension that defines its current transition period. The national target is 52% renewable in installed capacity by 2030, with ANRE approving a build-out from ~3.4 GW wind+solar today to 9.3 GW by 2029.

ONEE, MASEN & Market Structure

Morocco's electricity sector is organised around two key institutions:

  • ONEE (Office National de l'Électricité et de l'Eau Potable): Vertically integrated state utility; transmission, distribution, and legacy thermal generation; purchases renewable power from MASEN projects and private IPPs under PPAs; managing financial stress from subsidised tariffs vs. imported fuel costs.
  • MASEN (Moroccan Agency for Sustainable Energy): Independent agency managing renewable energy development; procures, structures, and oversees utility-scale RE projects; runs competitive tenders for solar, wind, and CSP; acts as offtaker or pass-through to ONEE.
  • Private IPPs: Nareva (ONA group), TAQA Morocco, EDF Renouvelables, Acciona, EDF EN, and international developers active across wind and solar; Morocco has one of the most active RE IPP markets in Africa.
  • Interconnections: Two 400 kV AC cables to Spain (Tarifa); interconnection with Algeria operational but limited; aspirations for subsea HVDC cable to France/Iberia for large-scale clean energy export.
Morocco is often cited as a model for African energy transition, and for good reason: the combination of MASEN's institutional capacity, competitive renewable tenders, world-class wind and solar resources, and proximity to European electricity markets creates a compelling clean energy development platform. Yet the headline 52% renewables target co-exists with two operational coal plants (Jorf Lasfar 2,056 MW + Safi 1,386 MW) that will not retire before 2030. The real transition story lies in what happens after 2030 — when Morocco's green hydrogen export ambitions come online and European carbon border adjustment mechanisms create financial pressure to close fossil capacity.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Morocco's 9.3 GW solar+wind target by 2029 requires near-tripling current capacity in four years — an unprecedented procurement rate for the country. ANRE's approval of detailed annual targets creates regulatory certainty, but the bottleneck is ONEE's financial capacity to absorb PPAs and the development of a domestic equipment manufacturing base. Morocco has been building EPC and O&M expertise through MASEN projects; scaling to 1.5–2 GW/year will test whether that expertise is deep enough.
2
The Noor CSP complex remains Morocco's most distinctive clean energy asset globally but is not replicable at lower cost. CSP with thermal storage (Noor III parabolic trough + molten salt) provides dispatchable clean electricity after dark — a capability PV alone cannot replicate. But costs ($0.15–0.20/kWh) far exceed PV + battery alternatives, and Morocco will likely not build more large-scale CSP. The Noor complex is a sunk-cost strategic asset that differentiates Morocco in a PV-dominated world.
3
The coal exit question defines Morocco's credibility as a clean energy leader. Jorf Lasfar (2,056 MW) and Safi (1,386 MW) are Morocco's two largest power plants, both coal-fired, both under long-term PPAs extending beyond 2030. Retiring them early requires compensating investors; keeping them makes Morocco's 2030 renewable targets a capacity fiction without a generation reality. The EU carbon border adjustment mechanism — applying to Moroccan electricity exports — creates a financial incentive to accelerate the coal exit that did not exist before.
4
Morocco's green hydrogen ambitions could be transformative but face the "first mover" challenge. The government is targeting large-scale green hydrogen production from Atlantic wind for export to Europe — leveraging the best wind resources in North Africa. But the entire green hydrogen value chain (electrolysers, pipelines or ammonia conversion, European import terminals) must develop simultaneously. The first projects will set costs and de-risk the supply chain for followers; Morocco's early mover advantage is real but the timing risk is high.
5
The proposed Morocco–Europe subsea HVDC cable would make Morocco the electricity equivalent of Norway for southern Europe. Like Norway's offshore wind and hydro connected to Britain via subsea HVDC, a Morocco-Europe cable would allow Iberian and French grids to draw on Moroccan solar and Atlantic wind during peak hours — and Morocco to earn export revenue while European grids decarbonise. The economic logic is compelling; the financing and political alignment across multiple countries is the hard part.
