Generation Mix & Capacity

Uruguay carried out the fastest wind build-out of any country in the Americas relative to its size. Between 2013 and 2019 it went from almost no wind capacity to roughly 1,500 MW, which now supplies close to 40% of annual generation. Combined with hydro and biomass, renewables typically cover around 90% of the country's electricity, and in favourable months considerably more.

The transformation was achieved without a liberalised market. UTE, the state utility, remained the single buyer and contracted private wind farms through long-term PPAs awarded by tender, under a cross-party energy policy agreed in 2008 that survived successive governments. That political continuity, rather than market design, is what most distinguishes the Uruguayan case.

The system's problem is now the mirror image of scarcity. In windy, wet periods Uruguay produces more than it can use and exports to Argentina and Brazil or curtails. In drought, as in 2023, hydro collapses and the country falls back on expensive thermal generation and imports. Storage, demand growth and green hydrogen are all being pursued as ways to absorb the surplus.

Market Structure & Institutions

  • UTE: The Administración Nacional de Usinas y Trasmisiones Eléctricas owns most generation, all transmission and all distribution, and is the buyer for private generators. Uruguay is a single-buyer system, not a competitive market.
  • ADME: The Administración del Mercado Eléctrico dispatches the system and administers the wholesale market that exists around UTE's contracts.
  • URSEA: The regulator for energy and water services, responsible for tariffs and technical standards.
  • MIEM: The Ministry of Industry, Energy and Mining sets policy, including the 2008 multi-party energy policy and the current green hydrogen roadmap.
  • Interconnections: Salto Grande and Concepción del Uruguay connect to Argentina at 50 Hz; Melo and Rivera connect to Brazil through frequency converters. For its size Uruguay is unusually well interconnected, which is what makes its surplus exportable at all.
  • Green hydrogen: Several large projects have been proposed to use surplus wind for hydrogen and e-fuels, most prominently on the Atlantic coast. None has reached financial close at scale, and their viability depends on European offtake.
Uruguay is the standing counter-example to the claim that deep renewable penetration requires a liberalised market. A state monopoly buyer, a cross-party policy agreement and competitive tenders for private wind delivered a 90% renewable grid in under a decade. The open question is what to do with the surplus, since a small domestic market limits how much value that clean energy can capture at home.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Political continuity did more for the transition than market design. The 2008 multi-party energy policy meant developers faced the same rules across three governments. In a region where energy policy is routinely reversed, that credibility is what made fifteen-year PPAs financeable at low cost.
2
A 90% renewable grid is still a hydrological system. The 2023 drought showed that wind does not hedge hydro when demand peaks and reservoirs are empty. Uruguay's residual thermal fleet is expensive and rarely used, which is exactly why it is hard to justify keeping and impossible to do without.
3
Surplus is only valuable if it can be exported or stored. Uruguay already curtails wind in favourable conditions. The converters to Brazil and the links to Argentina give it more export capability than most small systems, but regional demand is not always there when Uruguay's wind is.
4
Green hydrogen is the intended answer to the surplus, and it is unproven. Converting curtailed wind into exportable molecules would solve the absorption problem and create an industry. Nothing at scale has been built, and the projects depend on offtake contracts in Europe that have repeatedly slipped.
5
Uruguay is a replicable template for small systems, not a unique case. Its combination of tendered private renewables under a state offtaker, strong interconnection and policy stability is directly transferable to small systems elsewhere, which is why it is cited far more often than its 13 TWh would suggest.
