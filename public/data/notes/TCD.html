Electricity Sector Overview

Chad has one of the lowest electricity access rates in the world — approximately 4% of the population has a grid connection, making it an extreme outlier even by sub-Saharan African standards. The electricity system is almost entirely diesel and heavy fuel oil thermal generation, concentrated in N'Djamena (the capital) and a handful of secondary towns. There is no national interconnected grid; each city operates its own isolated thermal plant. Installed capacity of ~285 MW serves a country of over 18 million people, with generation totalling ~400 GWh/yr — less than a small EU city.

SNE — A Utility Under Extreme Stress

The SNE (Société Nationale d'Electricité) is Chad's national utility, responsible for generation, transmission, and distribution in the grid-connected towns. It is one of the most financially and operationally distressed utilities in Africa:

  • Revenue gap: Average tariff (~$0.25/kWh) is roughly half the generation cost ($0.45/kWh). SNE cannot recover its costs from customers, requiring chronic government transfers that are themselves unreliable due to Chad's volatile oil revenues.
  • Ageing infrastructure: Most of SNE's diesel generating sets are old, poorly maintained, and frequently out of service. Available capacity is consistently below nameplate capacity.
  • Fuel supply disruptions: Chad imports all its diesel and HFO. Landlocked geography means fuel transport costs are among the highest in Africa; fuel supply chains are vulnerable to regional insecurity and border disruptions.
  • Human capacity: SNE lacks trained technical staff; maintenance is often deferred until complete failure; there is no systematic asset management framework.
Chad is one of the most extreme cases of energy poverty in the world. An electrification rate of 4% in a country of 18+ million means that 17+ million people have no grid electricity — not because resources are absent (Chad has the world's best solar irradiance), but because the institutional and financial infrastructure to deliver electricity simply does not exist at scale. Diesel generators power the privileged few at enormous cost; the rest use kerosene and biomass.

The Solar Opportunity — Desert-to-Power

Chad lies in the global solar belt, with irradiance levels among the highest on earth (GHI 5.5–7.0 kWh/m²/day in the north). The Desert-to-Power initiative, led by the African Development Bank, specifically targets Chad and 10 other Sahelian countries for large-scale solar deployment to replace diesel and extend electrification:

  • Target: AfDB's Desert-to-Power programme aims to develop 10 GW of solar across the Sahel; Chad's share is proportionate to its solar resource and population.
  • Off-grid priority: Given Chad's dispersed population and lack of transmission infrastructure, off-grid solar (mini-grids, SHS) is far more cost-effective than grid extension for the vast majority of villages.
  • World Bank Energy Access Scale-Up Project: Active programme focused on solar mini-grids and solar home systems for rural households; represents the most impactful near-term intervention in Chad's energy access crisis.
  • Economic case: Solar PV + battery mini-grids now cost $0.30–0.50/kWh all-in — comparable to or cheaper than SNE diesel even before accounting for reliability. The economics have decisively shifted in favour of solar off-grid for rural Chad.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Chad's energy crisis is primarily an access crisis, not a supply mix crisis. With 4% electrification, the most important metric is not what fuel generates power but how many people gain access to any power at all. Every interventional dollar is better spent on solar mini-grids and SHS than on reforming the grid mix.
2
Diesel dependency at $0.45/kWh is economically catastrophic for Chad. SNE's structural loss — selling at half its generation cost — requires perpetual state subsidies in a country with limited and volatile fiscal revenues. This is not a sustainable model. The only viable exit is rapid solar substitution, which is now cost-competitive even in Chad's landlocked markets.
3
The Desert-to-Power initiative is the most relevant strategic framework for Chad's energy transition. Cross-border solar corridors and Sahelian interconnections are long-horizon aspirations, but the immediate Desert-to-Power programme — off-grid solar, mini-grids, solar irrigation — addresses Chad's most pressing energy needs without requiring institutional reform of the broken SNE.
4
Chad's security situation severely constrains energy investment. Ongoing regional insecurity (Lake Chad Basin conflict, Sudan spillover), political instability, and governance challenges create a risk environment that makes private investment in formal energy infrastructure extremely difficult. Donor-financed and NGO-operated off-grid programmes are the realistic delivery mechanism in most of the country.
5
Oil revenues have not been converted into energy infrastructure — and there is no sign this will change soon. Chad has been an oil exporter since 2003 (Chad–Cameroon pipeline). Those revenues have gone primarily to security, debt service, and elite consumption. Breaking this pattern to fund universal energy access requires political economy change that external energy programming alone cannot achieve.
