Generation Mix & Capacity

El Salvador reached 3,101 MW of total installed capacity by end-2024, with renewables comprising approximately 62% of installed capacity and 68.3% of actual generation. The sector's defining feature is the co-existence of three distinct state-owned renewable technologies — hydropower on the Lempa River (CEL), geothermal (LaGeo), and a private solar PV boom — alongside a fossil backup layer. Solar emerged as the capacity leader at 846 MW, having grown rapidly from 835 MW in 2023; this solar wave is primarily private-sector driven and continues to expand.

Sector Structure — CEL, LaGeo & SIGET

El Salvador's electricity sector is partially liberalised — generation is competitive (private + state), while transmission is a state monopoly (ETESAL) and distribution has private concessions (AES El Salvador, DEUSEM, EEO, CLESA):

  • CEL (Comisión Ejecutiva Hidroeléctrica del Río Lempa): State company; owns and operates the entire Lempa River hydro cascade; also owns controlling stake in LaGeo.
  • LaGeo: CEL subsidiary (with minor private share from Enel); operates the Berlin and Ahuachapán geothermal complexes; regionally distinctive as a stable, zero-fuel-cost baseload source.
  • ETESAL: State transmission company; operates the national interconnected system and El Salvador's SIEPAC border points.
  • SIGET (Superintendencia General de Electricidad y Telecomunicaciones): Regulator for electricity and telecoms; manages concessions and tariff oversight.
  • UT (Unidad de Transacciones): Market operator, analogous to Guatemala's AMM but within a more constrained competitive framework.
El Salvador's geothermal sector is a national strategic asset often underappreciated in regional comparisons. LaGeo's Berlin and Ahuachapán plants provide ~200 MW of firm, 24/7, zero-fuel-cost, zero-carbon baseload — a combination that no other technology can currently match. As solar expands, geothermal becomes even more valuable as the anchor of the nocturnal generation stack.

SIEPAC & Regional Trade

El Salvador is an active MER trader, both importing and exporting depending on season and hydro conditions. Key dynamics:

  • El Salvador imports predominantly from Guatemala (north), particularly during dry season when the Lempa cascade weakens.
  • El Salvador exports to Honduras and Nicaragua during wet season surplus periods.
  • Solar PV growth is expected to make El Salvador an increasingly frequent exporter during peak solar hours.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
El Salvador's solar surge is real and will accelerate — but integration challenges are approaching. 846 MW of solar on a system with ~3,100 MW total installed capacity means solar already provides peak midday power. As solar continues to grow, midday curtailment, duck-curve dynamics, and the need for storage or demand flexibility will become pressing system management challenges — paralleling experiences in Chile and California at an earlier stage.
2
LaGeo's geothermal assets are El Salvador's most valuable electricity infrastructure by strategic merit. Firm, dispatchable, carbon-free, fuel-import-free geothermal power at 200+ MW is irreplaceable in a high-solar system. Expanding El Salvador's geothermal base — the country sits on the Central American volcanic arc with significant unexploited potential — would be the single highest-value investment in long-term energy security.
3
El Salvador's Lempa cascade is a shared resource with significant cross-border water management implications. The Lempa River originates in Guatemala and Honduras before entering El Salvador; upstream land use and water extraction in neighbouring countries affects flow into CEL's reservoirs. Trilateral river basin management is both a technical and diplomatic priority that energy planning cannot ignore.
4
El Salvador's small market size makes SIEPAC integration especially valuable. As Central America's second-smallest country (after Belize), El Salvador's domestic market alone cannot achieve the scale economies needed for major new generation projects. SIEPAC access allows El Salvador to export surplus solar and import hydro balance from Guatemala — a liquidity benefit disproportionately valuable to smaller systems.
5
The fossil backup fleet remains large relative to renewable share — retirement planning is overdue. At ~31% of installed capacity, thermal plants still define El Salvador's reliability margin even though they generate less than 32% of electricity. As solar + storage can increasingly provide this reliability service at lower cost, a systematic plan for thermal retirement (aligned with PPA expiries) would reduce long-run system costs and emissions simultaneously.
