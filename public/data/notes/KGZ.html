1. Energy context

Kyrgyzstan is a small, mountainous, hydro-dominated system with a structural problem it has not yet solved: it generates far less electricity than its population demands in winter, and the gap is widening. With 3.9 TWh of imports needed in 2024 (from Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, and Russia), the country runs a significant net deficit despite being home to some of Central Asia's most powerful river systems. The contradiction is explained by a single reservoir: Toktogul. This 19.5 billion cubic metre storage lake on the Naryn River supplies ~40% of national electricity — and when its water level falls, the entire system enters crisis.

In August 2023, President Japarov declared a three-year state of energy emergency (through December 2026) as Toktogul water levels sank toward their critical minimum and winter blackouts became routine. The response is multifaceted: accelerated solar IPP procurement (~1.2 GW in the pipeline), targeted thermal maintenance, tariff increases starting the long road toward cost recovery, and the regional CASA-1000 corridor that will — when operational in 2027 — give Kyrgyzstan a summer export revenue stream for the first time in the post-Soviet era.

  • Hydro (83% cap / 88% gen): ~12.8 TWh in 2024. Dominated by the Toktogul cascade on the Naryn River (1,200 MW Toktogul GES + downstream Kurpsai, Tash-Kumyr, Shamaldysai, Uch-Kurgan plants). Output is heavily seasonal — spring snowmelt brings peak generation; winter draws down reservoir storage. A severe hydrological drought in 2023 pushed Toktogul to near its operational minimum of 5.5–6.5 billion cubic metres.
  • Coal thermal (17% cap / 12% gen): ~1.8 TWh in 2024. Bishkek CHP (combined heat and power) and several regional plants fuelled by domestic Kara-Keche coal. The thermal fleet is 30–40 years old, with reliability declining. In winter 2023–24, unexpected thermal outages compounded the Toktogul shortfall.
  • Solar and wind (~0% cap, 2024): Effectively zero today — but ~1.2 GW of solar IPP projects are in the pipeline (Bishkek 300 MW, Tokmok 300 MW, IFC 300 MW in Batken and Talas, others). Rosatom is developing a 100 MW wind pilot in Issyk-Kul. The first large-scale solar plants are expected to commission 2025–2026.
Kyrgyzstan's generation-demand gap of 3.9 TWh in 2024 was covered by imports from Kazakhstan (681 GWh), Uzbekistan (504 GWh), and smaller volumes via regional protocols. This structural import dependency — winter-concentrated, cross-border, and denominated in hard currency — is the central financial and security vulnerability of the sector.

Demand has grown ~1–1.1 TWh per year in recent years against essentially flat generation capacity. The gap is closing in the wrong direction. If the solar pipeline (~1.2 GW by 2027) delivers on schedule, solar output will contribute ~2–2.5 TWh/year and materially reduce summer imports — but winter peak demand, when solar output is lowest, will remain the critical period for many years.

Retail tariffs (June 2025): Residential KGS 1.37–1.53/kWh (~$0.013–0.018/kWh) — the lowest in Central Asia. Business ~KGS 3.74/kWh (~$0.043/kWh). A 23.8% residential increase took effect May 2025 (≤700 kWh/month bracket); the government has announced 20%/year increases through 2030, targeting cost-reflective pricing. The political durability of these increases across an election cycle is the key uncertainty — below-cost tariffs have been politically protected for decades.

2.3 Grid and interconnections

JSC NESK (the National Electric Grid of Kyrgyzstan, post-2022 merger of transmission and distribution) operates the HV grid. Kyrgyzstan's grid is part of CAPS and physically interconnected with Kazakhstan (north) and Uzbekistan (south/west), with links to Tajikistan via the Datka–Khujand CASA-1000 line (completed March 2025). The seasonal trade pattern is consistent across years: summer export to Uzbekistan when Toktogul is full; winter import from Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan when reservoir levels are drawn down and demand peaks.

2.4 Cross-border trade arrangements

🇵🇰
CASA-1000 — Kyrgyzstan's first export revenue stream Commissioning 2027

Kyrgyzstan is one of two suppliers (alongside Tajikistan) in the 1,300 MW CASA-1000 HVDC corridor to Afghanistan and Pakistan. Kyrgyzstan's contribution: ~1.25 billion kWh per year of summer surplus electricity (May–September), sold to Pakistan's NTDC at commercial rates. The Kyrgyzstan section of the transmission line was completed in March 2025; the full corridor will be operational in January 2027. This is the first time Kyrgyzstan will have a formalised, hard-currency electricity export arrangement — and the export revenue could, if well managed, fund the modernisation the sector urgently needs.

🤝
Trilateral agreement (KAZ–KGZ–UZB) Active 2024

A 2024 trilateral cooperation agreement formalises the longstanding seasonal exchange: Kazakhstan supplies thermal power to Kyrgyzstan in winter (and permits Russian electricity transit through its grid); Kyrgyz hydro surplus flows to Uzbekistan in summer. This bilateral-protocol approach is Central Asia's closest approximation of a regional power pool — replacing ad hoc negotiations with agreed frameworks and volumes. It is the building block on which the World Bank REMIT regional market programme (2026+) will build a more formal market structure.

3. Sector organisation and actors

3.1 Market structure

Kyrgyzstan's electricity sector was formally unbundled during the 1990s (generation, transmission and distribution into separate entities), but in practice the sector has remained fragmented and financially distressed, with the state retaining effective control across all segments via the National Energy Holding Company (NEHC). The 2022 merger of the four distribution companies into NESK simplified the structure but concentrated both transmission and distribution under a single state entity.

There is no functioning competitive wholesale market. Generators (primarily the Toktogul cascade and NESK's affiliated plants) supply at regulated tariffs to NESK, which supplies distribution networks, which supply consumers at regulated retail rates. Parliament has prohibited the privatisation or alienation of NEHC's generation and grid assets to repay external debt — a constraint that limits both private sector participation and balance sheet repair options. The key structural task remains: rebuilding the financial viability of NESK and the broader sector through tariff normalisation, technical loss reduction, and IPP procurement.

4. Key issues and dynamics

4.1 Toktogul — the single point of failure

  • Toktogul reservoir (19.5 billion cubic metres at full capacity) generates ~40% of Kyrgyzstan's total electricity. When its water level drops toward the operational minimum of 5.5–6.5 billion cubic metres, turbines cannot run at full capacity and the entire system enters rolling blackout mode. In April 2024, the reservoir reached approximately 6.5 bcm — within 1 billion cubic metres of shutdown. Rolling cuts of 1.5–2 hours daily were imposed across the country for weeks.
  • The crisis was a function of simultaneous bad luck: a dry hydrological year in 2023 (low Naryn River inflows), high heating demand during a cold winter, and the gradual accumulation of deferred maintenance across the thermal backup fleet. The government declared a three-year energy emergency in August 2023, extending through December 2026.
  • The irony is profound: Kyrgyzstan sits astride the upper Naryn River, one of Central Asia's most powerful water courses, and in a bad year it runs short of electricity. The solution — more reservoir storage above Toktogul (Kambarata-1 dam, 1,860 MW, $5.6 billion, planned with Kazakhstan) — would provide the multi-year storage buffer Toktogul alone cannot deliver. But Kambarata-1 has been in planning for decades without reaching financial close.

4.2 The solar diversification push

  • The energy emergency forced a step-change in Kyrgyzstan's approach to generation diversification. From near-zero solar capacity, the government has contracted or is advanced-stage negotiating ~1.2 GW of utility-scale solar: Bishkek 300 MW ($180M), Tokmok 300 MW, IFC-backed Batken 150 MW + Talas 150 MW (agreement June 2024), Masdar park (~2026), Chui Province 200 MW. Rosatom's 100 MW wind pilot in Issyk-Kul adds a wind dimension.
  • Solar addresses the right problem — summer daytime generation — but not the wrong one: winter evenings, when demand peaks and solar produces nothing. The real value of solar is that it conserves Toktogul water during summer (avoiding discharge to generate power that solar can provide for free), preserving more storage for winter discharge.
  • The 10% renewable target by 2030 — referring to non-hydro renewables — is modest but structurally meaningful. If 1.2 GW of solar commissions by 2027, solar's share of annual generation could reach 10–12%, materially reducing the summer thermal dispatch and the seasonal import need.

4.3 Financial distress and tariff reform

  • Kyrgyzstan's electricity utilities have run annual losses estimated at $50+ million per year for almost two decades. The root causes: tariffs far below cost recovery, technical losses of 15–20% in aging distribution networks, and non-technical losses (theft, non-payment) that metering programmes have only partially addressed. ADB's power sector improvement project ($16.7M loan + $28.1M grant) is specifically targeting automated metering infrastructure (SCADA rollout, smart meters) as a near-term loss reduction measure.
  • The tariff reform announced in 2024–2025 is the most significant pricing shift in Kyrgyzstan's post-Soviet electricity history. A 23.8% increase (≤700 kWh/month residential bracket) took effect May 2025, following a framework of 20%/year increases through 2030. At current exchange rates, residential tariffs would reach ~$0.040–0.045/kWh by 2030 — still low by international standards, but potentially cost-reflective in a low-cost hydro system.

4.4 CASA-1000 — the monetisation moment

  • CASA-1000 is the most significant structural change in Kyrgyzstan's electricity economics in a generation. The 500 kV Datka–Khujand HVDC line (Kyrgyzstan section) was completed March 2025. When the full corridor to Pakistan is operational in 2027, Kyrgyzstan can sell its summer hydro surplus (~1.25 TWh/year) at commercial rates to Pakistan's NTDC — instead of exporting cheaply to Uzbekistan under intergovernmental protocol pricing.
  • The financial logic is compelling but requires institutional discipline: CASA-1000 revenues must be reinvested into the sector (loss reduction, storage, grid reinforcement) rather than absorbed into the state budget. Whether that discipline can be maintained is the governance question that will determine whether CASA-1000 actually transforms Kyrgyzstan's electricity sector or merely provides temporary fiscal relief.

5. Five things to take away

1
Toktogul is Kyrgyzstan's Achilles heel. In April 2024 the reservoir came within 1 billion cubic metres of its operational shutdown threshold. The 3-year energy emergency (2023–2026) is recognition that a Soviet-era hydro monoculture is no longer adequate for a growing economy in a changing climate.
2
The 3.9 TWh import deficit is structural and will persist without diversification. Solar helps in summer; nothing cheap helps in winter nights when Toktogul is low. The Kambarata-1 upstream reservoir (1,860 MW, years from financial close) is the real long-term solution — but multi-year reservoir storage may be needed before Kambarata-1 arrives.
3
CASA-1000 (Kyrgyz section complete March 2025, full system January 2027) creates Kyrgyzstan's first formalised electricity export revenue stream — ~1.25 TWh/year of summer surplus to Pakistan at commercial rates. The financial question is whether those revenues can be directed into sector modernisation rather than general budget support.
4
The ~1.2 GW solar IPP pipeline is the largest generation diversification in Kyrgyzstan's history. Solar won't solve winter evenings — but it will conserve Toktogul water during summer days, preserving more hydro storage for winter discharge when it is most valuable.
5
Tariff reform is underway from a very low base ($0.018/kWh residential, June 2025 — the lowest in Central Asia). The government targets 20%/year increases through 2030. Cost-reflective pricing is achievable in a low-cost hydro system; the question is whether the political will to continue raising prices survives the electoral calendar.
