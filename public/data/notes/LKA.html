Generation Mix & Capacity

Sri Lanka has 4,636 MW of installed capacity, split roughly between thermal (47.5% — coal + oil), hydropower (42.5%), and grid-scale solar/wind + rooftop (10%). The defining feature of 2024 was the extraordinary rooftop solar boom: installed rooftop PV capacity more than doubled, from 632 MW to 1,433 MW in a single year, driven by households and businesses investing in solar following the 2022 economic crisis and its electricity supply disruptions. This grassroots solar expansion is reshaping the daily load curve and creating grid management challenges even as it reduces fuel import dependency.

The 2022 Crisis & Its Energy Legacy

Sri Lanka's 2022 economic crisis — the worst since independence — was partly an energy crisis. Foreign exchange shortages prevented fuel imports; power cuts of 7–13 hours daily paralysed industry and households; CEB's accumulated losses reached ~$1.7 billion (3.1% of public debt) from below-cost tariffs. The crisis forced several structural changes:

  • Tariff reform: Electricity prices were raised sharply for the first time in years — a painful but necessary correction that reduced CEB's fiscal drain.
  • Rooftop solar surge: Fear of recurrence drove mass investment in household and commercial solar as an energy security hedge — the 1,433 MW of rooftop solar in 2024 is a direct legacy of 2022 blackouts.
  • IPP opaqueness scrutiny: A post-crisis review of how overpriced power contracts with private generators had contributed to CEB's losses led to greater scrutiny of new PPA terms — directly relevant to the Adani wind controversy.
  • IMF programme: Sri Lanka's IMF programme (2023) includes energy sector conditionality around tariff cost-recovery and CEB financial reform.
The Adani wind saga encapsulates Sri Lanka's post-crisis energy governance dilemma: it needs 484 MW of clean wind power urgently to meet its 2030 RE target and reduce fossil fuel imports — but the PPA was approved at nearly double the rate local developers offered ($0.083 vs $0.048/kWh). The US indictment gave the government a face-saving reason to cancel a contract it should not have approved at those terms. The deeper question — how to attract credible wind developers at competitive prices without the secrecy that fuelled the 2022 crisis — remains unresolved.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Sri Lanka's rooftop solar boom is the most significant bottom-up energy transition in South Asia. 1,433 MW of rooftop solar installed in a single year — in a country of 22 million with 4,636 MW total capacity — represents a transformation driven by households responding to energy insecurity, not government planning. Managing this distributed generation (net metering, grid stability, curtailment during midday surplus) is now a critical CEB operational challenge that its systems and regulations were not designed for.
2
The 70% RE by 2030 target is ambitious but technically achievable if procurement is rapid and transparent. Sri Lanka has excellent wind resources in the northern coast (Mannar/Pooneryn) and good solar irradiance year-round. The 484 MW Adani site is being retendered; if won by a competent developer at market tariffs, it could commission by 2028. The bottleneck is not resource or technology but procurement process speed and CEB's ability to manage variable RE integration.
3
The Norochcholai coal plant is both a liability and an unavoidable reality for the next decade. The 900 MW plant — Sri Lanka's largest generator — cannot be retired without replacement, and its fuel costs (imported coal, USD-denominated) are a major contributor to CEB's financial volatility. A managed retirement plan linked to renewable additions and the planned India subsea cable (if realised) would reduce exposure; unplanned early retirement without replacement capacity would risk supply security.
4
CEB's financial reform is the enabling condition for everything else. Without cost-reflective tariffs and a sustainable CEB balance sheet, no new RE procurement is credible (developers need a counterparty that can pay), no grid upgrade investment can be funded, and Sri Lanka remains dependent on government fiscal transfers to keep the lights on. The IMF programme's CEB conditionality and the 2022 tariff reforms moved in the right direction — maintaining that trajectory under political pressure is the key governance test.
5
An India–Sri Lanka submarine power cable would be a strategic game-changer if realised. A proposed HVDC subsea cable linking southern India's grid to Sri Lanka would give Sri Lanka access to India's large and increasingly renewable power market — importing surplus solar/wind from Tamil Nadu in real time, exporting when Sri Lanka has surplus hydro, and providing a reliability backstop that eliminates the worst-case blackout risk. The technical feasibility is proven (similar cables exist in Europe); the stumbling block is financing and political alignment between the two governments.
