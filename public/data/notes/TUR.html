1. Energy context

Turkiye is one of the world's fastest-growing large electricity markets. With 85 million people and a large industrial base, it consumed 354 TWh in 2024 — more than Poland, the Netherlands and Belgium combined. The country imports nearly all of its fossil fuels (98% of gas, large share of coal), making the chronic energy import bill one of the biggest drags on Turkiye's current account. Reducing this dependency is the central driver of energy policy.

In 2024, Turkiye made the transition from a net electricity importer to a net exporter for the first time in several years, driven by a strong hydro year and rapid solar growth. The government's National Energy Plan (NEP 2035) targets 120 GW of wind and solar by 2035 and entry into the nuclear era via Akkuyu.

  • Coal (18% cap / 35% gen): ~122 TWh in 2024. Predominantly domestic lignite + imported hard coal. No new plants under construction. Turkiye overtook Germany as Europe's largest coal power producer in 2024. Politically sensitive due to lignite employment.
  • Hydro (27% cap / 21% gen): ~75 TWh in 2024 — near-record. Most large sites developed. Highly variable year-to-year; sensitive to climate change and reservoir depletion in western basins.
  • Gas (20% cap / 19% gen): ~67 TWh in 2024. Flexible back-up and peak capacity. 100% import-dependent (Russia, Azerbaijan, LNG). Share declining from ~37% a decade ago.
  • Wind (12% cap / 10% gen): ~37 TWh in 2024. Strong Aegean/Marmara resource. New additions slowed in 2022–2023 (permitting, grid bottlenecks) but recovering through YEKA tenders.
  • Solar PV (20% cap / 8.7% gen): ~31 TWh in 2024. Fastest-growing segment — ~20 GW by end-2024, ~25 GW by 2025. Predominantly commercial/industrial. YEKA auctions delivering ~2–3 €cent/kWh.
  • Geothermal (1.4% cap / 3.1% gen): ~11 TWh. Turkiye is top-5 globally. Concentrated in western Anatolia (Denizli, Aydın, Manisa).
  • Nuclear — Akkuyu (commissioning): 4×1 200 MW VVER-1200, built by Rosatom (Mersin). Unit 1 completed; trial operations began July 2025. Units 2–4 to follow at ~1-year intervals; full 4 800 MW by ~2029.

2.3 Grid and interconnections

The HV grid (400 kV and 154 kV) is operated by TEIAŞ. Turkiye synchronised with ENTSO-E's Continental European grid in 2010, giving it real-time access to European power markets — a major structural advantage over most regional peers. Network losses ~9–10%. Topological features worth noting:

  • East–West divide: the large industrial demand centres are in the north-west (Marmara, Thrace) while the best renewable resources — solar in south-east Anatolia, geothermal in western Anatolia, wind on Aegean coast — are geographically dispersed. Grid reinforcement investments are a bottleneck for renewable integration.
  • Seasonal hydro variability: eastern Anatolian basins (Euphrates, Tigris) supply large hydro capacity, but reservoir levels are increasingly affected by drought years, creating year-on-year generation volatility.
  • No major isolated zones — the Turkish grid is largely interconnected domestically, unlike some neighbouring systems.

2.4 Cross-border trade arrangements

🇧🇬
Bulgaria — main export corridor Structural

Bulgaria has become Turkiye's primary electricity export destination — ~19 TWh in 2024 via the Strandzha–Malkoclar 400 kV interconnection. Trade is commercial and market-based: Turkish generators (increasingly solar and wind) sell into the ENTSO-E day-ahead market when cross-border capacity is available and Turkish prices are competitive. The quadrilateral Azerbaijan–Turkiye–Georgia–Bulgaria green energy framework (launched 2024) aims to formalise a broader corridor routing Azerbaijani and Turkish renewables to the EU, with a potential second Bulgaria–Turkiye 400 kV line under discussion.

🇬🇷
Greece — EU market gateway Active

Bidirectional flows via the existing 400 kV interconnection (~600 MW). A second 400 kV line (130 km, 2 GW capacity, completion ~2029) has received €100M in EU RRF support — a signal of European interest in Turkiye as a low-cost renewable export platform. Turkiye's YEKA solar costs (2–3 €cent/kWh) make it a natural summer exporter to Europe once interconnection capacity expands.

🇮🇷
Iran — historical bilateral flows Modest

Turkiye and Iran have bilateral electricity trade arrangements governed by intergovernmental frameworks, not spot markets. Turkiye has historically been a net importer from Iran during periods of tight domestic supply (winter gas shortages). Flows reverse depending on system conditions. Volumes are relatively modest; the broader energy relationship is dominated by gas (Iran is a secondary gas supplier to Turkiye via the Iran–Turkiye pipeline).

3. Sector organisation and actors

3.1 Market structure

Turkiye's electricity market is structurally more advanced than most regional peers. The 2001 Electricity Market Law (EML) unbundled the former state monopoly TEK and created an independent regulator (EMRA/EPDK). The 2013 EML deepened the framework, establishing EPIAŞ as the market operator with a functioning day-ahead and intraday spot market since 2015. Distribution was fully privatised across 21 regional companies (completed 2013), and competitive retail supply is available above ~1 GWh/year threshold. The key remaining state role is in transmission (TEIAŞ, privatisation pending since 2021) and a portion of generation (EÜAŞ).

4. Key issues and dynamics

4.1 Akkuyu — Turkiye's nuclear entry

  • Unit 1 completed construction; trial operations began July 2025. Units 2–4 to follow at ~1-year intervals; full 4 800 MW by ~2029. At full capacity: ~35 TWh/year (~10% of current demand).
  • Project financed ~93% by Rosatom ($20bn total, "build-own-operate" model unique in nuclear industry). TETAŞ holds a 15-year PPA at $0.1235/kWh for 70% of Units 1–2 and 30% of Units 3–4.
  • Second plant at Sinop (Black Sea, MHI/Framatome consortium) in early planning. Third site in Thrace being studied. Turkiye's energy minister confirmed parallel discussions with the US and South Korea for second-plant technology (Oct 2025).
Akkuyu deepens Turkiye's energy relationship with Russia at a moment when Ankara is also diversifying geopolitically. The 93% Russian-owned structure, the Rosatom operating role, and fuel supply dependency create a significant long-term geopolitical entanglement.

4.2 Renewables push — YEKA model and 2035 ambitions

  • Target: 120 GW wind + solar by 2035 (from ~32 GW today). Near-quadrupling in 10 years.
  • YEKA tenders auction large blocks (1–3 GW) bundled with domestic manufacturing requirements. Recent solar YEKA bids: 2–3 €cent/kWh — among the lowest in the region.
  • Storage (DEKA) tender model, analogous to YEKA, being prepared for 2026 — critical for integrating the fast-growing intermittent fleet.
  • Offshore wind development scoped for 2026 under a YEKA-style offshore framework.

4.3 Turkiye as regional energy hub

  • ~19 TWh exported to Bulgaria in 2024 via Strandzha–Malkoclar (near-record). Corridor has ~43 TWh/year theoretical capacity, currently underutilised.
  • Second Greece–Turkiye 400 kV line (2 GW, ~2029) would add a second EU export corridor, backed by €100M EU RRF funding.
  • Azerbaijan–Turkiye–Georgia–Bulgaria quadrilateral green energy framework (2024) formalises Turkiye's role as transit hub for Azerbaijani renewables to EU.

4.4 Demand growth and supply adequacy

  • Demand grew 5.5% in 2024 — one of the fastest rates globally. Forecasts: 455 TWh by 2030, 511 TWh by 2035. Requires ~150–160 GW installed (vs 121 GW today).
  • Coal (~35% of generation) is declining in share but remains structurally important baseload — domestic lignite employment makes fast phase-out politically difficult.
  • Gas (~24 GW) is the main flexibility resource but expensive and import-dependent. LNG infrastructure expanding.
Turkiye's structural tension: demand grows faster than renewable capacity can be built, grid infrastructure is lagging behind, and the coal exit is constrained by industrial and social policy. Akkuyu's 4.8 GW helps but won't close the gap alone.

5. Five things to take away

1
Demand is growing faster than almost any large economy — +5.5% in 2024, 455 TWh by 2030. The capacity gap is real and creates urgency around every investment decision.
2
Akkuyu is Turkiye's most consequential energy event in a generation. Unit 1 in trial operation since July 2025, full 4.8 GW by ~2029. It reduces gas dependence but deepens the Russian energy entanglement.
3
The YEKA model delivers world-competitive renewable prices (solar at 2–3 €cent/kWh). Combined with ENTSO-E synchronisation, Turkiye is positioning as a low-cost renewable exporter to Europe — the Bulgaria corridor (19 TWh in 2024) is the first proof point.
4
The second Greece–Turkiye 400 kV line (~2029, 2 GW) and the Bulgaria–Azerbaijan–Turkiye quadrilateral are the physical and political infrastructure of Turkiye's hub ambition. Grid bottlenecks on both sides remain the binding constraint.
5
Coal is not going away quickly. With ~35% of generation and no new plants under construction, the share will decline — but domestic lignite and industrial energy costs make fast phase-out politically very hard.
