Generation Mix & Sector Overview

Denmark is one of the world's most advanced wind energy systems — the country generates approximately 60% of its electricity from wind, with biomass/bioenergy at ~19% and a rapidly growing solar contribution (~14%) making up most of the remainder. In 2025, more than 92% of Denmark's electricity came from low-carbon sources, one of the highest shares among large European economies. Fossil fuel use in power generation has fallen to near zero. Denmark has set a legally binding target of 100% renewable electricity by 2030 and a solar target of 20 GW by 2030, up from 4 GW at end-2024.

The Wind-First Energy Model

Denmark's energy model is built around onshore wind as baseload, offshore wind as growth driver, and strong European interconnection for balancing. The system routinely exports electricity during high-wind periods and imports during low-wind periods — net exporter in most years:

  • Onshore wind (~12.5 GW): Mature, highly efficient fleet; most turbines modern (5–10 MW); feed-in premium system phased out in favour of market contracts; community ownership model (vindmøllelaug) remains prevalent.
  • Offshore wind (~3.5 GW operational): Vesterhav Nord and Sud (350 MW, 2023), Kriegers Flak (605 MW), Horns Rev III (407 MW); Thor (1 GW) under construction targeting 2027-28; Danish government aiming for 35 GW offshore by 2050.
  • Interconnectors as a core asset: Denmark connects to Norway, Sweden, Germany, and now the UK (Viking Link). These connections allow Denmark to balance variable wind output through Norwegian hydro (pumped storage equivalent) and the broader European market.
  • District heating integration: Denmark's uniquely high district heating penetration (~63% of buildings) means electricity and heat markets are tightly coupled; heat pumps and CHP plants serve as flexible demand that absorbs wind surplus.
Denmark demonstrates that 100% renewable electricity is not a theoretical target but an engineering reality in a market economy. The system already exceeds 92% clean electricity in 2025, with the remaining 8% covered by backup gas and biomass during prolonged low-wind periods. The 2030 target of 100% RE is achievable with the Thor offshore project alone — the challenge is not generation but balancing and cross-border integration with neighbouring grids that are less flexible.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Denmark is the world's clearest proof of concept for a wind-dominant electricity system at scale — 60% wind share in a market economy with no blackouts and competitive prices. This is not achieved through autocratic planning or massive subsidy distortions, but through decades of technology learning, grid investment, interconnector development, and market design. Denmark's experience is the primary reference case for countries planning 40–70% variable RE shares — the critical lesson being that interconnection and demand flexibility matter as much as generation capacity.
2
The solar buildout from 4 GW to 20 GW by 2030 is the next major policy bet — and represents an entirely different deployment challenge from offshore wind. Offshore wind is utility-scale, well-understood in Denmark, and planned by the state. Reaching 20 GW of solar in 6 years requires permitting hundreds of smaller projects, managing land-use conflicts in a flat agricultural country, and upgrading distribution networks not designed for bi-directional power flows. The bottleneck will be permitting and grid connection speed, not technology cost.
3
Viking Link (700 MW to the UK) is a template for the next generation of European offshore interconnectors — and Denmark is well-positioned to benefit from UK decarbonisation. The UK's offshore wind buildout creates periods of massive surplus that, without interconnectors, are either curtailed or create negative prices. Viking Link provides an export route from Danish offshore surplus to UK demand — particularly valuable during UK winter peaks when UK offshore wind generation often falls short. Denmark stands to be a net electricity exporter to the UK for decades.
4
Ørsted's financial difficulties in 2023-24 (US project write-downs, cost overruns) reflect a sector-wide cost problem for offshore wind — not a Danish policy failure. The company's retreat from some US projects does not undermine the domestic Danish offshore programme, which benefits from shorter supply chains, established port infrastructure, and a government-backed long-term investment framework. Ørsted remains the world's dominant offshore developer and its difficulties highlight the global supply chain constraints that all offshore wind markets will face in the 2025-2030 period.
5
Denmark's green hydrogen ambitions — aiming to produce 4–6 GW of electrolysis capacity by 2030 — depend entirely on whether surplus offshore wind can be converted competitively. The business case requires very low-cost, high-utilisation electricity. Denmark's geography (North Sea access), existing gas infrastructure (potential for repurposing), and European pipeline connections (to Germany and the Netherlands) make it structurally well-suited for export-oriented green hydrogen production. The 2030 timeline is ambitious but the fundamentals are stronger than in most European countries.
