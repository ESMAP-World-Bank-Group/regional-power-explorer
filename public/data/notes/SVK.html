Generation Mix & Sector Overview

Slovakia operates one of Central Europe's cleanest electricity systems, with nuclear power accounting for approximately 60% of generation and hydro a further ~18%. The country has very low fossil fuel use in electricity (gas ~10%, coal nearly phased out) — a mix that predates the EU's decarbonisation agenda and reflects a long-standing commitment to nuclear energy built on Soviet-era technology. The landmark event of Slovakia's recent energy history is the commissioning of Mochovce Unit 3 in 2023 — a decade-long construction saga that ultimately delivered a 471 MW addition to the nuclear fleet, and Unit 4 completing commissioning in 2024-25. The operator is Slovenské Elektrárne (SE), majority owned by Enel (Italy, 66%) and the Slovak state (34%). The TSO is SEPS.

Nuclear fleet: 6 units at 2 sitesBohunice: 2 × VVER-440 units (V-213 type, total ~880 MW); commissioned 1985-85; SW Slovakia. Mochovce: 4 × VVER-440 units (V-213 upgraded); Units 1&2 commissioned 1998-2000; Units 3&4 commissioned 2023 and 2024-25. All fuel supply from TVEL (Russia) — with Westinghouse alternative fuel qualification under evaluation following Czech precedent. Slovak nuclear provides ~15-16 TWh/year.
Mochovce 3&4: Europe's most delayed nuclear projectMochovce 3&4 construction resumed in 2008 (originally planned 1980s, halted 1993). Commissioning repeatedly delayed: 2012, 2017, 2019, 2021, 2022, 2023 finally for Unit 3. Total cost: €5.6B (estimated at €2.8B in 2008). Issues: post-Fukushima safety upgrades, design changes, Enel write-downs, COVID-19. Unit 3 finally synchronized to grid March 2023; Unit 4 commissioning mid-2024. Despite the saga, both units operational — adding ~1 GW to Slovakia's nuclear fleet.
Váh River hydro cascadeThe Váh River system (west Slovakia) carries a series of run-of-river plants operated by Slovenské Elektrárne and Slovak Water Management Enterprise; total ~900 MW. Pumped storage: Čierny Váh (735 MW) — the largest pumped storage facility in Central Europe; serves as balancing plant for Slovak nuclear baseload and regional grid. Danube border plants shared with Hungary (Gabčíkovo, 720 MW Slovak side).
Enel ownership: geopolitical dimensionEnel (Italy) holds 66% of Slovenské Elektrárne — acquired 2006 as part of Slovak privatisation. After Mochovce delays and cost overruns, Slovak government repeatedly sought to buy back full control. Slovak state (EPH-linked funds + government) has been accumulating stake. Enel's long-term hold of Slovak nuclear assets is uncertain; potential state reacquisition ongoing. Nuclear ownership security is strategically important given fuel supply dependencies.
Bohunice decommissioning: EU-fundedSlovakia shut down its two older Bohunice V-1 VVER-440 reactors (units 1&2) in 2006-08 as an EU accession condition — despite public opposition. These were different from the currently operating Bohunice V-2 units. EU provided €615M in decommissioning aid. Slovakia considers this an unjust condition; the decommissioning is still ongoing (waste management complex, projected completion 2025+).

Central Europe's Nuclear Export Aspiration

Slovakia's nuclear expertise — built over four decades of VVER operation — positions it as a potential nuclear services exporter to the growing Central European nuclear expansion market:

  • VVER fuel diversification: Like Czech Republic and Hungary, Slovakia's nuclear fleet runs on Russian TVEL fuel. Post-2022, Slovakia has accelerated evaluation of Westinghouse VVER-440 fuel alternatives (WAFA — Westinghouse Advanced Fuel Assembly for VVER-440). Successful qualification would allow Slovak nuclear to operate without Russian fuel supply — a significant energy security upgrade.
  • Slovak grid transit role: SEPS operates in a central position in the Central European grid, interconnected with Czech Republic, Austria, Hungary, Ukraine, and Poland. Slovak grid carries significant transit flows between Germany/Czech Republic and Hungary/Romania. Ukraine's grid (integrated with ENTSO-E since 2022) feeds into Western Europe partly via Slovakia.
  • Solar growth acceleration: Slovakia's solar development was minimal until 2022, when EU REPowerEU funds and rising electricity prices triggered rapid small-scale solar growth. Utility-scale solar is being accelerated under Slovakia's NECP 2024 revision — targeting 4-5 GW solar by 2030 from a near-zero base.
Slovakia's Mochovce 3&4 story is Europe's most instructive nuclear cautionary tale — not because the units were cancelled, but because they were completed despite everything. The project survived a decade of delays, €3B in cost overruns, multiple operator write-downs, and post-Fukushima regulatory revision. The result: two operational 471 MW units that will produce clean electricity for 40+ years. The lesson is not that European nuclear is impossible — it is that European nuclear is very expensive when executed without Korean or Chinese cost discipline. If KHNP delivers Dukovany 5 at anything approaching its quoted price, it will transform European nuclear economics.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Slovakia's ~80% low-carbon electricity (nuclear + hydro) — achieved in a country with no coal and no significant wind — demonstrates that the CE4/Visegrád group's pro-nuclear stance is not merely political posturing but reflects genuinely clean electricity systems that have avoided the emissions of their coal-heavy neighbours. Slovakia's electricity CO₂ intensity is among the EU's lowest (~70g CO₂/kWh vs. Poland's ~700g). This gives Slovakia significant credibility in EU climate debates as a country that has delivered low-carbon electricity without needing to choose between intermittent RE and fossil backup.
2
The Mochovce 3&4 completion — despite the longest and costliest nuclear construction in modern European history — validates the Slovak government's decision to continue rather than cancel, given the 40-year operational lifetime of the added ~1 GW of clean baseload. Had Slovakia cancelled Mochovce 3&4 in 2015 (when costs first exceeded €4B), the €2.5B already spent would have been sunk with no return. The additional €1.5-2B to complete produced two units generating ~6 TWh/year for 40+ years at near-zero marginal cost. The NPV of that generation, at current Czech/Slovak electricity prices, substantially exceeds total construction cost — even accounting for delays.
3
Slovakia's TVEL fuel dependency is its single largest energy security vulnerability — and fuel diversification to Westinghouse VVER-440 fuel is the most consequential near-term energy policy decision facing Slovak nuclear operators. Westinghouse's qualification of VVER-440 alternative fuel for Dukovany (Czech) creates a direct pathway for Slovak adoption. If Slovakia qualifies Westinghouse fuel for Mochovce VVER-440s, it eliminates Russian fuel leverage over ~60% of Slovak electricity. The technical challenge is significant (fuel pin design, core neutronic calculations) but has been demonstrated in Czech reactors. Slovak timing depends on SE (Enel) commercial willingness and ÚJADROVÁ (nuclear safety authority) licensing timelines.
4
Enel's 66% ownership of Slovenské Elektrárne creates a governance misalignment: an Italian commercial utility manages Slovakia's strategically critical nuclear infrastructure on a profit-maximisation mandate that may diverge from Slovak energy security priorities. Enel wrote down its Slovak nuclear investment repeatedly during Mochovce delays; its long-term commitment to Slovak nuclear investment (safety upgrades, fuel diversification, life extensions) depends on returns being sufficient to justify the capital. Slovak state reacquisition of a controlling stake would align governance with national security interests — but requires political will and significant capital that Slovakia's constrained budget makes difficult.
5
Slovakia's solar development — accelerating rapidly from near-zero — must be managed carefully to avoid the curtailment and grid saturation problems that hit Czech Republic, Poland, and Germany in their solar boom phases. Slovakia's nuclear baseload is relatively inflexible (nuclear plants are cheapest operating at constant output). Rapid solar addition creates midday surplus that nuclear cannot easily back down to absorb. Čierny Váh pumped storage (735 MW) provides partial buffer — but it was designed for nuclear balancing, not solar integration. Grid-scale batteries and enhanced pumped storage capacity will be needed before 2030 to absorb 4+ GW of solar into a nuclear-anchored system.
