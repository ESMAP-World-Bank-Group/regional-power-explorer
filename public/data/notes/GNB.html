Chronic political instability. Guinea-Bissau has experienced 9 coups or coup attempts since independence in 1974 — one of the highest rates globally. The most recent significant crisis was in February 2022 when President Embaló survived an armed attack on the presidential palace. The country remains fragile: frequent government turnovers, drug trafficking (Guinea-Bissau is a major cocaine transit hub), and weak institutional capacity have made sustainable development extremely difficult. Despite this instability, the country has not experienced the jihadist insurgency affecting Sahel neighbours. The electricity sector, while extremely small, benefits from OMVG's multilateral structure as a relative island of technocratic stability.

1. Energy context

Guinea-Bissau has one of the smallest and most fragile electricity systems in the world, let alone WAPP. A population of ~2 million is served by approximately 30 MW of installed HFO/diesel capacity, almost all concentrated in the capital Bissau. The state utility EAGB (Empresa de Electricidade e Águas da Guiné-Bissau) operates at a near-permanent deficit — HFO fuel costs exceed revenue, billing collection is poor, and government budget support is irregular. Most of Bissau receives electricity for only a few hours per day; provincial towns have essentially no grid service.

The November 2023 commissioning of the OMVG (Organisation pour la Mise en Valeur du fleuve Gambie) transmission link — carrying Guinea's Kaléta hydropower toward Bissau — represents by far the most significant electricity sector development in the country's recent history. Like The Gambia, Guinea-Bissau is expected to transition from essentially 100% diesel to a mix of hydro (OMVG) and solar over the next 2–3 years, pending commercial agreement finalisations and grid upgrade work.

  • HFO / diesel (effectively 100% of domestic generation): EAGB operates two small HFO generators at the Bandim and Braima Seide power stations in Bissau, supplemented by a Karpowership floating HFO barge (~25 MW). The total available capacity fluctuates significantly depending on plant maintenance status and HFO fuel availability. Routine failures mean Bissau residents experience daily supply interruptions of 12+ hours.
  • OMVG hydro (from late 2023): The OMVG 225 kV transmission line from Guinea's Kaléta dam (via Senegal and The Gambia) reached partial operational status in late 2023. For Guinea-Bissau, this means the possibility of accessing up to ~15–20 MW of Kaléta hydro power for Bissau — which would be transformative given current domestic generation of only ~30 MW total. Commercial and metering arrangements for the Bissau connection are still being finalised.
  • Solar (nascent): Several small solar installations at health centres, schools, and ECOWAS-funded mini-grids in provincial towns. A 10 MWp solar plant for Bissau has been discussed in donor funding rounds but has not yet reached financial close. The off-grid solar market (solar lanterns, SHS) has grown through NGO-funded programmes and private importers.

3. Sector organisation

EAGB — Empresa de Electricidade e Águas da Guiné-Bissau Financially Non-viable

EAGB is the vertically-integrated state utility responsible for electricity and water. Its financial position is arguably the most precarious of any WAPP member utility: fuel costs routinely exceed revenue; billing collection is very low (~30–40%); government budget transfers are irregular and insufficient; and technical losses exceed 40%. EAGB has virtually no capital investment capacity and relies entirely on donor and government financing for any new assets. Multiple World Bank and AfDB utility reform operations have supported EAGB with limited sustained impact. The Karpowership contract, while expensive, has been EAGB's only reliable source of dispatchable power — a perverse dependency.

4. Key issues and challenges

  • Financial non-viability of EAGB: EAGB cannot function as a going concern under current conditions. HFO fuel costs consume revenues multiple times over. Tariffs are low (to protect the poorest consumers) but even full collection at current tariff levels would not cover EAGB's costs. This is a structural failure requiring either tariff reform + efficiency improvements, or sustained subsidy transfer from an already-constrained government budget. The OMVG lower-cost hydro could theoretically break this cycle by reducing the fuel cost component, if the commercial structure is right.
  • Political instability as sector constraint: No electricity sector reform can be sustained across the frequent government changes Guinea-Bissau experiences. PPAs cannot be signed, tariff reform cannot be implemented, and donor programme continuity is disrupted every time the government changes. The electricity sector requires at minimum 10–15 years of consistent policy direction to transition meaningfully — a duration that has never been achieved under the post-independence political system.
  • OMVG — the transformative but fragile hope: OMVG power reaching Bissau is genuinely transformational: it could replace essentially all of EAGB's expensive diesel generation. But EAGB's financial capacity to pay for bulk power is nearly zero. Without a concessional payment arrangement (subsidised tariff for OMVG power to GNB, financed by ECOWAS or multilateral donors), the commercial logic of OMVG for Guinea-Bissau is severely constrained. The institutional architecture of OMVG must explicitly account for Guinea-Bissau's payment incapacity.
  • Provincial energy poverty: Outside Bissau, Guinea-Bissau has essentially no electricity service. Rural populations rely on candles, kerosene lamps, and increasingly on small off-grid solar lanterns (imported from China). There is no grid to extend — provincial towns would require either stand-alone diesel mini-grids or off-grid solar mini-grids to have any electricity service. The donor community has funded several such mini-grids in Bafatá, Gabu, and other towns, but coverage remains extremely sparse.

5. Five key takeaways

1
Guinea-Bissau has the smallest electricity system in WAPP (80 GWh/year, essentially all confined to Bissau city) and arguably the most financially non-viable utility in the region — EAGB exists as a functioning institution only because of Karpowership's contract, government subsidies, and donor life support.
2
The OMVG Kaléta link (late 2023) is Guinea-Bissau's most significant electricity sector development in decades — but converting physical line availability into reliable, affordable, commercially-contracted power supply requires solving EAGB's payment capacity problem, which is as much a financing challenge as an infrastructure one.
3
Political instability is the root cause of the electricity sector's dysfunction: nine coups since 1974, frequent government changes, and a political economy dominated by drug trafficking interests have made it impossible to sustain the decade-plus policy continuity that electricity sector transformation requires.
4
OMVG's multilateral governance structure (Guinea, Senegal, Gambia, Guinea-Bissau) provides relative institutional stability for Guinea-Bissau's energy future — the OMVG secretariat is a more durable anchor than any bilateral arrangement with EAGB, and donors should channel electricity support through OMVG rather than EAGB wherever possible.
5
Off-grid solar is Guinea-Bissau's most realistic near-term rural electrification pathway: provincial towns are beyond any affordable grid extension, the OMVG line serves only Bissau city, and the solar SHS market is growing through private channels — what is missing is a structured government or donor programme to accelerate and quality-control this organic transition.
