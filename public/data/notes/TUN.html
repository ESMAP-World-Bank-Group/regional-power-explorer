Generation Mix & Capacity

Tunisia has 5,944 MW of installed capacity across 25 power plants, generating 19,395 GWh in 2024. Gas accounts for approximately 95% of electricity generation, supplied almost entirely through imports from Algeria (providing ~75% of Tunisia's gas in 2024) and declining domestic Tunisian production. Renewable capacity stood at only 777 MW as of April 2025 — predominantly small-scale solar PV, wind (Borj Cedria, Bizerte), and micro-hydro. Tunisia's electricity sector faces a structural cost crisis: heavy reliance on imported gas (at market prices), subsidised consumer tariffs well below cost recovery, and STEG's accumulated financial losses.

STEG's Financial Crisis & Reform Path

Tunisia's electricity sector cannot finance its transition without addressing STEG's fundamental financial distress:

  • Consumer electricity tariffs are significantly below the cost of production — particularly as gas must be imported at international market prices while electricity is sold at subsidised domestic rates. STEG's annual losses are partially covered by state transfers and partially accumulated as debt.
  • The TEREG programme (World Bank $430M, Nov 2025) explicitly conditions financing on tariff reform and regulatory improvements — moving Tunisia toward cost-reflective pricing and an independent energy regulator.
  • Tunisia's fiscal crisis (IMF negotiations, sovereign debt restructuring discussions) limits the state's ability to continue subsidising STEG indefinitely, creating pressure for reform that prior periods of political stability had allowed to be deferred.
  • Private investment in renewable energy requires a creditworthy offtaker (STEG) and predictable PPA enforcement — both currently uncertain, which is why the TEREG private investment targets have been slow to materialise.
Tunisia is caught between a clear strategic logic (replace expensive imported gas with cheap domestic solar) and institutional constraints (STEG financial distress, slow procurement, tariff subsidies, unclear RE regulation). The World Bank's $430M TEREG programme is the most significant international energy finance commitment to Tunisia in a decade and could serve as the catalyst for regulatory modernisation. The Kairouan 100 MW plant commissioning in December 2025 is a concrete proof point. Whether it's followed by 4 GW more by 2030 depends on whether STEG reform moves faster than Tunisia's broader fiscal challenges deteriorate.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Tunisia's gas import bill is the engine of its energy crisis and the strongest argument for solar deployment. Paying for Algerian gas at near-market prices while selling electricity to consumers at subsidised tariffs creates a structural loss that grows with every year of solar delay. Kairouan-level solar costs (~$0.025–0.035/kWh in Tunisia's market) are far below the avoided cost of gas-fired electricity (~$0.08–0.12/kWh). The economics have never been more compelling; the constraint is regulatory and institutional, not economic.
2
The TEREG programme is Tunisia's best chance for an accelerated energy transition — but depends on tariff reform the government has long avoided. World Bank conditionality requires Tunisia to move toward cost-reflective tariffs and establish an independent regulator. Both have been promised in multiple prior reform programmes without delivery. The current fiscal pressure — Tunisia cannot afford indefinite STEG subsidies — may finally create the political conditions for implementation.
3
Tunisia's wind resource (particularly northern coast and northwest) is underexploited and could be its most competitive renewable source. Capacity factors of 35–40% along the northern coast compare favourably with Moroccan Atlantic wind. Yet only ~350 MW of wind is installed. The combination of good wind, proximity to European markets, and the planned Tunisia–Italy subsea cable (Elmed/HPCM, 600 MW HVDC) creates an export opportunity that would make Tunisian wind internationally competitive.
4
The Tunisia–Italy Elmed HVDC cable (600 MW) is strategically transformative if realised. A subsea cable directly connecting Tunisia to the Italian grid would allow Tunisia to export solar and wind electricity to Europe during peak production and import when needed — creating revenue and grid flexibility simultaneously. Project financing discussions are ongoing; the cable would validate Tunisia's place in Europe's clean energy supply chain and create a commercial anchor for the entire TEREG programme.
5
Tunisia's political context (post-Saied consolidation, IMF programme delays) creates risks for private RE investment that are underappreciated in project pipelines. Investor confidence in PPA enforcement and regulatory predictability depends on governance stability. The concentration of power since 2021, ongoing negotiations with the IMF, and questions about rule of law in commercial disputes create a risk premium for private capital that will either delay private RE investment or require higher returns — both of which slow the energy transition.
