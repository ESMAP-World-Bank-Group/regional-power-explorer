1. Energy context

Armenia is a landlocked country of 3 million people with two closed borders – Turkey and Azerbaijan – since the early 1990s. 78.6% of total primary energy supply was imported in 2020, dominated by Russian natural gas (59.6%) via a single corridor through Georgia. Armenia is the only nuclear power producer in the South Caucasus – a key strategic differentiator.

The electricity sector is the one segment where Armenia achieves effective autonomy: it is a net exporter, operates a diversified nuclear-hydro-gas mix, and has carried out a substantial market reform since the mid-1990s – moving from a near-bankrupt Soviet-era monopoly to a partially privatised market.

  • Nuclear – Metsamor (408 MW): sole baseload anchor (~33% of output). VVER-440, commissioned 1980. Life extended to 2036 via $65M Rosatom contract (Dec 2024). Currently in maintenance shutdown Apr–Sep 2026.
  • Large hydro (965 MW): Sevan-Hrazdan cascade (IEC/Tashir, 561 MW) and Vorotan cascade (ContourGlobal/KKR, 404 MW). Seasonal peak spring/summer.
  • Small hydro (~390 MW): 300+ plants, fragmented ownership. Developed via feed-in tariffs since 2007.
  • Gas thermal (690 MW): Hrazdan Unit 5 (440 MW, Tashir) and Yerevan CCGT (250 MW, ArmPower). Cover winter peak; fuel the Iran barter circuit. Dependent on Gazprom Armenia.
  • Solar PV (~800 MW): ~15% of 2025 generation. Fast-growing – utility IPPs and rooftop prosumers, feed-in tariff driven.
  • Wind (<10 MW): near-zero despite 4 900 MW theoretical potential (USAID).

2.4 The Armenia–Iran gas-for-electricity barter

Iran supplies ~365 Mm³/year of gas; Armenia delivers 3 kWh per m³ received (~1.1 TWh/year, 88% of total exports). Gas fuels the Yerevan CCGT and Hrazdan Unit 5 whose output is re-exported – a closed circuit. Benefits: implicit gas price below the Russian rate + partial Gazprom hedge. The third 400 kV line will double the exchangeable volume.

3. Sector organisation and actors

3.1 Market structure

Vertically separated but fully regulated. No spot market, no retail competition. PSRC sets all tariffs. HVEN operates as single buyer – procuring from generators under annually approved quota contracts at regulated prices, dispatching to ENA for distribution to end consumers.

4. Key issues and dynamics

4.1 Metsamor – the nuclear succession question

  • VVER-440 V230, commissioned 1980. One of the oldest operating NPPs globally.
  • Life extended to 2036 via $65M Rosatom contract (Dec 2024). Currently in maintenance shutdown Apr–Sep 2026 – the main operational risk of the year.
  • New NPP: dedicated company created early 2025. PM Pashinyan confirmed SMR decision Oct 2025 – simultaneous talks with US, France, Russia, China, South Korea.
  • Supplier choice = direct geopolitical litmus test between Moscow, Washington and Paris.

4.2 Renewables acceleration

  • Target: 66% renewables by 2036 (from 7% in 2012). Requires 2 185 MW new capacity.
  • Solar PV at ~15% of 2025 generation (~800 MW). Feed-in tariff driven.
  • Wind: near-zero despite 4 900 MW potential. Regulatory and grid barriers unaddressed.
  • World Bank least-cost plan (2024): 2 630 MW PV + 375 MW wind + 580 MW hydro + 130 MW storage + 600 MW NPP by 2033. Total ~$13bn.

4.3 ENA nationalisation – 2025 governance shock

  • June 2025: Tashir owner Karapetyan arrested. PM announces nationalisation.
  • 3 July 2025: Parliament votes. November 2025: PSRC revokes ENA licence.
  • ~1M subscribers, 5 TWh/yr, 47–54bn AMD investment programme under temporary administrator. Final structure unclear.

4.4 Supply security

  • Gas corridor risk: single Russia–Georgia pipeline. Any disruption → reliance on hydro + nuclear only.
  • Nuclear dependency: 5-month maintenance shutdown (2026) requires compensating via imports.
  • Infrastructure vulnerability: Sep 2022 Azerbaijani strike on the Armenia–Iran HV line as precedent.
  • Mitigants: installed capacity (~3 200 MW) well exceeds peak demand (~1 700 MW); solar growth reducing thermal exposure.
A normalisation with Turkey – whose peak load exceeds 58 GW – would be the single most transformative development for Armenia's long-term energy security. It remains a geopolitical question, not a technical one.

5. Five things to take away

1
Metsamor is irreplaceable short-term. Its Apr–Sep 2026 maintenance shutdown is the main operational risk of the year. Any extension becomes a supply crisis.
2
The Iran barter is Armenia's most valuable bilateral energy arrangement – cheaper gas, Gazprom hedge, strategic depth. The third 400 kV line will cement it.
3
ENA's nationalisation resolves a political problem but opens an operational transition of uncertain duration – the main governance risk for 2025–2026.
4
The new NPP supplier decision is the decade's defining energy choice – and a geopolitical litmus test between Moscow, Washington and Paris.
5
Solar growth is real, but the 66% target by 2036 requires ~$13bn, a functional DSO, and wind development that has not yet started. All three are uncertain.
