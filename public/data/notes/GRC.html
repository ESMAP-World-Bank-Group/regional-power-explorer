Generation Mix & Sector Overview

Greece has undergone one of the EU's most dramatic electricity transitions in recent years: from a lignite-dominated system (60%+ coal in 2016) to 50%+ renewable in 2024, driven by rapid solar and wind deployment. Wind (~7 GW, especially on the Aegean islands and Thrace coast) and solar (~12 GW, accelerating quickly) now combine with hydro (~3 GW, northern Greece rivers) to form the RE backbone. Gas (~25-30%) provides backup and peaking. Lignite — historically centred on the Ptolemaida and Megalopolis basins operated by PPC — is being phased out: target 2028 under the updated National Energy and Climate Plan. The main utility is PPC (Public Power Corporation / ΔΕΗ), partially privatised (state now ~34%), competing with independent producers RWE, Mytilineos, Terna Energy. The TSO is ADMIE/IPTO. Greece's RE ambition extends beyond its borders — it is developing submarine interconnectors to become a clean electricity exporter to the Eastern Mediterranean and a solar power bridge between Africa and Europe.

PPC (DEI): from lignite giant to RE developerPublic Power Corporation (Δημόσια Επιχείρηση Ηλεκτρισμού); state formerly held 51%, now ~34% after partial privatisation 2021-23; operates remaining lignite plants (closing 2028), hydro fleet, and is building 5+ GW RE (solar, wind) under its Green Agenda transformation plan; €6B RE investment plan 2021-2026; acquired Elven Energy (Romania), entering CEE market. Mytilineos (listed industrial group) and Terna Energy (GEK Terna subsidiary) are the main independent generators.
EuroAsia Interconnector: Crete-Cyprus-Israel HVDCProposed 2,000 MW HVDC submarine cable: Israel → Cyprus → Crete → mainland Greece → Italy; world's longest and deepest submarine power cable (~1,200 km, max depth 3,000m); EuroAsia Interconnector Ltd (private developer); PCI (Project of Common Interest) status from EU; would connect the isolated Cypriot grid to the European network for the first time; also enables Israeli solar exports to Europe; Israel grid synchronisation required; financing at ~€5-6B; FID status: pre-FID, environmental and routing challenges ongoing.
GREGY: Greece-Egypt solar superhighway (3 GW)Greece-Egypt Interconnector (GREGY): 3 GW HVDC submarine cable (~1,400 km, Sidi Krir Egypt → Crete → mainland Greece); would carry Egyptian solar (world's lowest LCOE) and wind to European markets. Copelouzos Group (Greek) + Egyptian government + ACWA Power (Saudi). EU PCI status 2023. Financing: €3-4B. Timeline: FID target 2027; operational ~2030-31. If built, GREGY would be the world's longest power interconnector and a model for Mediterranean RE export corridors.
Aegean island electrification programmeGreece's 3,000+ islands include ~50 inhabited non-interconnected islands with isolated diesel-fired mini-grids (Mykonos, Santorini, Lesbos, Rhodes, Corfu). Electricity prices on non-interconnected islands are significantly higher (consumers pay mainland tariffs; state subsidises the difference — €500M+/year). ADMIE is building submarine HV cables to connect major islands to the mainland grid (Cyclades, Dodecanese, Ionian projects). Each interconnected island eliminates diesel generation and enables RE deployment.
Aegean offshore wind: world-class resourceAegean winds (Meltemi, N-NE summer wind) deliver capacity factors of 40-50% — among Europe's highest offshore. The Aegean islands have shallow coastal waters suitable for fixed-foundation offshore wind. Greece is developing its offshore wind regulatory framework (Offshore Wind Law 2022); first offshore tenders expected 2025-26. Target: 2 GW offshore by 2030. Main challenge: Aegean territorial disputes with Turkiye complicate maritime zone demarcation for offshore wind licensing.

The Eastern Mediterranean Energy Crossroads

Greece's geographic position — at the intersection of Europe, Middle East, and Africa — has made it the pivot country for the EU's most ambitious cross-Mediterranean electricity interconnection projects:

  • Why Greece is the hub: Greece sits at the eastern tip of the EU electricity grid, closest to the Eastern Mediterranean's enormous RE potential (Egyptian solar at $15/MWh LCOE, Israeli wind, Jordanian solar). Connecting these resources to European demand markets requires passing through Greek waters and territory. Greece has proactively sought this hub role — lobbying for PCI status for EuroAsia and GREGY, offering fast-track permitting, and investing in domestic grid reinforcement.
  • Crete's special role: Crete (700,000 people) is currently an isolated island grid, supplied partly by diesel. The Ariadne submarine cable (HVDC, 500 MW, Crete-Attica) is under construction (commissioning 2024-25) — ending Crete's energy isolation. Crete is then the key waystation for both EuroAsia (Israel-Cyprus-Crete) and GREGY (Egypt-Crete) cables. Crete's grid is being upgraded as a regional hub rather than just a local distribution network.
  • Gas transit role: The Trans Adriatic Pipeline (TAP) — part of the Southern Gas Corridor from Azerbaijan — enters the EU at the Greek-Turkish border, crosses Greece, and exits under the Adriatic to Italy. Greece hosts ~550 km of TAP, making it a gas transit country as well as an electricity RE hub. The FSRU at Alexandroupolis (northeastern Greece) provides additional LNG import capacity for Greece, Bulgaria, and North Macedonia.
  • Geopolitical dimension: Greece-Turkiye tensions over Aegean delimitation complicate offshore wind licensing, fishing rights, and cable routing. The EuroAsia Interconnector cable avoids Turkish waters entirely — a deliberate routing choice. Greek-Egyptian-Israeli trilateral energy cooperation (GREGY, earlier East Med Gas Pipeline discussions) reflects a strategic alignment that extends beyond electricity.
Greece is the most strategically underrated energy country in Europe. A decade ago it was associated primarily with fiscal crisis and energy sector dysfunction; by 2026 it operates a 50%+ RE system, is developing the world's most ambitious cross-Mediterranean electricity interconnectors, and is positioned to become the EU's primary electricity gateway to African and Middle Eastern solar resources. The question is not whether Greece's energy transition is real — it clearly is — but whether the EuroAsia and GREGY cables will actually be financed, built, and operated within their announced timelines.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Greece's electricity sector transformation — from 60% lignite in 2016 to 50%+ renewable in 2024 — is one of the fastest and most complete energy transitions in EU history, achieved in eight years without nuclear power, in a country that was simultaneously managing a sovereign debt crisis. This is not a wealthy country's decarbonisation story. Greece's RE boom was driven by competitive renewable auctions, EU taxonomy-eligible green bonds for PPC, falling solar and wind costs, and the EU ETS price making lignite uneconomical. The fiscal crisis paradoxically helped: Greece had to privatise and liberalise its electricity market earlier than it might have chosen, which accelerated private RE investment.
2
The GREGY interconnector — connecting Egyptian solar to European demand via Greece — is the most transformative infrastructure proposal in European energy since the North Sea Wind Power Hub, and its success or failure will determine whether the EU can access the world's cheapest RE at scale. Egyptian solar LCOE is $12-18/MWh — less than half European solar costs. If GREGY delivers 3 GW of Egyptian solar to European markets, it demonstrates a replicable model for Mediterranean RE import corridors (Morocco-Spain, Tunisia-Italy, Libya-Italy). The infrastructure cost (~€4B) is trivial relative to the long-term supply value. The barrier is political and regulatory, not economic or technical.
3
PPC's transformation from a state-owned lignite monopoly to a partially privatised diversified RE developer — in less than five years — is among the most dramatic utility transformations in EU history, and its success is directly linked to Greece's willingness to accept EU-mandated market liberalisation as a condition of its debt restructuring programme. PPC was the EU's most emissions-intensive utility per unit of generation as recently as 2018. By 2024, lignite represents a minority and declining share of its portfolio. The company's €6B RE investment plan, CEE acquisitions, and green bond programme make it a credible European RE developer — not merely a reformed domestic monopoly. Forced liberalisation and EU competition rules created competitive pressure that accelerated PPC's transformation faster than any voluntary strategy would have.
4
Greece's Aegean island diesel dependency — ~50 inhabited islands on isolated mini-grids, costing the state €500M+/year in cross-subsidies — is a solvable problem through submarine interconnection, but has been partially neglected for decades because the political cost of visible electricity price increases on tourist islands outweighs the fiscal cost of hidden diesel subsidies. Interconnecting Mykonos, Santorini, or Rhodes to the mainland grid ends diesel generation and enables local RE deployment — producing cleaner, cheaper electricity. The barrier is not technological or even primarily financial; it is political economy. Tourist island electricity prices are a politically sensitive issue; ADMIE's interconnection programme is real and progressing, but has moved slowly relative to the fiscal savings available.
5
The Meltemi wind of the Aegean — the world's most consistent and powerful summer marine wind system in temperate latitudes — is Europe's most underexploited offshore wind resource, with potential capacity factors of 45-50% that exceed North Sea averages and would produce electricity more cheaply than almost any other European offshore location. The Meltemi blows north-to-northeast at sustained 8-10 Beaufort (34-55 km/h) across the central and southern Aegean from June through September — exactly the period when Greek electricity demand peaks from tourism and cooling. Wind that matches demand seasonality requires less storage and backup than wind that doesn't. Aegean offshore wind development is limited by Aegean delimitation disputes with Turkiye; resolving this geopolitical constraint is worth billions of euros in stranded RE investment annually.
