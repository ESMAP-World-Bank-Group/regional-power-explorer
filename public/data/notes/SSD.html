Data quality warning — Fragile and conflict-affected state. South Sudan has experienced recurring armed conflict since independence (2011) including a devastating civil war (2013–2018) and ongoing sub-national violence. Reliable electricity sector statistics are largely unavailable; most figures are rough estimates by the World Bank, UNDP, or aid organisations working in Juba. The 5.4% electrification rate (World Bank 2023) is itself uncertain and refers primarily to very limited diesel-powered supply in Juba. The situation in secondary cities and rural areas is essentially undocumented in terms of electricity access.

1. Energy context

South Sudan holds the lowest electricity access rate in Africa and one of the lowest on earth: approximately 5.4% of the population has access to electricity (World Bank 2023). In a country of ~12 million people, this means roughly 11 million lack any electricity access. There is no national electricity grid. What exists is a small diesel-powered system centred almost entirely on the capital Juba, operated by a combination of the state-owned Juba Power Station (~37 MW), private diesel generators from Ezra Energy (an IPP), and numerous small informal generators serving individual buildings and neighbourhoods.

South Sudan is paradoxically oil-rich: oil revenue (~USD 2–3 billion/year at pre-conflict peak) funded the state budget but virtually none was channelled into electricity infrastructure. Oil production has declined sharply from ~350,000 bbl/day at independence (2011) to ~120,000–140,000 bbl/day by 2024. Most oil fields are in the Unity State and Upper Nile region — where conflict has been most intense and where almost no electricity infrastructure exists.

The 2018 Revitalised Agreement on the Resolution of the Conflict in South Sudan (R-ARCSS) nominally ended the worst of the civil war. But political tensions, sub-national armed group activity, and a fragile transitional government have prevented serious investment in electricity infrastructure. The most credible donor vehicle is the World Bank EASE project (Electricity Access Scale-up and Expansion, ~USD 125 million, approved 2022), which focuses primarily on Juba and peri-urban solar hybrid systems.

2. The electricity system

2.1 Generation — diesel islands

South Sudan has no grid-scale generation in the conventional sense. Its electricity "system" consists of:

  • Juba Power Station: State-owned diesel generators, approximately 37 MW total installed. Output is highly variable depending on fuel availability. Frequent and prolonged outages are normal. Fuel supply (diesel) must be imported at significant cost via road from Uganda or Kenya.
  • Ezra Energy (Juba): Private IPP operating diesel HFO generators under a concession with the South Sudan Electricity Corporation (SSEC). Capacity estimates vary; reportedly ~25–40 MW operating at times. Power Purchase Agreement with the government has faced payment arrears issues.
  • Isolated generators (secondary cities): Malakal, Wau, Aweil, Torit, and other provincial capitals have minimal isolated diesel supply from municipal generators or aid organisations, typically ranging from 0.5 to 5 MW per town. These are not connected to any grid.
  • Off-grid solar: Growing presence of PAYGo solar home systems (d.light, BBOXX, SunFunder partners) particularly in rural areas. These provide lighting and phone charging but minimal productive use. Total off-grid solar installed base: estimated 50,000–100,000 small systems.
Tariff context: Electricity in Juba costs approximately USD 0.40–0.60/kWh from the grid and USD 0.80–1.50/kWh from informal diesel vendors — among the most expensive power in the world relative to local incomes. The median monthly household income in Juba is USD 80–120; a reliable electricity connection would consume a significant fraction of household income. This is why even when supply exists, it reaches only a small minority.

2.2 Grid — nothing exists outside Juba

South Sudan has no national transmission grid. There are no 132 kV or higher voltage lines connecting any two points in the country. Juba has a small 33 kV distribution network of limited reliability. All other urban centres operate on isolated low-voltage diesel grids of a few MVA or less. Rural South Sudan — 80% of the population — has no electricity infrastructure whatsoever beyond donor-funded solar installations.

4. Key issues and dynamics

4.1 Oil curse and electricity deficit

  • South Sudan's oil revenues — the dominant source of government income — have not been converted into electricity infrastructure. The political economy of a rentier state where elites capture oil revenues and have no interest in broad service delivery is the central explanation for 5.4% electrification despite >10 years of independence.
  • Declining oil production (reservoir depletion, pipeline disputes with Sudan) has further squeezed the fiscal space. The pipeline through Sudan to Port Sudan was disrupted from April 2023 (Sudan war) through much of 2024–2025, periodically halting South Sudan's oil exports entirely and pushing the government toward economic crisis.

4.2 The Uganda interconnector — game-changer if completed

  • The Nimule–Juba 220 kV transmission line, if completed, would connect Juba to Uganda's surplus hydro from Karuma and Owen Falls. Uganda has ~1 GW of excess capacity looking for buyers; Juba is a natural near-term off-taker. At 100 MW, this single interconnection would roughly triple the current electricity available to the capital.
  • The project has been in development for over a decade. NELSAP (Nile Equatorial Lakes Subsidiary Action Program) financing has been partially secured. Construction progress has been intermittent. The binding constraint is not engineering or financing — it is South Sudan's political environment, which makes contractors and financiers reluctant to commit to large-scale infrastructure.

5. Five things to take away

1
South Sudan has the lowest electrification rate in Africa — approximately 5.4% of its 12 million people have access to electricity. There is no national grid; the entire electricity system consists of a small diesel operation in Juba plus scattered isolated generators in provincial towns. This is not a development challenge — it is a near-total absence of modern energy infrastructure.
2
Electricity in Juba costs USD 0.40–0.60/kWh from the grid and up to USD 1.50/kWh from informal vendors — among the most expensive in the world relative to local incomes. High costs reflect the diesel-dependent supply chain, which requires expensive fuel imports by road from Uganda. This cost structure is self-perpetuating: without a grid, there is no scale for cost reduction; without cost reduction, demand remains low.
3
The Uganda–South Sudan 220 kV interconnector (Nimule–Juba) is the single highest-impact electricity infrastructure project for South Sudan. Uganda has surplus clean hydro; Juba needs reliable supply. The engineering is straightforward and the economics are workable. The binding constraint is South Sudan's political instability, which has prevented commitment from contractors and financiers for over a decade.
4
Oil revenues — the primary source of government income — have not been invested in electricity infrastructure. The political economy of oil-state governance (elite capture, no fiscal incentive for public service delivery) explains why an oil exporter has the world's lowest electrification rate. Declining oil production and the 2023 Sudan war (which disrupted South Sudan's export pipeline) have eliminated even the theoretical fiscal resource for investment.
5
The World Bank EASE project (USD 125 million) is the most credible current investment vehicle, focusing on solar hybrid supply for Juba, peri-urban mini-grids, and household solar. It will make a real difference for tens of thousands of households but is orders of magnitude below what nationwide electrification requires. Without peace, political stability, and a functioning government, no financing programme can deliver transformation.
