Generation Mix & Capacity

Bangladesh has achieved one of the world's fastest electricity sector expansions — growing from 5 GW in 2009 to ~28.9 GW by November 2025. This transformation underwrote Bangladesh's emergence as a garment manufacturing powerhouse and drove rapid economic growth. The fuel mix remains dominated by natural gas (~40% of capacity), followed by coal (~23%) and heavy fuel oil (~19%), with renewables contributing under 5%. Gas availability is now a binding constraint: domestic gas reserves are declining, LNG import terminals have been added to compensate, but generating electricity from gas has become increasingly expensive, pushing a structural shift toward coal and imported power.

Key Projects & Actors

  • BPDB (Bangladesh Power Development Board): Main state utility; generation + wholesale buyer; manages a mix of own plants and power purchase from IPPs and imports.
  • PGCB (Power Grid Company of Bangladesh): Transmission company; manages national grid and India interconnections.
  • Rooppur NPP: 2×1,200 MW VVER-1200 reactors; Rosatom-built; $12B project; financed 90% by Russian state loan. Unit 1 commissioning expected 2026 following completion of hot/cold functional tests in 2025. When both units operate, Rooppur will add 2,400 MW of baseload — transforming Bangladesh's generation mix and reducing gas/coal dependency.
  • Payra TPP: 1,320 MW coal (two units); Chinese-financed (CMC/CMEC); on the Bay of Bengal coast; fully operational; Bangladesh's largest coal plant.
  • Rampal TPP (Maitree): 1,320 MW coal; joint venture with India's NTPC; in Khulna division near the Sundarbans — a project that drew strong environmental opposition.
  • Matarbari: Japan-financed coal + LNG import terminal (JICA); 1,200 MW coal; deep-water port facility enabling future LNG imports.
Bangladesh's electricity expansion is a story of extraordinary success in access provision — but the fuel strategy embedded in that expansion now poses a serious long-term risk. Having committed to large coal plants (Payra, Rampal, Matarbari) and a $12B nuclear plant that locks in a Russian fuel supply relationship, Bangladesh's electricity system is increasingly dependent on imported fuel and expensive long-term contracts. Gas decline has removed the one domestic fuel buffer. Renewable energy — which Bangladesh has almost no utility-scale deployment of — is the only pathway to energy independence, yet development has barely begun.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Rooppur's commissioning in 2026 will be the most consequential single event in Bangladesh's electricity sector in decades. 2,400 MW of baseload nuclear — on a grid of ~28 GW — will reshape the dispatch order, reduce gas and HFO burning, and prove Bangladesh's nuclear operating capability. The transition from first synchronisation to stable commercial operation will require BPDB and Rosatom to navigate a technically complex process with limited domestic nuclear expertise.
2
The Adani arbitration dispute exposes a structural vulnerability in Bangladesh's import dependency on India. 1,600 MW of cross-border power from Adani's Godda plant — constituting ~6% of Bangladesh's installed capacity — is now subject to a $485M payment dispute. Bangladesh's growing inter-dependence on Indian power imports (2,000–2,500 MW total) is both an energy security benefit and a geopolitical exposure, contingent on bilateral relations that can change with government.
3
Bangladesh's utility-scale renewables deficit is a strategic gap that grows more costly each year it persists. With <5% renewables in a ~28 GW system, Bangladesh has done almost nothing to build utility-scale solar or wind despite having reasonable solar irradiance and a Bay of Bengal coastal wind resource. Every year of delay locks in more expensive coal and gas imports; the land-constrained geography makes large solar harder but not impossible (floating solar on haors/floodplains, rooftop solar on garment factories).
4
The coal expansion path (Payra + Rampal + Matarbari) creates stranded asset risk within the planning horizon of the plants themselves. Coal plants built in 2020–2025 have 25–30 year lifespans, taking them to 2045–2055. Global coal economics and climate policy are increasingly hostile to long-lived coal assets. Bangladesh may face early closure decisions on expensive Chinese/Japanese-financed coal plants before they have fully paid down construction debt — exactly the stranded asset scenario that has troubled other Asian coal-heavy systems.
5
Bangladesh's 6 million solar home systems represent the world's largest off-grid solar programme — a genuine achievement that has no parallel in utility-scale renewable policy. IDCOL's rural SHS programme electrified millions of households before grid extension reached them. This domestic institutional capacity and household familiarity with solar is an asset that utility-scale solar policy has not yet capitalised on. A national rooftop solar programme leveraging garment factory rooftops and urban buildings could add 2–5 GW at low additional system cost.
