Electricity Sector Overview

The Maldives is an archipelago of ~1,200 islands of which ~200 are inhabited, each operating its own isolated electricity system. Total installed capacity is approximately 600 MW, of which 68.5 MW (11.4%) is solar PV — the remainder being diesel generators. This extreme geographic fragmentation makes the Maldives one of the most complex electricity systems per capita in the world: not one grid but 200 mini-grids, each with its own generation, storage (increasingly), and distribution infrastructure. Diesel import dependency is near-total, making electricity costs (~$0.35–0.45/kWh) among the highest in Asia, heavily subsidised by the state through FENAKA and STELCO transfers.

The 200-Island Challenge

The Maldives' geography is its defining electricity challenge. Each inhabited island — ranging from Malé (population ~150,000) to atolls with a few hundred residents — requires a self-sufficient energy system:

  • Malé & Hulhumalé: The capital is served by STELCO with interconnected generation across the Malé metropolitan area; the most sophisticated system in the country; solar + battery hybridisation most advanced here.
  • Resort islands: Private resort islands typically operate their own diesel generation with increasing solar + storage; some achieve 80–100% renewable electricity during daylight hours. These are the testing ground for island-scale RE integration.
  • Outer atolls (FENAKA): Dispersed populations, expensive diesel logistics, highest subsidy per kWh; solar home systems and mini-grid solar + batteries are being deployed; transformative for communities receiving them but slow to scale across 157 islands simultaneously.
  • Inter-island cable projects: Selected clusters of nearby islands are being connected by submarine cables, allowing single generation systems to serve multiple islands — reducing the per-unit infrastructure cost significantly.
The Maldives faces a uniquely dual existential threat: rising sea levels threaten the physical existence of most islands (average elevation 1.5 m above sea level) while diesel-dependent electricity is one of the highest per-capita sources of CO₂ emissions among SIDS. The Maldives has been a vocal advocate for 1.5°C climate ambition globally while still generating 89% of its electricity from diesel domestically. The Energy Roadmap 2024–2033 is the country's attempt to close this gap — transforming every island's energy system from diesel to solar + storage within a decade.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
The 70% renewable by 2030 target requires adding ~350–400 MW of solar + storage across 200 islands in 4 years — an unprecedented logistics challenge. It is technically achievable: solar + battery mini-grids are now cost-competitive with diesel even at small island scale. But deploying them on 150+ outer islands simultaneously requires procurement frameworks, installation capacity, and O&M training that have historically been the bottleneck in Maldivian energy projects. Execution speed and logistics are the binding constraints, not technology or economics.
2
FENAKA's financial distress is the structural barrier to outer-island renewable deployment. As the utility serving 157 outer islands at subsidised tariffs below diesel generation cost, FENAKA runs persistent operating losses. New solar + battery assets reduce per-unit costs once installed, but the upfront capital requires grants or concessional loans — which FENAKA cannot service at cost-reflective rates. Structuring solar deployment as donor-financed capital grants with FENAKA as operator is the viable model; pure commercial financing is not.
3
Maldivian resort islands are the world's most advanced proving ground for 100% renewable island electricity systems. High-end resorts with solar panels, battery storage, and (increasingly) desalination integration have demonstrated that 80–100% renewable electricity is commercially viable on small islands. The technology stack, O&M models, and economics validated in resort islands are directly transferable to inhabited outer atolls — the main difference being willingness to pay.
4
Floating and lagoon-mounted solar is a Maldives-specific solution that could unlock significant RE capacity without land use conflict. With extremely limited land area on most islands, ground-mounted solar competes with housing and vegetation. Floating solar on shallow lagoons — which the Maldives has in abundance — avoids this conflict and can achieve large capacities close to demand centres. STELCO's tender for lagoon-mounted installations is the first step toward commercialising this opportunity at scale.
5
The Maldives is simultaneously a climate victim, a climate advocate, and a climate laggard — and resolving this contradiction is its most important energy policy challenge. As a country that will be uninhabitable with 2°C of warming, Maldives has unmatched moral authority in climate negotiations. Converting that authority into domestic action — replacing diesel with solar across all 200 islands — would make the country's advocacy credible and provide a global demonstration model for SIDS energy transitions that currently lack successful examples at scale.
