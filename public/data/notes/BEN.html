1. Energy context

Benin hosts the headquarters of the West African Power Pool (WAPP) in Cotonou, making it the symbolic centre of regional electricity integration — while paradoxically being one of the most import-dependent WAPP members, with only 42% electrification and ~56% of national consumption supplied from imports (primarily Nigeria via a 330 kV interconnection, and from Ghana via Togo and CEB). Benin's electricity access gap is one of the most severe in the region and a major focus of the government's Programme d'Action du Gouvernement (PAG).

The sector has undergone significant capacity additions in recent years: the Maria Gléta 1 (127 MW, 2016) and Maria Gléta 2 (120 MW, 2022) gas plants near Cotonou represent the most modern generation fleet in the country. Growing solar IPP activity and a commitment to the North Core interconnection mark Benin as an increasingly active investment destination for power sector infrastructure.

  • Gas (~73% of domestic generation): Maria Gléta 1 (127 MW open-cycle, China/Globeleq, 2016) and Maria Gléta 2 (120 MW, Aggreko/PHIBL, 2022) are the backbone of domestic thermal generation, located near Cotonou. Both run on natural gas supplied by pipeline from Nigeria via the West African Gas Pipeline (WAGP). Gas supply reliability from Nigeria is variable, creating dispatch uncertainties for the plants.
  • Oil/diesel (~24% of domestic generation): SBEE's own diesel and HFO units (older Sèmè, Porto-Novo, and Cotonou plants) provide peaking and backup capacity. These are increasingly expensive to operate as fuel costs rise. Emergency rental generators (Aggreko-type) are deployed during supply crises.
  • Solar (~3% of domestic generation): Several small solar systems (25–50 MW range) in development or early commissioning, including IFC-supported utility-scale solar. Benin's solar ambitions are significant: the government has sought solar IPP investment under a standardised tender process.

3. Sector organisation

SBEE — Société Béninoise d'Énergie Électrique State Utility

SBEE is the vertically-integrated state utility responsible for generation, transmission, and distribution of electricity across Benin. It manages import contracts with TCN (Nigeria) and through CEB. SBEE's financial position is structurally weak: tariffs are below cost recovery (~XOF 80–100/kWh average end-user tariff), technical and commercial losses exceed 30%, and domestic collection rates are low in peri-urban and rural areas. SBEE relies on government budget transfers and concessional loans to finance capital expenditure.

ABERME — Rural Electrification Agency Rural Off-grid

The Agence Béninoise d'Électrification Rurale et de Maîtrise d'Énergie (ABERME) manages off-grid rural electrification through mini-grid concessions and individual solar systems. Benin's rural electrification remains low (~20%): the gap between urban (70%+) and rural access is extreme, and ABERME's ambition outstrips its financial capacity without significant concessional funding.

WAPP Secretariat — Cotonou HQ Regional Institution

The West African Power Pool (WAPP) secretariat is headquartered in Cotonou, Benin. WAPP coordinates the planning and development of regional electricity interconnections among 14 ECOWAS member states, oversees the North Core and CLSG projects, and is developing the WAPP Energy Information System. The Secretariat's location in Cotonou gives Benin a privileged institutional role in regional electricity governance, though this has not translated into direct electricity supply advantages for domestic consumers.

4. Key issues and challenges

  • Nigeria gas supply reliability: Maria Gléta 1 and 2 run on Nigerian gas via WAGP. Nigerian gas supply to WAGP has been periodically disrupted — by pipeline vandalism in the Niger Delta, upstream production issues, and NNPC priority constraints. When WAGP supply fails, Maria Gléta plants are idle and Benin's domestic generation drops sharply, increasing import dependence on Nigeria's 330 kV grid or Ghana via CEB.
  • North Core 330 kV — the strategic bet: Benin is central to the North Core interconnection both geographically and institutionally. When completed, the North Core will allow Benin to position itself as a transit country, earning wheeling revenues from Nigerian power transiting to Togo and Ghana (and vice versa). The 330 kV upgrade through Benin territory is under active construction with AfDB financing.
  • Electrification access gap: Benin's 42% electrification rate masks a stark geographic disparity — Cotonou and Parakou are reasonably well served, while northern and rural areas have near-zero access. The PAG programme targets universal access by 2026, a goal that requires massive mini-grid and solar deployment that SBEE/ABERME cannot finance unilaterally without donor coordination.
  • Adjarala hydro (CEB) — a decade of delays: The 147 MW Adjarala dam on the Mono River (shared with Togo under CEB) would be transformational for both countries' domestic generation capacity. But the project has been delayed for over 10 years by social and environmental concerns (large resettlement required), financing gaps (the IDA/AfDB package has been restructured multiple times), and institutional complexity. If commissioned, it would reduce both countries' import dependence significantly.

5. Five key takeaways

1
Benin is both the institutional home of WAPP and one of its most electricity-deprived members — hosting the secretariat for regional electricity integration while 58% of its own population lacks grid access. This paradox underlines the gap between institutional ambition and execution across the WAPP zone.
2
The Maria Gléta 1 and 2 additions (247 MW combined, 2016–2022) represent the most significant domestic generation buildout Benin has achieved — tripling domestic output — yet total supply still depends 56% on imports, illustrating how quickly electricity demand can outpace supply additions in a fast-growing economy.
3
Nigeria gas supply via WAGP is the critical dependency behind Maria Gléta's dispatch: Nigerian gas pipeline vandalism, production disputes, or NNPC supply prioritisation can instantly turn Benin's newest gas plants idle, creating a hidden fragility in what looks on paper like a modern, diversified domestic fleet.
4
The North Core 330 kV interconnection's Benin section is the most strategically valuable segment: it transforms Benin from an import dependent into a potential transit country and creates the physical backbone for a genuine regional electricity market between Nigeria, Benin, Togo, and Ghana — the four most connected WAPP members by population and GDP.
5
The Adjarala hydro project's decade of delays is a cautionary tale for shared infrastructure development: even with strong institutional frameworks (CEB), political will in both countries, and donor support, the combination of social safeguard requirements, financing complexity, and institutional coordination has prevented financial close for over 10 years — a governance lesson that applies across WAPP shared projects.
