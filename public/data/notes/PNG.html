Generation Mix & Sector Overview

Papua New Guinea presents the Pacific's most extreme version of the dual-economy energy challenge: a small, underperforming national grid serving urban centres, and a massive mining/LNG enclave economy that generates its own power almost entirely from diesel, gas, and in one case geothermal — essentially invisible to PNG Power's statistics. The national grid, operated by PNG Power Ltd. (state utility), serves approximately 500,000-600,000 formal grid customers in Port Moresby, Lae, Madang, and secondary towns — approximately 13-15% of PNG's population of ~10 million. The grid is hydro-anchored: Yonki Dam (75 MW, Ramu River, Eastern Highlands, commissioned 1991) and the Ramu Hydro Scheme (75 MW, Ramu River, Madang Province) together provide most of Port Moresby's and Lae's baseload. Diesel backup generation is pervasive — Port Moresby's Herd Road diesel plant (~120 MW) is essential during drought, maintenance, or grid faults. The defining irony of PNG's energy situation: the country sits atop enormous gas reserves (PNG LNG exports 6.9 mtpa of LNG, primarily to Japan, South Korea, and China) yet nearly all this gas is exported with almost none used for domestic electricity — Port Moresby's households and businesses experience daily power outages while tankers of liquefied natural gas depart the coast.

PNG LNG: the great energy export paradoxPNG LNG (ExxonMobil 33.2% operator, Oil Search/Santos 29%, Nippon Oil 4.7%, PNG government via Kumul 16.8%) has operated since 2014 from gas fields in the Southern and Hela Highlands (Hides, Angore, Juha). The project exports approximately 6.9 mtpa of LNG from Caution Bay terminal (near Port Moresby) — worth approximately US$6-8B/year in export revenue. Domestic gas use for electricity: near-zero. The pipeline from Hides to Caution Bay (700 km) passes within 50 km of Port Moresby, yet no gas-to-power plant has been developed. Reasons: (1) ExxonMobil and the PNG government have had contractual disagreements about domestic supply obligations and pricing; (2) PNG Power lacks the technical capacity to operate a gas-fired plant; (3) the regulatory framework for domestic gas supply pricing was unresolved for years. Papua LNG (TotalEnergies, FID 2024, Elk-Antelope fields, Gulf Province) includes a domestic gas supply obligation negotiated with the PNG government — the first LNG project to do so explicitly. A planned 150 MW gas-fired plant at Port Moresby (Motukea Island) using Papua LNG associated gas would transform the capital's electricity supply from chronically inadequate diesel/hydro to reliable gas-fired generation — if project delivery timelines are met.
Mining enclaves: captive power outside PNG PowerPNG's extractive industry operates outside the national grid — each mine and LNG facility is a self-contained energy island: Ok Tedi copper mine (Western Province): ~100 MW captive diesel. Porgera gold mine (Enga Province, suspended 2020-2023, reopening): captive diesel + small hydro. Lihir gold mine (Lihir Island, New Ireland Province): ~56 MW geothermal (volcanic island, New Ireland arc) — the Pacific's only operating mine-scale geothermal, operated by Newcrest/Newmont. Hidden Valley/Hamata (Morobe Province): captive diesel. Ramu Nickel (Madang): captive diesel. These mining operations collectively consume perhaps 300-400 MW of electricity — comparable to PNG Power's entire national grid — but entirely separate from it and generating zero spillover benefit to surrounding communities. The geographic isolation of mines and communities means grid extension is rarely economic; solar SHS and mini-grids are the access solution for communities adjacent to mines.
Port Moresby: a capital city with chronic blackoutsPort Moresby (~500,000 people, growing at ~4%/year) experiences daily load shedding of 4-8 hours in a typical year — worsening to 8-16 hours in drought years when Yonki reservoir is low. The Herd Road diesel plant (~120 MW) is aging (1960s-80s units, repeatedly overhauled), expensive (HFO import cost), and unreliable (frequent forced outages). PNG Power's revenue collection in Port Moresby is poor (~60-70% billing rate) — limiting its capital expenditure capacity. Private generators are near-universal for businesses and hospitals; residential generators are common among middle-income households. The paradox is acute: Papua LNG's Caution Bay terminal (5 km from Port Moresby CBD) ships LNG to Tokyo while the capital's businesses run diesel generators. The Motukea Island gas-fired plant (planned 2025-2028, Papua LNG domestic supply) would directly address this if delivered on schedule.
Rural access: 85% off-grid, SHS the primary solutionPNG's ~8.5 million rural residents (85%+ of population) live in thousands of villages across one of the world's most rugged and inaccessible terrains — the Highlands, Sepik River basin, island provinces, and coastal villages. Grid extension to most of these communities is economically infeasible for decades. The primary access technology: solar home systems (SHS) and small solar mini-grids (0.5-5 kW). World Bank "PNG Electrification Partnership" (2020-2028, US$150M): targeting 70,000 new connections (predominantly SHS and mini-grids) across rural provinces. ADB "Pacific Private Sector Development Initiative" supports small business SHS leasing. Government "Rural Electrification Authority" (REA): tasked with rural programme delivery but capacity is thin. The 70% RE target by 2030 and 100% access by 2030 are both highly aspirational — the current trajectory suggests ~30-35% access by 2030.
Ramu 2 and new hydro: the investment pipelineRamu 2 Hydro (120 MW, upper Ramu River, Madang Province): proposed for decades; multiple feasibility studies; ADB financing under discussion 2023-2024; cost estimate ~US$600M; construction timeline 5-7 years from FID. If developed, would transform Lae's electricity supply (currently diesel-heavy despite proximity to Ramu 1 hydro). Purari River hydro (1,000+ MW theoretical potential, Gulf Province): assessed by PNG Power, JICA, and WB; extraordinarily high potential but remote location (200+ km from any grid), challenging jungle terrain, and significant environmental/social complexity make near-term development unlikely. The Ramu 2 project is more credible than Purari in any 2030-horizon planning scenario.

The Domestic Gas Question: PNG's Defining Energy Policy Choice

PNG's most consequential near-term energy decision is not about RE deployment — it is about whether and how to redirect a fraction of its enormous gas export volumes to domestic electricity generation:

  • The opportunity: Papua LNG's domestic gas supply obligation (negotiated with the PNG government as part of the project's land access agreement) would provide approximately 50-80 MMscf/day of gas for domestic use — enough to fuel a 200-300 MW gas-fired power station. This would give Port Moresby reliable 24/7 electricity for the first time in a generation.
  • The challenge: PNG Power lacks the institutional capacity to procure, contract, and manage a 150-300 MW gas-fired plant. The project requires a financially capable developer (likely an international IPP) and a gas supply agreement whose pricing is commercially viable for both the LNG project and the power plant. The regulatory framework for domestic gas pricing in PNG has been disputed for years.
  • The trade-off: Gas-to-power is not RE. Using PNG LNG gas domestically rather than exporting it displaces diesel (a climate improvement) but locks in gas dependency and does not count toward PNG's 70% RE target. The WB and ADB are generally cautious about financing fossil gas power plants in SIDS contexts; a PNG gas-to-power plant would likely require non-MDB commercial financing.
PNG is the Pacific's most complex energy policy challenge — and arguably the world's most extreme example of the resource curse's energy dimension: a country exporting billions of dollars of liquefied natural gas while its capital city experiences daily blackouts and 85% of its population lacks electricity access. The path forward requires simultaneously: gas-to-power for Port Moresby in the near term (reliable electricity, not necessarily RE); massive solar SHS roll-out for rural access; and longer-term hydro expansion for Ramu 2. None of these can wait for the others; all require institutional capacity that PNG's government and utility currently lack at the necessary scale.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
PNG's defining energy policy failure is not a lack of RE resources or financing — the country has exceptional hydro, solar, and geothermal potential, and ADB/WB financing is available — but a lack of institutional capacity to plan, procure, and deliver energy projects at the required scale, manifesting as a decades-long queue of feasibility studies for projects (Ramu 2, Purari, Motukea gas plant) that are never built. The project delivery bottleneck — not resource scarcity or finance — is PNG's primary energy constraint. Addressing it requires sustained investment in PNG Power's engineering capacity, PNG government's procurement capability, and the regulatory framework's clarity — investments that are less photogenic than dams and solar farms but more consequential for outcomes. MDB technical assistance to PNG Power on project management and procurement is as important as MDB financing of the projects themselves.
2
The mining enclave captive power model — where Ok Tedi, Porgera, Lihir, and Ramu Nickel collectively consume 300-400 MW of electricity entirely separate from PNG Power — represents a missed opportunity for PNG's national electrification: each mine is surrounded by communities with near-zero grid access, yet the mine's generation infrastructure benefits none of them, because the regulatory framework has never required mines to share grid infrastructure with host communities. A community power sharing requirement — mandating that mines with surplus generation capacity (common during periods of reduced production) interconnect with local villages and sell surplus electricity at regulated rates — would be the highest-impact near-term rural electrification measure available to PNG without requiring any new generation investment. Lihir's geothermal (operating at ~40 MW load, 56 MW capacity) has significant surplus that could power all of Lihir Island's non-mining residents. The regulatory change needed is a community power sharing obligation in the Mining Act — a legal instrument, not a capital investment.
3
Developing gas-to-power for Port Moresby using Papua LNG associated gas is the correct near-term energy policy choice for PNG despite its non-RE character — because reliable electricity in the capital is a prerequisite for economic development that enables future RE investment, and PNG's 70% RE target (achievable through hydro expansion and solar rural access) does not require the capital city to remain on unreliable diesel while waiting for large hydro projects that take 7-10 years to build. The sequential logic: gas → reliable electricity → economic growth → fiscal capacity for Ramu 2 hydro → long-term RE transition. This is not a retreat from RE ambition; it is a recognition that energy poverty relief and RE transition are not the same timeline. Port Moresby's businesses, hospitals, and schools cannot wait for Ramu 2. Gas-to-power is the bridge that enables the long-term RE future to be financed from a stronger economic base.
4
PNG's solar home system rollout — targeting 70,000 new connections in 8 years under the World Bank Electrification Partnership — is the correct primary tool for rural electrification given PNG's terrain and settlement pattern, but the programme's sustainability depends on establishing local private sector SHS retailers, maintenance networks, and pay-as-you-go financing that can continue after the World Bank project closes, which none of PNG's previous donor-funded SHS programmes have successfully achieved. Previous SHS programmes in PNG (JICA, AusAID, European Commission) provided SHS free or at subsidised upfront cost; when donor funding ended and systems needed replacement batteries or inverters, there was no local market to supply them and systems degraded rapidly. The WB Electrification Partnership is trying to develop a private sector SHS retail market (using results-based financing to incentivise private SHS retailers rather than direct subsidy); its success in establishing durable private sector supply chains is the key programme evaluation metric.
5
Lihir Island's mine-scale geothermal plant (Newmont, ~56 MW) is the Pacific's only operating proof-of-concept for commercial geothermal at island scale — demonstrating that volcanic arc geothermal resources in PNG's island arc can be developed commercially — and should be the reference site for feasibility assessments of community-scale geothermal in PNG's island provinces (New Britain, New Ireland, Bougainville) where volcanic resources exist but have not been explored. Lihir geothermal's development (driven by Newcrest/Newmont's need to avoid costly diesel fuel on a remote island) demonstrates that commercial necessity is a more reliable driver of geothermal development than government planning alone. The technology (flash steam and binary cycle) is proven at Lihir; the geological model (volcanic arc, New Ireland arc specifically) is replicable at Rabaul (East New Britain), Wewak (volcanic proximity), and Bougainville (active volcanoes). A PNG national geothermal survey — funded as a single ESMAP exploration programme across all volcanic island provinces — would map the resource base needed to identify commercial development candidates.
