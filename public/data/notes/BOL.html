Generation Mix & Capacity

Bolivia's electricity has been cheap because its gas has been cheap. Roughly two thirds of generation comes from thermal plants burning domestic natural gas sold to the power sector at regulated prices well below export parity, with hydropower supplying most of the rest. The result is among the lowest generation costs in South America and a tariff structure that depends on that input price holding.

It is not holding. Gas production has been declining for a decade as the large fields discovered in the 1990s deplete without replacement, export volumes to Brazil and Argentina have fallen sharply, and Bolivia has moved from being a regional gas supplier toward importing fuels. The fiscal and energy consequences reach the power sector directly.

The state's response has been a large hydro and transmission programme, including combined-cycle conversions of the existing gas turbines and projects such as Misicuni and San José, explicitly framed around becoming an energy exporter to Brazil and Argentina. Little of that export ambition has materialised, and the country now has more capacity than domestic demand requires.

Market Structure & Institutions

  • ENDE: Empresa Nacional de Electricidad was renationalised from 2010 and is now a vertically integrated state group covering most generation, transmission and much distribution. Bolivia reversed the 1990s unbundling more completely than any of its neighbours.
  • CNDC: The Comité Nacional de Despacho de Carga dispatches the national interconnected system (SIN) and settles the wholesale market that remains.
  • AETN: The Autoridad de Fiscalización y Control Social de Electricidad y Tecnología Nuclear regulates tariffs and supervises the sector.
  • Subsidised gas to power: The transfer price of gas to generators is administratively set far below export parity. It is the main reason Bolivian electricity is cheap and the main reason the sector's economics are not transferable to a future where gas is imported.
  • Isolated systems: Parts of Pando, Beni and rural Santa Cruz remain outside the SIN, served by diesel; extending the interconnected system to these areas has been a continuing public investment priority.
  • Interconnections: Physical links to Brazil and Argentina are limited and operate marginally; the planned export corridors have not converted into commercial trade.
Bolivia built a power system for an export market that never opened, funded by gas rents that are now disappearing. It has surplus capacity, near universal access and some of the lowest tariffs in the region, all resting on an input price that cannot survive the transition from gas exporter to gas importer. That adjustment, rather than any generation project, is the sector's defining issue.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
The subsidised gas price is the hidden foundation of the whole tariff structure. Every comparison showing Bolivian electricity as cheap is really a comparison of fuel transfer prices. As domestic gas gives way to imports at market prices, either tariffs rise sharply or the fiscal transfer grows, and neither is politically easy.
2
Surplus capacity without export contracts is a stranded asset. Capacity was added on the premise of selling to Brazil and Argentina. Without firm offtake, the country is paying capital costs for plants that run at low utilisation while domestic demand grows slowly.
3
Gas decline is an energy security problem, not only a fiscal one. A system two thirds dependent on domestic gas becomes import-dependent for its core fuel. Bolivia is landlocked, so LNG is not a straightforward substitute, and pipeline imports would come from the same neighbours it hoped to sell to.
4
Renewables are the cheapest available hedge and are barely deployed. Bolivia has excellent highland solar resource and under 200 MW of solar. Building it would directly displace gas the country can no longer afford to burn, which makes the case unusually clear-cut.
5
Lithium industrialisation would change the demand picture entirely. Processing at scale is electricity-intensive and would absorb the existing surplus. It has been announced repeatedly and delivered marginally, so it should be treated as an option rather than a plan.
