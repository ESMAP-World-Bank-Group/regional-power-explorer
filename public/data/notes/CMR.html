Generation Mix & Capacity

Cameroon has the largest and most developed electricity system in Central Africa, anchored by a cascade of hydropower plants on the Sanaga River. In 2024, the commissioning of the Nachtigal hydroelectric plant (420 MW) — a public-private partnership with EDF and IFC as co-investors — increased national installed capacity by approximately 30%, marking the country's most significant generation addition in decades. ENEO, the private utility operating the main grid concession, previously held ~968 MW; post-Nachtigal, national capacity approaches ~1,400 MW across all operators.

Market Structure & Regulation

Cameroon's electricity sector is organised around a concession model: the state owns assets via AES Sonel (now ENEO's predecessor structure) but operations were privatised. The sector features multiple state entities alongside the private concession holder.

  • ENEO (Energy of Cameroon): Private concessionaire (~51% Actis Energy, ~44% state, ~5% other); operates the Southern Interconnected Grid (SIG) — the main grid serving Douala, Yaoundé, and the south. Holds 968 MW of own capacity excluding Nachtigal.
  • EDC (Electricity Development Corporation): State entity owning and developing new generation assets (including Lom Pangar dam and the Nachtigal stake); passes infrastructure to ENEO or PPP vehicles for operation.
  • ARSEL (Agency for Electricity Sector Regulation): Regulator; sets tariffs and monitors the concession.
  • SONATREL: State-owned transmission company (unbundled from ENEO in 2015); manages the high-voltage grid and is responsible for expanding the transmission backbone.
  • Northern Grid: Largely separate system serving the north; managed through ENEO but structurally isolated; relies on diesel/HFO with growing solar injection.
The Nachtigal commissioning in 2024 is the most consequential event in Cameroon's electricity sector in decades. Adding 420 MW in a single project — nearly 30% of prior capacity — relieves the chronic power shortages that have constrained Cameroon's industrial base, including the Alucam aluminium smelter (one of the largest single electricity consumers in sub-Saharan Africa).

The Two-Grid Challenge

Cameroon operates two structurally separate electricity grids that reflect the country's geography and development disparities:

  • Southern Interconnected Grid (SIG): Covers Douala, Yaoundé, and the southern/coastal regions; anchored by the Sanaga hydro cascade; supplies ~85% of national consumption; benefits from Nachtigal addition.
  • Northern Interconnected Grid (NIG): Covers the Far North, North, and Adamawa regions; relies almost entirely on diesel and heavy fuel oil; high generation costs (~$0.40–0.50/kWh); growing solar additions at Maroua and Guider are beginning to change the economics of this grid.

There is no transmission link connecting the two grids. The 1,800+ km distance and challenging terrain make interconnection extremely expensive. The northern grid's isolation means energy poverty and high costs for some of the country's most vulnerable populations.

Untapped Hydro Potential

Cameroon is often called the "water tower of Africa." Its rivers — the Sanaga, Nyong, Wouri, Benue, and others — represent one of the continent's largest undeveloped hydropower resources, estimated at 12,000–23,000 MW of technically exploitable potential, of which less than 10% has been developed. Key projects in the pipeline include:

  • Grand Eweng HPP: ~600 MW; Sanaga River; downstream of Nachtigal; advanced feasibility.
  • Song Mbengue: ~950 MW; lower Sanaga; largest single hydro project identified.
  • Mentchum HPP: ~200 MW; northwest Cameroon; important for northwest electrification.
  • Regional export potential: Cameroon's surplus hydro could, in theory, power interconnections to the Congo Basin and CAPP pool — making it the region's potential hydro hub if transmission infrastructure is built.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Nachtigal (2024) fundamentally changes Cameroon's power balance. The 420 MW addition is the biggest single capacity jump in the country's history. It ends years of rationing for industrial customers, revitalises the Alucam aluminium concession, and creates the surplus capacity needed to attract new industrial investment.
2
Cameroon has the largest untapped hydro potential in Central Africa. With 12–23 GW technically exploitable and less than 10% developed, the country could supply power to the entire CAPP pool and beyond. The barrier is not resource availability — it is transmission infrastructure, project finance, and political will to develop cross-border grids.
3
The northern grid remains a persistent equity and cost crisis. North Cameroon relies on diesel generation at $0.40–0.50/kWh — 5–8× the cost of Sanaga hydro. The 2024 Maroua/Guider solar doubling is a meaningful step but addresses only a fraction of northern demand. Northern electrification requires sustained investment in solar mini-grids and eventual grid extension.
4
ENEO's concession model is a template for the region — but it has limits. Privatising operations while the state retains asset ownership has improved operational performance, but ENEO faces chronic tension between politically suppressed retail tariffs and the need for investment-grade returns. Tariff reform is the sector's most sensitive and most deferred governance challenge.
5
Alucam's energy demand is a strategic anchor for Cameroonian hydro policy. The aluminium smelter is one of the largest industrial electricity consumers in sub-Saharan Africa; its power purchase agreement with ENEO/EDC historically underpinned the economics of major hydro projects. Nachtigal was partly sized around Alucam's long-term demand — making the smelter's future a key variable in whether subsequent hydro projects proceed.
