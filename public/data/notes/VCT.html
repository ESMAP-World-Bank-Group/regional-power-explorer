Generation Mix & Sector Overview

St. Vincent and the Grenadines (SVG) consists of the main volcanic island of St. Vincent (~90,000 people) and a chain of smaller Grenadine islands extending southward toward Grenada (including Bequia, Mustique, Canouan, Mayreau, Union Island, and the Tobago Cays Marine Park). Electricity is provided by VINLEC (St. Vincent Electricity Services Ltd.), a fully state-owned utility, with the notable exception of Mustique Island — which is served by the Mustique Company's own private diesel generation as part of the island's exclusive private ownership arrangement. The main island system (~105 MW installed) runs approximately 80% on diesel and HFO from the Lowmans Bay Power Station, with the Rabacca River hydroelectric scheme (Cumberland and Peniston plants, ~11 MW combined) providing seasonal baseload. Solar (~6 MW utility + ~3 MW rooftop) and wind (~2 MW at Ribishi Point) add ~8% combined. The system is defined by two intersecting dynamics: the energy transition ambition (60% RE by 2030, one of the OECS' highest targets) and the recent volcanic disruption — La Soufrière eruption (April 2021) forced evacuation of ~20,000 people from northern St. Vincent and deposited volcanic ash across the island, coating solar panels and reducing PV output for months while the ash was cleared.

La Soufrière 2021: volcanic eruption as energy infrastructure eventLa Soufrière (1,234 m, northeastern St. Vincent) began explosive eruptions on 9 April 2021 — the first major eruption since 1979. The eruption forced evacuation of ~20,000 people from the Red Zone (northern St. Vincent); ash fall covered the entire island. Energy infrastructure impact: (1) VINLEC solar installations island-wide covered in volcanic ash — PV output fell to near-zero for 2-4 weeks as panels required cleaning; (2) road infrastructure damage complicated diesel fuel delivery; (3) Rabacca hydro catchment received ash fall — turbine siltation risk requiring additional maintenance. The ash was cleaned from solar panels within 4-8 weeks; no permanent PV damage occurred. The eruption demonstrated an underappreciated vulnerability of solar PV in volcanic island contexts: ash fall can temporarily eliminate all solar output, requiring backup diesel capacity even in high-RE-penetration scenarios. VINLEC now includes volcanic ash contingency in its system operations planning — maintaining diesel reserve capacity equivalent to near-100% of peak demand.
VINLEC: fully state-owned, limited commercial flexibilityVINLEC (St. Vincent Electricity Services Ltd.) is 100% government-owned, lacking the listed commercial structure of LUCELEC (St. Lucia) or the private parent expertise of DOMLEC (Fortis). Financial performance has been constrained by: (1) government tariff caps that keep residential rates below full cost recovery during HFO price spikes; (2) limited access to commercial borrowing at LUCELEC-equivalent rates; (3) volcanic reconstruction needs competing with RE capital budget. VINLEC's tariff: ~US$0.31/kWh residential (subsidised during 2022 HFO price surge). The government has managed VINLEC's capital investments primarily through IDB and CDB concessional loans, supplemented by GEF grants for RE projects. VINLEC's technical capacity is reasonable by OECS standards — the utility maintains its own generation and distribution workforce — but the RE procurement and project finance expertise needed for 60% RE by 2030 is thin.
The Grenadines: fragmented outer island energy supplyThe Grenadine islands are SVG's most complex energy supply challenge: approximately 25 inhabited islands and cays, each with its own diesel mini-grid or household diesel/solar supply. Main Grenadines energy profile: Bequia (~5,000 people, VINLEC diesel mini-grid ~2 MW, IDB solar hybrid pilot 2022 — 400 kW solar + 500 kWh BESS); Canouan (~1,000 people, IDB solar + VINLEC diesel); Union Island (~3,000 people, VINLEC diesel + small solar); Mustique (~500 resident staff, Mustique Company private diesel grid, 100% privately managed, no VINLEC involvement). The Mustique exception: The Mustique Company (a private company owning most of Mustique, with luxury villas for high-net-worth owners including UK royals) operates its own generation, water supply, and telecommunications outside VINLEC's jurisdiction. The company has installed ~500 kW rooftop solar on resort facilities but maintains diesel backup. The Mustique private energy governance model — entirely independent of the national utility — reflects the island's unique constitutional status.
Soufrière geothermal: early-stage explorationLa Soufrière's geothermal resource is less confirmed than Dominica's Wotten Waven but has attracted exploratory attention. IDA-funded preliminary assessment (2016): identified potential resource at Fancy/Soufrière Estates area (northwest St. Vincent, near the volcano); estimated resource unclear (preliminary work suggests 20-40 MW possible). The April 2021 eruption created complications for geothermal exploration — the new eruptive activity indicated an active magmatic system, which has energy potential but also requires careful geological mapping to distinguish high-temperature zones from active intrusive zones (where drilling is unsafe). Post-eruption geological survey (2022-2023) has provided updated subsurface data. A Phase 2 geothermal exploration programme (IDA/ESMAP funded, ~US$5M) is under discussion for 2025-2026. Timeline to commercial geothermal from St. Vincent: realistically 2035+, if resource is confirmed.
Wind and hydro: SVG's non-solar RE assetsRabacca hydro (Cumberland 8 MW + Peniston 3 MW): run-of-river on the Rabacca River system (also called the Cumberland River); seasonal variation — higher output in wet season (June-November); La Soufrière ash fall reduced effective operation for 2-3 months post-April 2021. Additional hydro sites: the Colonaire River and Yambou River systems have been assessed for additional 5-10 MW run-of-river capacity (IDB study, 2019) — pending environmental assessment and community consultation. Wind: Ribishi Point (southeastern St. Vincent) has good trade wind exposure (~7 m/s average); 2 MW installed; 10 MW additional wind planned for the Ribishi-Richland Park corridor under VINLEC's 60% RE programme. Wind is seasonal (trade winds December-June stronger than July-November) — complementary to hydro's wet season output profile.

Energy and Volcano: Planning for the Active Geological Context

St. Vincent is the only Eastern Caribbean island with an historically active volcano (La Soufrière has erupted in 1718, 1812, 1902, 1979, and 2021) where electricity planning must explicitly incorporate volcanic risk:

  • Ash fall and solar PV: The 2021 eruption demonstrated that volcanic ash can eliminate solar output for weeks. Any RE planning for SVG above ~30% solar penetration must account for a scenario where solar generation falls to near-zero for 2-8 weeks during volcanic events — requiring either battery storage sufficient to bridge this gap (technically enormous, economically prohibitive) or maintained diesel backup equivalent to peak demand (contradicting the RE target rationale). The practical solution: VINLEC maintains diesel backup capacity regardless of RE penetration level, accepting that volcanic risk makes 100% RE without backup inappropriate for St. Vincent specifically.
  • Northern evacuation zone and infrastructure: The Red Zone (northern St. Vincent, where ~20,000 people live during non-eruption periods) contains no major VINLEC generation infrastructure — the Lowmans Bay plant is in the central-south. But distribution lines, substations, and customer connections in the north are vulnerable to eruption damage. VINLEC's post-2021 infrastructure rebuild has incorporated higher pole heights and underground cabling in select northern areas to reduce eruption damage risk.
  • Geothermal in an active volcanic field: The paradox of SVG's energy geography is that the volcanic activity that creates the geothermal resource also complicates its development — active magmatic systems require more careful site selection and drilling risk management than dormant volcanic fields. Post-2021 eruption data has both enhanced understanding of the subsurface system and added caution about drilling in the most active zones.
St. Vincent and the Grenadines is unique in the OECS for combining the full range of Eastern Caribbean RE resources (hydro, wind, solar, and potential geothermal) with the most active volcanic risk in the region. This combination makes SVG the most technically complex OECS energy planning challenge: the 60% RE target must be achieved while maintaining diesel backup capacity sufficient to cover volcanic ash events, the Grenadines must be served as individually optimised diesel-solar hybrids, and geothermal development must navigate post-eruption geological uncertainty. VINLEC's institutional capacity for this complexity is the principal limiting factor.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
La Soufrière's April 2021 eruption revealed that solar PV — normally considered a resilient, low-maintenance RE technology — has a specific vulnerability in volcanic island contexts (ash fall eliminating output for weeks) that must be explicitly incorporated into SVG's grid planning as a non-negotiable constraint: any RE scenario above ~35% solar penetration requires either multi-week battery storage (prohibitively expensive) or maintained diesel backup capacity equivalent to near-peak demand. This volcanic-ash solar vulnerability is unique to St. Vincent among the OECS — no other English-speaking Eastern Caribbean island has a historically active erupting volcano. It means that VINLEC's grid architecture must retain diesel backup capacity even as RE penetration increases, which constrains the cost savings from the RE transition (diesel plants must be maintained even if not regularly used) and should be factored into VINLEC's RE economic analysis as an ongoing insurance cost.
2
The Grenadines' fragmented outer island energy supply — 20+ inhabited islands each with its own diesel mini-grid or household energy system — represents the OECS' most complex archipelago energy challenge after the Bahamas, and the Bequia and Canouan solar hybrid pilots (IDB-financed) are demonstrating that solar + battery is commercially and technically superior to diesel-only mini-grids even at very small scales (500 kW - 2 MW), with 40-60% diesel displacement achievable at island-scale project costs below US$3M. The Grenadines solar hybridisation programme should be completed across all inhabited islands (Union, Mayreau, Palm Island, Petit St. Vincent, and others) before VINLEC invests in additional large-scale generation on the main island — the per-household energy cost reduction from outer island solar hybridisation is higher than from equivalent investment in main island RE, and the reliability improvement for outer island communities with currently unreliable diesel service is dramatic.
3
VINLEC's fully state-owned structure — without the commercial discipline, dividend pressure, or private management expertise of LUCELEC or DOMLEC — requires compensating governance mechanisms (independent regulatory body with tariff authority, external management audit, project finance committee with independent members) to achieve RE procurement outcomes comparable to its mixed-ownership OECS peers, and the government should resist the temptation to substitute political objectives for commercial governance in VINLEC's RE programme design. VINLEC has historically been more susceptible than LUCELEC to politically motivated tariff interference (government-mandated tariff caps during HFO price spikes that prevent cost recovery) and procurement delays (RE tenders that are cancelled or modified late-stage for non-technical reasons). These governance vulnerabilities slow the RE transition more than technology or finance constraints. A Regulatory Authority for SVG (currently under discussion) with genuine tariff-setting independence would be the most impactful governance improvement for VINLEC's RE investment programme.
4
SVG's Rabacca River hydro cascade (11 MW) and the additional Colonaire/Yambou hydro potential (5-10 MW identified) represent the lowest-cost, most reliable near-zero-marginal-cost RE additions available to VINLEC in the near term — more valuable per MW than additional solar given the volcanic ash risk — and should be prioritised for development before further utility-scale solar additions that face higher ash vulnerability. Run-of-river hydro is not affected by volcanic ash (catchment receives ash but turbines are in enclosed plants that can be cleaned; output resumes within days of ash fall, unlike solar panels which require weeks of cleaning). The Colonaire additional hydro (5 MW estimated) at an estimated cost of ~US$8-10M would provide year-round baseload generation unaffected by volcanic events — the most resilient RE addition available to VINLEC given SVG's specific geological context. VINLEC's current procurement programme prioritises solar and wind over additional hydro; this sequencing should be revisited in light of the 2021 eruption evidence.
5
Mustique's energy autonomy — the Mustique Company operating its own private diesel and rooftop solar generation entirely outside VINLEC's jurisdiction — represents both a precedent and an opportunity: a precedent that private island energy management can coexist with the national utility framework, and an opportunity for a negotiated Mustique-VINLEC solar PPA arrangement that would allow VINLEC to offtake Mustique's surplus rooftop solar and provide backup grid connection, reducing Mustique's diesel cost while adding renewable generation to SVG's national statistics. Mustique's ~500 kW of rooftop solar generates surplus during mid-day periods when luxury villa air conditioning demand peaks; the Mustique Company currently curtails this surplus (no grid export mechanism exists). A VINLEC-Mustique interconnection (submarine cable ~3 km from Mustique to Bequia) would allow this surplus to be absorbed by Bequia's mini-grid, reducing Bequia diesel consumption. The cable cost (~US$5M) is modest relative to the energy system benefit; the barrier is jurisdictional (Mustique operates under its own land and utility governance framework that requires a specific bilateral agreement with VINLEC, not standard regulatory treatment).
