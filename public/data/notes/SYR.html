From 8,500 MW to 1,600 MW: War's Toll

Syria's electricity sector has suffered the most severe war-related damage of any country in this region. Pre-conflict installed capacity of 8,500 MW has been reduced to an estimated ~3,500 MW of nominally surviving infrastructure — and the actual operational output is only about 1,600 MW, supplying urban residents 2–4 hours of electricity per day. Major plants destroyed include Mahardah thermal station, the Aleppo gas plant, and Zayzoun — all representing hundreds of MW lost to bombardment and neglect over 14 years of conflict. Assad's fall in December 2024 opened a new chapter: international reconstruction finance is arriving, and the new government is engaging with Türkiye, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and Western institutions for the first time in over a decade.

Post-Assad Reconstruction Momentum

Since Assad's fall in December 2024, Syria's electricity sector has received more international attention in six months than in the prior six years of siege and sanctions:

  • The Qatar-led $7B energy package (May 2025) — combining 4,000 MW of gas generation with 1,000 MW solar — represents the largest single international energy commitment to Syria. Qatar has geopolitical motivations (influence in post-Assad governance) alongside the humanitarian rationale.
  • SOCAR's gas supply via Türkiye (August 2025) brings Azerbaijani gas to Aleppo and Homs power plants — using the existing Trans-Anatolian Pipeline infrastructure. This ends the Syrian power sector's complete isolation from international energy markets.
  • Turkish power ships (announced January 2025) offer the fastest near-term supply addition. Power barges can be operational within weeks of arrival; Syria has experience with Turkish energy partnerships during the conflict period.
  • US and EU sanctions waivers for humanitarian reconstruction have been partially loosened, enabling more conventional financing to flow to Syria's recovery — including for energy infrastructure.
Six months after Assad's fall, Syria's public power output remains stuck at 1,600 MW — enough for 2–4 hours daily, unchanged from the late Assad period. The gap between announced reconstruction commitments ($7B Qatar package, Turkish ships, SOCAR gas, World Bank grant) and actual delivered electricity remains enormous. Syria's electricity recovery faces the same challenges as every post-conflict reconstruction: damaged transmission infrastructure that cannot carry additional generation, institutional capacity that has been hollowed out by 14 years of conflict and brain drain, and the geopolitical complexities of multiple foreign actors competing to shape Syria's energy future.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Syria's electricity reconstruction requires sequencing: generation without transmission is useless. Building 4,000 MW of new gas plants (Qatar's plan) will not help Aleppo if the 400 kV transmission lines from Damascus are severed or unable to carry the load. The World Bank's SEEP grant specifically targets transmission and substations — the essential precondition for any generation investment to reach consumers. Transmission must run ahead of generation in the reconstruction sequence.
2
Power barges are Syria's fastest path to additional supply — and Türkiye's most effective geopolitical instrument. Turkish floating power plants (like those deployed to Lebanon's Zouk station) can reach Syrian ports and generate within weeks. This timeline is incomparably faster than any new plant construction. Türkiye has used power barge diplomacy across Africa and the Middle East; Syria's post-Assad government, strongly aligned with Ankara, is the natural next deployment.
3
Syria's solar potential is extraordinary and almost entirely untapped — and can be deployed by local installers without heavy state involvement. GHI of 5.5+ kWh/m²/day across most of Syria, combined with hundreds of thousands of damaged rooftops needing reconstruction anyway, creates an opportunity for distributed solar deployment without grid dependency. Lebanese-style rooftop solar — privately financed, neighbourhood-scale — is already spreading in parts of Aleppo and Damascus. A donor programme supporting solar + storage for critical facilities (hospitals, water pumps, schools) would deliver more reliable electricity to more people faster than any centralized generation investment.
4
Syria's new government must manage competing geopolitical sponsors of its electricity reconstruction — each with different interests. Qatar brings $7B but wants political influence in shaping governance. Türkiye brings technical partnerships but wants cross-border Kurdish policy alignment. Azerbaijan brings gas. Saudi Arabia and UAE bring potential Gulf capital. The new Syrian leadership's ability to receive reconstruction support from multiple competing sponsors without becoming captive to any single patron will define both the pace of recovery and Syria's long-term sovereignty.
5
Syria's pre-war electricity system was better than many post-conflict countries — the institutional knowledge to rebuild it exists in Syria's diaspora. Before 2011, Syria had functional utility management, engineering expertise, and a reasonably well-maintained grid. Thousands of Syrian engineers, technicians, and energy professionals now live in Türkiye, Lebanon, Europe, and the Gulf. A diaspora-return programme specifically for energy sector professionals would accelerate reconstruction more than any financial package — because the bottleneck is human capacity, not capital alone.
