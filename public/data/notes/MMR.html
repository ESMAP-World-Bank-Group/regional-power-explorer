Fragile and conflict-affected state (FCAS) — active civil war since February 2021 coup. Myanmar has been under military authorities (SAC) control since the coup against the elected government in February 2021. A multi-front civil war is ongoing, with multiple armed groups contesting territory. The electricity sector has been severely disrupted: infrastructure is deliberately targeted, bill collection has collapsed (<3% of billing revenue collected in opposition-controlled areas), international energy companies have exited, and large parts of the country face daily power cuts of 16–20 hours. Data reliability is very low — all figures in this note carry significant uncertainty. Standard economic and financial analysis does not apply in a wartime context.

1. Energy context

Myanmar entered the 2021 military coup with an electricity sector that was already deeply under-resourced relative to its population: even at the pre-coup peak, ~27% of the population lacked any grid access, rural electrification was shallow (many villages had a grid connection but erratic supply), and tariffs were heavily subsidised at levels that made the sector financially unsustainable. The sector depended on a combination of hydropower (concentrated in Kayah and Shan states, ethnically contested areas), natural gas (onshore and offshore, operated under pre-coup PSCs with TotalEnergies, Chevron, PTTEP), and limited coal generation.

The February 2021 coup triggered an immediate collapse of the sector's already precarious operating framework. The Civil Disobedience Movement (CDM) led to mass walkouts by utility staff. Hydropower plant operators abandoned stations. Gas field workers walked off. The National Unity Government (NUG) declared a revenue-denial campaign — citizens and businesses in large cities have refused to pay electricity bills. As a result, by 2025, the Myanmar Electricity Enterprise (MOEE) and its successor entities are functionally bankrupt in large parts of the country, with <3% bill collection and 89+ high-voltage transmission pylons deliberately destroyed as acts of sabotage.

This briefing note describes Myanmar's electricity sector as it was structured before the coup, with analysis of the post-coup collapse. Given the ongoing conflict, all figures are indicative estimates and conditions are changing rapidly. Planning, investment, and sector reform are not feasible under the current circumstances.

  • Hydropower (~44–54% of capacity, ~50% of pre-coup generation): Myanmar has abundant hydropower resources along the Ayeyarwady, Salween, and Chindwin rivers. Major pre-coup hydro plants include Yeywa (790 MW, Mandalay region), Paunglaung (1,280 MW, Kayah), Baluchaung (168 MW, Kayah), and several Chinese-constructed plants in Shan State. Many hydro plants are in contested ethnic areas and have been damaged, seized by armed groups, or abandoned since 2021. Paunglaung and several other large plants have been affected by the conflict.
  • Natural gas (~40–50% of capacity, ~40% of pre-coup generation): Myanmar's gas sector was operated by offshore (Yadana, Zawtika, Shwe) and onshore fields under PSCs with TotalEnergies (withdrew July 2022), Chevron (withdrew 2022), PTTEP (Thailand, continued), and others. Gas is exported to Thailand (Yadana pipeline, ~600–700 mmcfd) — export revenue was the military authorities' major foreign currency earner. Domestic gas-fired plants (primarily in Yangon, Mandalay) have faced fuel supply problems due to disinvestment in upstream maintenance.
  • Coal (2–3% of capacity): Limited coal capacity, mainly at Tigyit (120 MW, Shan State) — Chinese-built. Post-coup, Chinese construction companies have reportedly continued or accelerated some coal plant projects under bilateral agreements with the military authorities.
  • Solar (3–5% of capacity): Several solar projects (40–220 MW range) were under construction or in early operation at the time of the coup. Most have stalled. Limited utility-scale solar is operational in the post-coup period.

Pre-coup, Myanmar's electricity demand grew at ~6–8%/yr from a low base, driven by rapid urbanisation, industrial growth, and expanding electrification. Peak demand reached ~5,483 MW by 2020/21. Post-coup, effective demand served has collapsed: major industries have shut or relocated, urban households face 16–20 hours of daily power cuts, and the economic contraction (GDP -18% in FY2021/22 per IMF) has reduced industrial and commercial consumption. Actual power supplied may be as low as 30–40% of pre-coup levels in Yangon; in conflict zones, supply has effectively ceased in many areas.

3. Sector organisation and actors

MOEE / MEPE (military authorities-controlled utilities) Conflict-Affected State Entity

The Ministry of Electricity and Energy (MOEE) and its operating entities — Myanmar Electric Power Enterprise (MEPE) for generation and transmission, Electricity Supply Enterprise (ESE) for rural distribution, and Yangon, Mandalay, and Ayeyarwady EDCs for urban distribution — have been under military (SAC) control since February 2021. These entities are effectively bankrupt in large parts of the country: the CDM revenue-denial campaign has resulted in <3% bill collection in opposition-held areas. Senior technical staff participated in CDM walkouts and have not returned; many are in exile or have been imprisoned. The entities continue to operate with reduced staffing and severe financial constraints, relying on state budget transfers and gas export revenues.

National Unity Government (NUG) — energy policy in exile Parallel Authority

The NUG — the parallel government of elected MPs and civil society leaders — has issued its own energy policy pronouncements and has declared the military authorities' electricity revenues illegitimate. The NUG controls or is allied with groups controlling significant territory, including areas with hydro resources. Its energy ministry has engaged with international donors and development institutions, but has no ability to deliver electricity services on a significant scale. The NUG's international legitimacy (recognised by some governments) creates a dual-authority problem for any post-conflict reconstruction effort.

International companies — most have exited Private Sector

TotalEnergies withdrew from the Yadana gas field in July 2022, citing human rights due diligence under French law and international ethical obligations. Chevron also divested its Myanmar holdings in 2022. PTTEP (Thailand's state oil company) and MOGE (Myanmar Oil and Gas Enterprise, military authorities-controlled) continue operating Yadana under an arrangement that has drawn criticism from human rights organisations. Chinese companies (CITIC, China Power International) remain active in hydro construction and operations under bilateral agreements with the military authorities. New IPP investment in power generation has effectively ceased from Western and multilateral sources.

4. Key issues and challenges

  • Active armed conflict and infrastructure destruction: Myanmar's civil war has seen deliberate targeting of electricity infrastructure: 89+ pylons destroyed, substations sabotaged, and hydro plant personnel evacuated or forced to flee. The Electricity Generating Authority of Thailand (EGAT), which purchases power from Myanmar-side facilities, has experienced supply disruptions. Power infrastructure repair under fire is impossible; the grid in many areas may require wholesale reconstruction post-conflict.
  • Financial collapse of the sector: The revenue-denial campaign has effectively collapsed the utility's revenue base in large population centres. With <3% bill collection in many areas and hyperinflationary kyat depreciation reducing the real value of what is collected, MOEE entities cannot pay for fuel, maintenance, or salaries. The military authorities subsidises the sector from gas export revenues and Chinese loans, but the level of fiscal transfer required to maintain even minimal supply is unsustainable without a political settlement.
  • Gas export revenues as military authorities lifeline: Yadana pipeline gas exports to Thailand (~USD 1–1.5B/yr) are a primary financial lifeline for the SAC military authorities, alongside jade and gem exports. Sanctions debates (particularly in the US and EU) have centred on whether to target these revenues. PTTEP's continued operation of Yadana has been a source of significant controversy. Disruption of gas exports would accelerate the military authorities' financial collapse but would also hurt Thailand's energy security (Yadana supplies ~8–10% of Thailand's gas).
  • Electrification regression: The pre-coup electrification rate of ~72.5% (2021) has almost certainly regressed in conflict-affected areas. In Chin, Kachin, and Sagaing regions — which have seen heavy fighting — grid infrastructure has been destroyed and off-grid diesel generators are the only electricity source for many communities. The humanitarian and development implications of the power crisis compound the direct violence of the conflict.
  • Post-conflict reconstruction deficit: Even under an optimistic scenario of a political settlement in 2025–2026, the electricity sector will require massive reconstruction investment (transmission network, hydro plant rehabilitation, distribution restoration) before normal service can resume. The scale of destruction, the legacy of pre-coup under-investment, and the need to restore technical staff confidence (most CDM participants remain outside MOEE) make reconstruction a multi-year, multi-billion dollar challenge requiring international support.

5. Five key takeaways

1
Myanmar's electricity sector has effectively collapsed under the weight of the post-2021 civil war: an estimated 2,000–2,800 MW of daily generation serves a pre-coup peak demand of 5,483 MW, resulting in 16–20 hours of daily load-shedding in major cities and near-total supply loss in contested areas.
2
The revenue-denial campaign is one of the most effective economic warfare tactics in the conflict: <3% bill collection in opposition areas has rendered the military authorities' distribution utilities financially non-functional, forcing reliance on gas export revenues and Chinese financial support to maintain any semblance of service.
3
Yadana gas exports to Thailand (~USD 1B+/yr) are a critical SAC military authorities revenue source; PTTEP's continued operation is commercially necessary for Thailand's energy security but ethically and politically contested — this is the electricity sector's most sensitive geopolitical pressure point.
4
International energy companies (TotalEnergies, Chevron) have exited; Chinese companies remain active under bilateral agreements with the military authorities — Myanmar's post-coup energy sector is increasingly a Chinese-sphere dependency, with long-term implications for post-conflict energy architecture and financing.
5
Any credible post-conflict reconstruction scenario requires sector-wide rehabilitation: 89+ destroyed pylons, abandoned hydro plants in contested territories, hollowed-out technical workforce (CDM participants largely in exile), and a financial framework that does not assume continuity of pre-coup institutions — this is not a repair task but a reconstruction.
