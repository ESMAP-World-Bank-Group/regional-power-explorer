1. Energy context

Mauritania is a large, sparsely populated Saharan country (4.6 million people, 1 million km²) with electricity infrastructure concentrated in Nouakchott (the capital) and the main urban centres, while rural areas — home to the majority of the population — have virtually no grid access (rural electrification rate: 3.8%). The country has two major energy transformation stories underway simultaneously: the advent of offshore oil and gas production at the Grand Tortue Ahmeyim (GTA) FLNG facility (shared with Senegal, first LNG Phase 1 in early 2025), and the development of extraordinary renewable energy potential — Mauritania's wind and solar resources are among the best in Africa, anchoring the CWP Global "Aman" project: a proposed 30 GW green hydrogen complex that would be one of the world's largest.

The electricity sector is managed by SOMELEC (Société Mauritanienne d'Electricité), a state-owned utility with significant IFC and World Bank support. SOMELEC serves primarily Nouakchott, Nouadhibou, and the Iron Mining Area (SNIM, the state iron ore company), with provincial towns served by isolated diesel mini-grids. Like most WAPP utilities, SOMELEC operates at a loss due to HFO fuel costs and below-cost tariffs, though the GTA LNG development offers a potential path to cheaper gas-fired generation in the medium term.

  • Oil/HFO (~78% of generation): SOMELEC's HFO thermal plants — principally the Nouakchott Power Station (multiple units, ~500+ MW total) and isolated generators in Atar, Kiffa, Zouerate (SNIM mining), and other towns — dominate generation. Fuel imports account for the largest share of the country's energy import bill. The GTA first gas (early 2025) is expected to begin supplying a gas-to-power conversion unit at Nouakchott, potentially displacing 200–300 MW of HFO generation with cheaper domestic gas by 2026–2027.
  • Hydropower (~10% of generation): Mauritania's OMVS share of the Manantali hydropower plant (200 MW total, Mauritania's allocation ~15% = ~30 MW) provides the only hydro generation. The OMVS 225 kV ring connects Manantali to Nouakchott. Hydro output is subject to the same Sahel drought variability affecting Mali and Senegal.
  • Solar (~7% of generation): Several utility-scale solar parks have been commissioned: Toujounine (15 MWp, Nouakchott, IRENA-supported), Sheikh Zayed (15 MWp), and several smaller sites total ~65–70 MW. A larger 50–100 MW solar programme is under development with AfDB and IRENA support. Mauritania's solar irradiance exceeds 5.5 kWh/m²/day — among the highest on the continent.
  • Wind (~5% of generation): Mauritania's Alizé wind zone (Atlantic coast) has exceptional wind resources. SOMELEC commissioned a 30 MW wind farm at Nouakchott. The planned "Aman" green hydrogen project (CWP Global, 30 GW total wind/solar, phased development) would dwarf all current wind capacity by several orders of magnitude — it is aspirational rather than imminent, but indicative of the resource endowment.

3. Sector organisation

SOMELEC — Société Mauritanienne d'Electricité State Utility

SOMELEC is the vertically-integrated state utility, responsible for generation, transmission, and urban distribution in Mauritania. It serves approximately 220,000 metered customers (as of 2023), concentrated in Nouakchott and Nouadhibou. SOMELEC is financially stressed — HFO costs dominate the P&L — but has been a relatively competent technical operator compared to many WAPP peers, benefiting from IFC institutional support and consistent management continuity. A World Bank-supported technical assistance programme has helped SOMELEC modernise billing and improve collection efficiency in recent years. SOMELEC manages the OMVS Manantali power purchase agreement on behalf of Mauritania.

SNIM — Société Nationale Industrielle et Minière Mining / Self-generator

SNIM, the state iron ore company operating the Guelb el Rhein and Tazadit mines near Zouerate, is Mauritania's largest electricity consumer. SNIM operates its own dedicated HFO/diesel power plant (jointly managed with SOMELEC under a co-generation arrangement) and a 57 km electric mining railway. SNIM's electricity demand is the principal industrial load in the country; any SNIM production expansion directly drives electricity infrastructure investment requirements at Zouerate.

4. Key issues and challenges

  • GTA gas-to-power — the transformative upstream development: The GTA LNG FLNG facility (Phase 1, ~2.5 MTPA, BP/Kosmos/PETROSEN/SMHPM joint development) produced first LNG in early 2025. Mauritania has provisions to purchase domestic gas at below-export-parity prices for power generation. Converting Nouakchott's HFO thermal fleet to natural gas would reduce generation costs by ~30–40%, significantly improving SOMELEC's financial position and lowering tariffs. The midstream challenge — a gas pipeline from the offshore facility to a Nouakchott-area CCGT — is the critical missing link, requiring USD 400–600M of investment that is not yet fully financed.
  • Aman green hydrogen — transformative but distant: CWP Global's "Aman" project (30 GW of wind and solar for green hydrogen/ammonia export) is one of the world's largest proposed green energy projects. If built, it would transform Mauritania's economy — export revenues from green hydrogen could exceed current mining revenues. But the project faces immense challenges: infrastructure, water supply (in a desert country), local content requirements, offtake agreements, and financing at a scale that has never been achieved for a single project. The most credible scenario is a phased development beginning with a 100–500 MW pilot, rather than the full 30 GW at once.
  • Urban-rural access chasm: Nouakchott electrification is ~85% and improving, but rural electrification is 3.8% — among the lowest in Africa. The semi-nomadic pastoral population of eastern Mauritania and the Saharan interior is effectively unserved by any grid or off-grid programme. Reaching these populations requires mobile solar solutions (solar lanterns, solar-powered water pumps) rather than fixed infrastructure. The government's rural electrification programme (ANER — Agence Nationale des Energies Renouvelables) has made progress in peri-urban areas but barely touches the remote interior.
  • Transition risk from HFO: As long as SOMELEC's generation mix is 78% HFO, global oil price volatility is an existential financial risk. The 2021–2022 oil price spike was absorded through government subsidy transfers and SOMELEC balance sheet deterioration. GTA gas-to-power conversion is the structural hedge against this risk, but the timeline and scale of conversion need to be accelerated to provide meaningful financial protection before the next oil price shock.

5. Five key takeaways

1
Mauritania is WAPP's most geographically isolated member from the main WAPP interconnection spine, yet it is on the verge of a major energy transition: GTA LNG first gas (2025) will for the first time give Mauritania access to domestic natural gas for power generation, potentially replacing expensive HFO and improving SOMELEC's financial viability.
2
The Aman 30 GW green hydrogen project is the most ambitious energy export proposal in West Africa — if even 10% of its planned scale is realised, it would transform Mauritania's economy. But it is a 2030s-and-beyond story, dependent on global green hydrogen offtake markets that are still developing.
3
The urban-rural electrification gap (85% urban vs. 3.8% rural) is the starkest in the WAPP zone after Niger — it reflects the geographic impossibility of grid extension in a country the size of Egypt with 4.6 million people scattered across desert and semi-desert terrain, requiring off-grid solar as the primary access vehicle.
4
SNIM (iron ore mining) is Mauritania's most important electricity customer and a driver of infrastructure investment at Zouerate — maintaining SNIM's electricity supply is a direct national security and revenue concern, making the Zouerate industrial power system as strategically important as the Nouakchott city grid.
5
Mauritania's wind and solar resource is genuinely exceptional — Saharan solar irradiance and Atlantic Alizé wind combine to create one of the lowest LCOE renewable environments in Africa. Whether this resource is captured for domestic electrification, green hydrogen export, or both will be one of the defining infrastructure governance questions of the next decade.
