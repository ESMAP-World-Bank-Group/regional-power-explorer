1. Energy context

Somalia's electricity sector has operated without a national utility or national grid for over three decades. The state-owned Somali National Electricity Company (SNEC) collapsed with the government in 1991 during the civil war. In its absence, a fragmented system of private diesel operators, informal neighbourhood mini-grid operators, and increasingly solar PV micro-enterprises has grown organically to serve urban areas. This private-sector-by-default electricity market is one of the most unique on the continent — expensive, fragmented, and without any regulatory framework until very recently.

Mogadishu is served primarily by BECO (Benadir Electric Company), established in 2013 as a parastatal utility to replace the informal private operators in the capital. BECO operates approximately 80 MW of diesel and HFO generation, providing electricity to roughly 85% of Mogadishu's connected consumers. Outside Mogadishu, electricity is supplied by a patchwork of private operators ranging from 50 kW neighbourhood generators to 5–10 MW town-scale diesel plants in Garowe, Bosasso, Kismayo, and other regional cities. None are interconnected.

Somalia passed a National Electricity Act in March 2023 and established a National Electricity Agency (NEA) in July 2023 — representing the first serious attempt to create a regulatory framework since 1991. The World Bank's SERP (Somali Energy Resilience Project, USD 150 million) is the primary financing vehicle for near-term solar hybrid capacity and grid formalisation in Mogadishu. Progress is real but incremental in a deeply constrained institutional environment.

2. The electricity system

2.1 Generation — private diesel and growing solar

  • BECO (Mogadishu): Benadir Electric Company operates ~80 MW of HFO and diesel generation. Serves ~550,000 consumers in Mogadishu across ~350 km of distribution lines. BECO was created from the merger/absorption of the informal private operators who filled the vacuum after 1991. It charges a commercial tariff (~USD 0.50/kWh), collects pre-payment (mobile money-linked metering), and is financially self-sustaining — a significant achievement for a post-conflict utility. BECO currently supplies ~4–6 hours per day of reliable power to most consumers.
  • Private operators (secondary cities): Licensed or unlicensed private diesel operators in Garowe, Bosasso, Kismayo, Beledweyne, Dhusamareb, Baidoa, and other regional centres. Capacity ranges from 0.5 MW to ~10 MW per town. Tariffs: USD 0.40–0.80/kWh. Most are family-owned businesses with no external financing or regulatory oversight. Al-Shabaab extorts payments from operators in areas it controls.
  • Solar PV (growing rapidly): Falling solar panel prices have driven rapid uptake of solar PV micro-systems in Somalia. Solar is increasingly competitive against diesel given Somalia's high irradiance (~2,200 kWh/m²/year). Solar home systems (PAYGo), rooftop commercial solar, and small hybrid systems are growing in all urban centres. Solar share of total supply is estimated at ~10–15% and rising.
  • Fanoole Hydropower (defunct): The 4.6 MW Fanoole dam on the Jubba River (Jubaland) was built in the 1980s and destroyed during the civil war. Rehabilitation has been discussed but not financed. The only potential hydropower site in Somalia proper.
Somalia's private electricity sector — accidental market: Somalia's electricity supply emerged entirely from private initiative in the absence of the state. This makes it unique in Africa: prices are market-clearing (high, USD 0.40–0.80/kWh) rather than subsidised; operators are commercially oriented; mobile money pre-payment (M-Pesa/EVC Plus/Hormuud) reduces collection risk; and competition between operators in some cities has driven modest price reductions. The market works — expensively — in a way that most state-owned utilities in the region do not. The challenge is expanding access and reducing costs, not creating a commercial culture that is already there.

2.2 Electrification — what the 50% figure means

The World Bank's ~50% electrification rate for Somalia is significantly higher than the situation would suggest. This figure uses a broad definition that includes any household with access to any electricity source — including a single PAYGo solar lantern, a shared neighbourhood generator providing 2 hours per day, or a small private solar system with only lighting capacity. Under a strict definition (meaningful electricity access: enough for multiple appliances, study lighting, phone charging, and some productive use), the effective rate is likely 15–25%.

The geographic distribution is highly skewed: Mogadishu (~2.5 million people), Hargeisa (Somaliland, covered separately), and major port cities have meaningful access. Rural Somalia — particularly in southern and central areas where Al-Shabaab presence is strongest — has essentially no electricity infrastructure of any kind.

4. Key issues and dynamics

4.1 Building on the private sector — Somalia's unusual starting point

  • Somalia's electricity challenge is the inverse of most African countries: there is no subsidised state utility to reform, no cross-subsidy to dismantle, and no political patron distributing cheap electricity. Instead, a commercially functioning private market exists at high cost. The policy challenge is: how to use regulation and public financing to lower costs, expand coverage, and improve quality without destroying the commercial incentives that have driven the private sector to serve where the state cannot.
  • BECO is the most instructive case: it grew from informal private operators, was formalised into a parastatal, introduced mobile money pre-payment, and is now financially self-sustaining while serving 85% of Mogadishu's connected customers. Replicating this model in Garowe, Bosasso, and Kismayo is the core of the SERP strategy.

4.2 Solar transition — the economics are compelling

  • At USD 0.40–0.80/kWh for diesel, the economics of solar are compelling in Somali cities. A USD 0.10–0.15/kWh solar hybrid system (with battery storage) would cut consumer costs by 60–80%. SERP's Mogadishu 30 MW solar hybrid target is designed to demonstrate this economics. If BECO can transition from diesel to solar hybrid at scale, it becomes a regional model for post-conflict electricity transition.
  • The key constraint is financing: solar requires upfront capital that private operators in post-conflict Somalia cannot access commercially. Donor-funded capital grants or guarantees are necessary to achieve the first movers; thereafter the market should follow.

5. Five things to take away

1
Somalia has had no national electricity grid since 1991. The electricity market that exists — serving roughly 50% of the population under a broad definition — emerged entirely from private initiative in the state's absence. This private-sector-by-default model produces expensive but commercially functional supply in urban areas, and is genuinely unique in the African electricity landscape.
2
BECO (Mogadishu) is Somalia's most advanced electricity institution — a parastatal that grew from informal private operators, integrated mobile money pre-payment, and serves ~550,000 customers commercially at roughly USD 0.50/kWh. It is financially self-sustaining and is the model the SERP project intends to replicate across secondary cities. BECO's trajectory is one of the few genuinely positive electricity sector development stories in the Horn of Africa.
3
The March 2023 National Electricity Act and July 2023 NEA establishment represent Somalia's first electricity regulatory framework in over 30 years. They are early-stage and institutionally fragile, but they create the legal foundation for licensing, investment attraction, and consumer protection that the private market has operated entirely without. The NEA's ability to build credibility without destroying private sector incentives will determine whether this framework enables or hinders sector development.
4
Solar economics make the diesel-to-solar transition commercially compelling for every city in Somalia: at USD 0.40–0.80/kWh diesel costs, solar hybrid at USD 0.10–0.15/kWh represents a 60–80% cost reduction. The World Bank SERP (USD 150 million) is targeting 30 MW of solar hybrid in Mogadishu as a demonstration. If successful, private operators in secondary cities should follow commercially without requiring further public subsidy.
5
Al-Shabaab's control of significant rural territory — and its extortion of electricity operators in those zones — is the most direct security constraint on electricity access expansion. No private operator voluntarily invests in areas where profits will be extorted, and no donor can ensure project safety in insecure zones. Sustainable electricity access in rural Somalia is contingent on political and security stabilisation that the electricity sector alone cannot deliver.
