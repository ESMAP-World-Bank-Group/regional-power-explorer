Generation Mix & Capacity

Argentina's power system is built on natural gas. Roughly 55–60% of generation comes from thermal plants burning domestic gas, increasingly from the Vaca Muerta shale formation in Neuquén, with hydro supplying most of the balance and a small but established nuclear fleet providing firm baseload.

The sector's defining feature for two decades has not been technical but fiscal. End-user tariffs were frozen far below cost, and the gap was absorbed by the treasury through CAMMESA, the wholesale market administrator that functions as a single buyer. The result was a chronic transfer that at its peak consumed a significant share of the federal deficit, starved distribution networks of investment, and left generators owed large arrears.

Since late 2023 the government has pursued the sharpest correction in the sector's history: large tariff increases, a shift from blanket subsidies to targeted social tariffs, and a stated intention to move from the CAMMESA single-buyer model toward direct contracting between generators and large consumers. The transition is incomplete and politically contested, but the direction is a genuine break.

Market Structure & Institutions

  • Secretaría de Energía: Sets policy and, in practice, sets prices, since seasonal programming resolutions determine what generators are paid and what distributors are charged.
  • CAMMESA: Administers the wholesale market (MEM), dispatches the system and acts as counterparty to almost all transactions. Its accumulated payables to generators have been a recurring source of sector stress.
  • ENRE: Regulates transmission and the federal distribution concessions of Edenor and Edesur in greater Buenos Aires; provincial distributors answer to provincial regulators, which fragments tariff policy.
  • Transener: Operates the 500 kV backbone. Transmission investment lagged generation for years, and congestion between the Comahue region and Buenos Aires is a persistent constraint on both hydro and wind.
  • Interconnections: Back-to-back converters at Garabi and Paso de los Libres link Argentina to Brazil; Salto Grande and Concepción del Uruguay to Uruguay; Yacyretá to Paraguay; and several lines to Chile, historically used for gas-linked trade in the north.
  • Rural access gap: National access is about 98.4%, but rural access is near 80%, one of the widest urban-rural gaps in the Southern Cone and concentrated in the northern provinces and Patagonia.
Argentina is the clearest case in the region of a power sector whose problems are financial rather than physical. The country has abundant gas, world-class wind resources in Patagonia, and enough installed capacity to meet demand. What it has lacked is a price that clears, and the institutional credibility for private capital to commit to fifteen-year contracts. The current adjustment tests whether that can be rebuilt without a social rupture that reverses it again.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Subsidy reform is the sector's single determining variable. Every other question, generation investment, distribution quality, arrears to generators, follows from whether tariffs converge to cost and stay there. Previous attempts were reversed within an electoral cycle, which is why investors discount the current one until it survives one.
2
Dismantling the single buyer is harder than announcing it. Moving from CAMMESA-mediated trade to bilateral contracts requires a creditworthy counterparty structure, functioning collateral, and distributors able to buy at market prices. None of those conditions is fully in place, so the reform is likely to proceed by widening the MATER carve-out rather than by a clean break.
3
Vaca Muerta makes Argentina a potential regional gas and power exporter. Reversed pipeline flows already let Argentina export gas to Chile and, intermittently, to Brazil via Bolivia. Sustained shale output would let Argentina supply both molecules and electricity across the Southern Cone, which would change the regional trade balance more than any transmission project.
4
Patagonian wind is stranded by transmission, not by resource. Capacity factors in Santa Cruz and Chubut are among the best onshore anywhere, but the 500 kV corridor north is full. Without new lines the resource cannot reach demand, and the MATER queue keeps allocating capacity that cannot be evacuated.
5
The rural access gap is the quiet equity issue. With urban access effectively universal and rural access near 80%, the remaining unserved population is dispersed, poor and politically marginal. Closing that gap is a distribution and off-grid problem that no wholesale market reform addresses.
