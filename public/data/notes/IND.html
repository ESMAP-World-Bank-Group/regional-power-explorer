Generation Mix & Capacity

India is the world's third-largest electricity market and the third-largest in renewable energy installed capacity, with a total of approximately 476 GW as of June 2025. In a historic milestone, India crossed 50% non-fossil fuel installed capacity that month. Solar PV has driven the transformation — reaching 110.9 GW (up from near zero in 2010) — while coal at ~222 GW remains the dominant technology by installed capacity and is the source of the majority of actual electricity generated. Wind stands at 51.3 GW, large hydro at ~47 GW, and nuclear at 8.78 GW.

Market Structure

India's electricity sector is a federal, multi-layer system with complex centre-state division of responsibility:

  • CEA (Central Electricity Authority): Federal planning and technical advisory body; publishes the National Electricity Plan and capacity outlooks.
  • CERC (Central Electricity Regulatory Commission): Federal regulator for inter-state transmission and wholesale tariffs; SERCs regulate at state level.
  • NTPC: State-owned generation company (~73 GW); largest power producer in India; transitioning toward solar and hybrid RE.
  • Power Grid Corporation (PGCIL): State-owned national transmission company; manages inter-state transmission corridors and HVDC links.
  • DISCOMs: State-owned distribution companies — the weakest link; aggregate losses historically Rs 5–6 trillion; AT&C losses 15–20%; financially stressed entities that cannot pay generators on time, creating a systemic payment risk chain.
  • IEX (India Energy Exchange): Power exchange; real-time and day-ahead market; growing short-term trading volume.
  • Private IPPs: Adani Green (largest private solar/wind developer), ReNew Power, Greenko, Torrent — private sector has become the dominant driver of new RE capacity.
India's electricity transition is simultaneously the world's largest decarbonisation opportunity and one of its most complex implementation challenges. At 476 GW and growing, India adds more power capacity each year than most countries have in total. The solar surge is real — 110 GW installed, the price of solar power at INR 2–3/kWh ($0.024–0.036/kWh) making it the cheapest electricity ever procured. But coal still generates ~60% of electricity and the DISCOM payment chain remains the sector's structural Achilles' heel.

The 2030 Targets & CEA Roadmap

India committed to 500 GW of non-fossil installed capacity by 2030 at COP26. The CEA's 20th Electric Power Survey and subsequent plans map out how:

  • 500 GW non-fossil by 2030: Requires ~280 GW solar, 100 GW wind, 50 GW hydro + pumped storage, 10 GW nuclear; against a 2025 baseline of ~240 GW non-fossil. A ~260 GW non-fossil addition in 5 years — achievable if deployment rates are sustained.
  • CEA 2029-30 optimal mix (777 GW total): 292.6 GW solar, 99.9 GW wind, 63.6 GW hydro, 94.5 GW coal (reduced from 222 GW), with coal's share falling from 47% to 12% of installed capacity.
  • Transmission bottleneck: Adding 260 GW of renewables requires massive new transmission — green energy corridors, interstate HVDC, and distribution network upgrades — where India has historically lagged generation capacity additions.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
India's solar transition is the world's most consequential energy story of the 2020s. Going from 10 GW (2017) to 110 GW (2025) to a projected 292 GW (2030) in 13 years — in a market of 1.4 billion people — will determine whether global 1.5°C targets remain plausible. Solar at sub-INR 3/kWh has made new coal uneconomical in most comparative analyses; the question is whether the political economy allows an orderly coal transition.
2
The DISCOM problem is India's most persistent and dangerous structural barrier to the energy transition. State distribution companies with Rs 5+ trillion in accumulated losses, AT&C losses of 15–20%, and payment arrears to generators are the choke point in India's power sector. DISCOM reform — revising tariffs, reducing agricultural cross-subsidies, improving collection — is a state-level political challenge that central government incentive programmes (UDAY, RDSS) have partially but not fully addressed.
3
Pumped storage hydro is India's strategic battery — and the government has recognised it. With 110+ GW of solar and 51 GW of wind, India faces a growing need for long-duration storage to shift daytime solar surplus to evening peak demand. Pumped storage hydro (PSH) — India has 96 GW of assessed potential — is the only technology that can provide this at the scale needed. MNRE's 2023 pumped storage policy is the right framework; execution speed will determine whether the 2030 grid stays stable.
4
India is emerging as the anchor of South Asian electricity trade — but has been slow to operationalise this role. Nepal and Bhutan already export hydro to India. Bangladesh trades bi-directionally. Sri Lanka is studying a subsea cable. The SAARC framework has been ineffective, but bilateral agreements are advancing. India's willingness to expand import capacity from Nepal and Bhutan — while also exporting to Bangladesh and Myanmar — will define the region's electricity integration trajectory.
5
New coal additions are economically unjustifiable but politically persistent. At INR 2–3/kWh solar vs. INR 5–7/kWh new coal, new coal plants should not be built. Yet India continues commissioning coal plants partly due to employment in coal states, partly due to plant-level capacity contract obligations, and partly due to genuine peak demand security concerns. The 94.5 GW coal target in CEA's 2029-30 optimal mix — down from 222 GW today — implies significant early retirement of existing plants, which will require social and political management.
