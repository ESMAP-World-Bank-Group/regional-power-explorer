Generation Mix & Capacity

Colombia runs the most genuinely competitive wholesale power market in the Andean region. Generators bid into a day-ahead energy market cleared by XM, and firm capacity is remunerated separately through the Cargo por Confiabilidad, a reliability charge backed by obligations that generators must honour when prices exceed a scarcity threshold. That design, introduced after the 1992 rationing crisis, is widely studied and has been copied in part elsewhere.

Generation is roughly 65 to 70% hydro, with gas and coal thermal providing the balance and, critically, the dry-season backstop. Colombia's hydrology is driven by ENSO, so El Niño years produce simultaneous low inflows, high prices and heavy thermal dispatch. The 2023–24 episode pushed reservoirs low enough that rationing was publicly discussed.

Two structural pressures now dominate. Domestic gas reserves are declining, making the thermal backstop dependent on imported LNG through Cartagena just as it is needed most. And the non-conventional renewable build-out, centred on the wind resource of La Guajira, has been repeatedly delayed by transmission and by prior consultation processes with Wayúu communities.

Market Structure & Institutions

  • Ministerio de Minas y Energía: Sets policy; UPME does planning and publishes the expansion and transmission reference plans.
  • CREG: The Comisión de Regulación de Energía y Gas sets tariffs and market rules, including the reliability charge design. Its quorum and independence have been politically contested in recent years, which itself became a regulatory risk.
  • XM: Operates the national interconnected system and administers the wholesale market. Colombia is bid-based, unlike Brazil or Chile, which makes its price signal sharper and its scarcity episodes more visible.
  • Unbundled and partly private: Generation, transmission and distribution are separated, with a mix of state, municipal and private ownership. EPM, owned by the city of Medellín, is the largest single group.
  • Interconnection with Ecuador: 230 kV links carry substantial trade, historically with Colombia exporting to Ecuador in Ecuador's dry season. This is the most-used international interconnection in the Andean region.
  • Interconnection with Panama: The long-proposed link to the SIEPAC system in Central America would connect two regional markets; it has been studied for decades and repeatedly deferred on cost and regulatory grounds.
Colombia's reliability charge is the region's most successful answer to the question of how a hydro-dominated market survives drought. It works, but it works by paying for thermal capacity, and the fuel for that capacity is running out domestically. The next decade tests whether the same mechanism can be made to procure storage, demand response and imports rather than gas Colombia no longer produces.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
ENSO exposure is the system's defining operational risk. A hydro share near 70% means every strong El Niño becomes an energy security and price event simultaneously. Colombia manages it better than its neighbours, but management costs money and depends on thermal fuel being available.
2
Declining domestic gas turns reliability into an import question. The reliability charge assumes the contracted thermal plants can actually run. With reserves falling, that depends on LNG cargoes and regasification capacity, which introduces price and logistics risk the original design did not contemplate.
3
La Guajira shows that permitting, not resource or finance, is the binding constraint. Auction-winning wind projects with signed contracts have sat undeliverable for years because of transmission delays and unresolved prior consultation. It is the clearest case in the region of social licence determining an energy transition timetable.
4
Regulatory stability at CREG matters as much as any tariff. Investors price the risk that market rules change or that the regulator cannot act. Periods of contested appointments and quorum have coincided with weaker investment commitments, independently of the underlying economics.
5
Colombia is the natural hub of any Andean market integration. It already trades meaningfully with Ecuador, sits between the Andean system and Central America, and runs the region's most market-compatible rules. Any serious Andean–SIEPAC integration would be built around Colombian institutions.
