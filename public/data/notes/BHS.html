Generation Mix & Sector Overview

The Bahamas presents one of the Caribbean's most complex electricity supply challenges: a fragmented archipelago of 700 islands spread over 260,000 km² of ocean, with 30 inhabited islands each requiring independent power supply, and the national utility — Bahamas Power and Light (BPL) — historically struggling to serve even the two main grids (New Providence/Nassau and Grand Bahama/Freeport) reliably. Virtually all generation is diesel or heavy fuel oil: BPL's main plants are the Blue Hills Power Station (New Providence, ~400 MW HFO) and the Clifton Pier station. The Family Islands (Exuma, Eleuthera, Andros, Bimini, Cat Island, Long Island, San Salvador, and others) each have BPL diesel mini-grids supplemented by private generators in tourism resorts. The Bahamas has no domestic fossil fuel production and imports all fuel, making it one of the Western Hemisphere's most fuel-import-dependent economies relative to income level — high per-capita GDP (US$34,000+, tourism-driven) with high energy costs of approximately US$0.33/kWh. Hurricane Dorian (Category 5, September 2019) destroyed much of Abaco Island and severely damaged Grand Bahama — the most costly Atlantic hurricane in history by some measures — demonstrating the existential electricity infrastructure risk from intensifying storms.

BPL: a struggling state utility with a reform historyBahamas Power and Light was formerly Bahamas Electricity Corporation (BEC); rebranded 2016 after a concession agreement collapsed (PowerSecure International, a Duke Energy subsidiary, withdrew from a 10-year management contract after 18 months in 2016). BPL remains state-owned, government-subsidised (US$150-200M/year implicit subsidy), and hampered by aging equipment (Blue Hills Plant was built in the 1960s-80s, repeatedly extended), high transmission losses (~15%), and poor billing collection in outer islands. The Electricity Regulatory Authority (ERA) established 2016 as an independent regulator — still developing regulatory capacity and tariff methodology. A new 185 MW LNG-to-power project for New Providence (BFSB procurement, 2022-26) would shift main island generation from HFO to cleaner gas — pending.
Hurricane Dorian 2019: electricity infrastructure destroyedHurricane Dorian (Category 5, sustained 295 km/h, September 2019) made landfall on Great Abaco and Grand Bahama. Abaco: ~70% of structures destroyed; BPL electrical infrastructure entirely destroyed; island re-electrification required complete rebuild. Grand Bahama: BPL Freeport plant flooded and offline for 6+ months; interim diesel generators. Reconstruction cost: ~US$3.4B total (Bahamas GDP ~US$13B). The electricity rebuild in Abaco used more resilient infrastructure and incorporated solar + battery microgrids in parts. Dorian has permanently changed Bahamas electricity planning: all new Family Island projects must meet Category 5 wind standards, and solar + battery is increasingly preferred over diesel-only mini-grids for resilience reasons.
Family Islands: solar + storage replacing dieselThe ~28 inhabited Family Islands each have diesel mini-grids of 0.5-10 MW scale. Fuel delivery cost (barge from Nassau, once or twice monthly) means delivered diesel cost is US$1.20-1.80/litre in remote islands — making solar + battery economically superior on a levelised cost basis even without any capital subsidy. IDB has financed 5 Family Island solar hybrid projects (2020-2025): Exuma, Cat Island, Bimini, Long Island, San Salvador — each replacing ~50-80% of diesel with solar + BESS. The programme is the practical foundation of Bahamas' 30% RE target. Full Family Island solar hybridisation would represent ~50 MW of solar across 28 islands.
Tourism demand: hotels as RE anchor tenantsThe Bahamas receives ~6-7M tourists/year (cruise + stopover); major resort complexes on New Providence (Atlantis Paradise Island, Baha Mar), Grand Bahama, Exuma, and Eleuthera are major electricity consumers and increasingly installing rooftop solar. Atlantis (2,300 rooms) has 5 MW solar on casino and conference facility rooftops; Baha Mar (2,300 rooms) has 3 MW rooftop solar. Hotel solar reduces BPL demand but also represents embedded generation that complicates BPL revenue model. The Bahamian government is developing net metering regulations (draft 2024) to formalise hotel solar interconnection terms.
30% RE target 2030: pathway and gapsBahamas NDC commits to 30% RE by 2030; current RE share ~4% (dominated by hotel + BPL utility solar on New Providence). Gap: ~26 percentage points in 6 years. Required additional capacity: ~150-180 MW of solar (New Providence: 80-100 MW utility-scale; Family Islands: 50 MW hybrid; Grand Bahama: 30-40 MW). No offshore wind development is planned (Jones Act complications for US-flagged vessels complicate offshore logistics). LNG for base-load (Blue Hills replacement) reduces emissions but doesn't count as RE. The 30% target is achievable if Family Island solar programme scales as planned and New Providence utility-scale solar site is identified and permitted.

The Archipelago Energy Challenge

The Bahamas' defining energy challenge — shared with Maldives, Kiribati, and other multi-island SIDS — is that a 700-island geography makes every grid engineering problem harder and more expensive:

  • No interconnection possible: The islands are spread over 260,000 km² of ocean, making any inter-island cable economically infeasible except possibly Nassau-Andros (~50 km) or Nassau-Eleuthera (~80 km). Each island must balance its own grid in real-time — a challenge that grows as solar penetration increases on mini-grids with limited inertia.
  • Fuel logistics as critical infrastructure: BPL's reliability depends on fuel barge scheduling from Nassau. A disruption — storm, mechanical failure, supplier default — creates immediate generation risk on outer islands. During Dorian, multiple Family Islands ran out of diesel within days. Solar + battery reduces this single-point-of-failure risk.
  • Dorian's lesson on resilient infrastructure: The Abaco rebuild incorporated elevated substations, underground cabling in some areas, and solar + battery at hospital and emergency facilities. This resilience-by-design approach is now standard for Family Island BPL projects — adding 10-15% to capital cost but dramatically reducing storm restoration time.
The Bahamas is the Caribbean's most extreme case of archipelago electricity fragmentation: 30 separate grids, 100% fuel-import dependency, high per-capita income that inflates energy costs without subsidising them adequately, and existential hurricane risk intensified by climate change. The Family Island solar hybridisation programme (IDB-financed) is the most impactful RE initiative in the country — not because it's large, but because replacing diesel on isolated mini-grids achieves the highest marginal cost reduction and resilience gain of any Caribbean energy investment per dollar.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
The Bahamas' electricity geography — 30 separate mini-grids across 260,000 km² of ocean — means that the relevant unit of analysis for energy policy is not the national grid but the individual island mini-grid, and interventions that work at national scale (large utility solar, grid storage, demand response) require island-specific engineering solutions that multiply cost and complexity by a factor of 10-30. A 100 MW solar farm on New Providence is technically and commercially straightforward; an equivalent energy impact across 28 Family Islands requires 28 separate feasibility studies, 28 EIAs, 28 procurement processes, 28 interconnection agreements, and 28 O&M contracts. The fragmentation premium — the additional cost and time imposed by archipelago geography — is the defining structural constraint in Bahamian energy planning.
2
Hurricane Dorian permanently changed the Bahamas' energy risk calculus: Category 5 resilience is now a baseline requirement for all BPL infrastructure, and solar + battery mini-grids are demonstrably more hurricane-resilient than diesel plants because solar panels (mounted low-profile) and BESS containers withstand wind better than above-ground fuel tanks and diesel generator buildings. This resilience premium for solar + battery — which would not exist in a temperate climate — makes the economic case for Family Island RE transition stronger than pure LCOE calculations suggest. Insurance actuaries and sovereign debt rating agencies increasingly treat Bahamas' electricity infrastructure resilience as a credit factor. Post-Dorian reconstruction that embedded solar + battery in Abaco's new infrastructure has reduced that island's restoration time from storms by an estimated 60-70%.
3
BPL's institutional weakness — two failed private sector partnerships (BEC→BPL), chronic maintenance underfunding, billing losses, and political interference in tariff-setting — is a more binding constraint on Bahamas' energy transition than technology availability or financing access, because even well-designed RE projects cannot be sustained without a competent utility capable of operating and maintaining them. The 2016 collapse of the PowerSecure management contract revealed that private operators cannot solve governance problems that originate in political economy — BPL's board appointments, procurement decisions, and tariff adjustments are all politically influenced. Institutional strengthening (independent board, performance-based management, transparent tariff methodology) must precede or accompany RE project development, not follow it.
4
The Bahamas' high per-capita GDP (US$34,000+) excludes it from most concessional SIDS financing windows (IDA, LDC climate funds) despite having energy vulnerability metrics — import dependency, hurricane exposure, tariff affordability for lower-income households — that exceed those of many lower-income Caribbean peers, exposing a systematic gap in international climate finance architecture for upper-middle-income SIDS. The Bahamas cannot access IDA-priced loans for electricity infrastructure despite its climate vulnerability. This forces BPL to borrow commercially (at 6-8% vs. 1-2% for IDA borrowers) for the same solar + battery projects that receive subsidised MDB finance in Haiti, Guyana, or Pacific SIDS. The Bridgetown Initiative's proposed vulnerability-adjusted lending rates would directly address this gap for the Bahamas.
5
The Bahamas' proposed LNG-to-power transition for New Providence (replacing aging HFO at Blue Hills) is the correct near-term decision for reliability and emissions improvement, but must be explicitly designed as a bridge to renewable energy rather than a 30-year lock-in — LNG FSRU contracts of 10-15 years are appropriate; longer-term power purchase agreements for gas-fired generation would compromise the 2030 RE target and strand assets prematurely. New Providence needs reliable base-load capacity that existing aging HFO cannot provide; LNG gas turbines are cheaper to build than equivalent RE + storage at the scale needed (~200 MW firm capacity). But the LNG investment is only justifiable if the gas infrastructure is sized to transition to renewable hydrogen or green ammonia co-firing after 2035 — requiring that vessels and terminal infrastructure are compatible with future fuel substitution from the outset.
