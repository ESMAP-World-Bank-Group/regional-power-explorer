Generation Mix & Capacity

Jordan has approximately 6.85 GW of installed electricity capacity: ~4.2 GW conventional (gas, diesel, shale oil) and 2.63 GW renewable (solar PV, wind, small hydro). Jordan is a resource-poor country — no oil, limited gas, no major rivers — that has transformed its electricity system through aggressive renewable deployment in response to chronic energy import dependency and high fuel costs. Distributed solar generated 2,240 GWh in 2024 (9% of electricity sales), a dramatic bottom-up transformation driven by businesses and households investing in rooftop solar following the removal of a restrictive 1 MWAC net metering cap in September 2024. National Energy Strategy targets 50% renewable capacity by 2030.

Energy Security Context

Jordan's renewable energy push is explicitly an energy security and fiscal response to fuel import dependency:

  • Jordan imports ~90% of its energy needs, making it one of the most energy-import-dependent countries in the world. Historical reliance on Egyptian gas via the Arab Gas Pipeline was disrupted repeatedly by pipeline sabotage in Sinai (2011–2013), costing Jordan billions.
  • The pivot to domestic renewables reduces the foreign currency drain from fuel imports — an acute fiscal pressure for Jordan, which receives US and Gulf aid to cover chronic current account deficits.
  • The 200 MWAC solar BOO tender launched April 2025 is the latest in Jordan's systematic solar expansion; private developers compete with tariffs typically $0.02–0.04/kWh in Jordan's competitive market environment.
  • Jordan–Israel energy deals: a 600 MW solar-to-Jordan deal (Jordanian solar powering desalinated water to Jordan) and an electricity import agreement from Israel are active — reflecting the pragmatic normalisation of energy relations following the 1994 peace treaty.
Jordan is arguably the Arab world's most successful renewable energy market per capita. A country of 11 million with no fossil fuel resources has built 2.6 GW of renewable capacity, driven by competitive IPP tenders, a functional regulatory framework, and a national energy strategy that treats renewables as an economic survival imperative, not an environmental aspiration. The September 2024 removal of the 1 MWAC distributed solar cap is the latest reform step — and the 2,240 GWh of distributed generation it has already enabled dwarfs what the cap-era saw.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Jordan's renewable energy transition is the Arab world's most instructive model for resource-poor, import-dependent economies. Without oil, gas, or major rivers, Jordan has leveraged solar and wind as domestic substitutes for imported fuel — treating them as import substitution, not environmentalism. The institutional framework (NEPCO, EMRC regulator, competitive IPP tendering) is functional. Other resource-poor Arab states (Lebanon, Palestine, Yemen) could learn from Jordan's model if political conditions allowed.
2
The September 2024 removal of the 1 MWAC distributed solar cap is the most consequential single energy policy reform in Jordan in a decade. The cap, introduced in 2019 as a cost-control measure, suppressed a wave of commercial and industrial solar investment. Its removal has unleashed a bottom-up solar deployment that is already measurable in generation data. Jordan is discovering what Lebanon discovered through necessity: distributed solar is the most resilient form of electricity supply in a politically and economically uncertain region.
3
The Jordan–Israel energy relationship is the Gulf-to-Mediterranean clean energy corridor's most strategically sensitive element. The UAE–Israel–Jordan energy deal (UAE solar → Israel → Jordan electricity, in exchange for desalinated water) is the most innovative cross-border energy transaction in the Arab world. It normalises energy trade between Jordan and Israel, creates infrastructure that links Gulf financial capital to Levant electricity markets, and signals that commercial energy logic can operate independently of political conflicts.
4
Jordan's 50% RE capacity target by 2030 is achievable — but grid stability at 50% variable RE share requires storage investment that Jordan has not yet mobilised. The Jordan Solar and Energy Storage Project (100 MW solar + 400 MWh storage, expected 2025) is the first meaningful storage project. As solar and wind reach 50%+ of capacity, evening demand peaks without solar will require dispatchable alternatives — either gas peakers, storage, or demand response. Jordan's grid is not yet sized for this challenge.
5
NEPCO's financial distress is the hidden constraint on Jordan's energy transition pace. Like many emerging market transmission utilities, NEPCO purchases power from IPPs at agreed PPA rates and sells to distributors at regulated tariffs — and has accumulated significant debt from the mismatch, particularly from higher-cost legacy gas PPAs. New renewable IPP PPAs at $0.02–0.04/kWh improve NEPCO's economics long-term, but the legacy debt overhang limits its ability to contract new capacity or invest in grid upgrades without government guarantees or multilateral support.
