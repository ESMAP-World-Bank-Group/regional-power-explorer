Generation Mix & Sector Overview

Poland is the EU's largest coal economy and the bloc's most challenging energy transition case. Coal (hard coal from Silesia and lignite from Turów, Bełchatów, and Pątnów) still accounts for approximately 60% of electricity generation in 2024, down from 75% in 2015 but still the highest share of any EU member state. The country has no nuclear power, no significant hydro, and a historically slow approach to renewables investment — driven by the mining industry's political influence and concerns about energy security and employment in coal communities. However, wind and solar have accelerated dramatically since 2020: onshore wind reached ~17 GW and solar grew from near zero to 20+ GW in five years, the fastest solar deployment rate in the EU relative to starting base. The PSE TSO operates the transmission grid; dominant utilities are state-owned PGE, Tauron, and Enea, joined by Orlen (the oil refiner that acquired Energa and is now Poland's largest energy conglomerate).

Nuclear: Pomerania plant (Westinghouse AP1000)Poland selected Westinghouse (US) to build its first nuclear plant in 2022 — a decision driven partly by energy security after Russia's invasion of Ukraine. Site: Lubiatowo-Kopalino (Pomeranian coast). 3 × AP1000 units, total ~3,450 MW. FID (Final Investment Decision) expected 2025-26; first unit commissioning target 2033-35. Full 3-unit plant by 2040-42. Cost estimate: €20-25B. Government commitment was backed by US strategic partnership; contracts with Westinghouse and Bechtel signed 2024.
Baltic offshore: Orlen/Equinor Baltic PowerPoland's Baltic Sea has ~22 GW of offshore wind potential in Polish waters. First round of permits awarded: Baltic Power (600 MW, Orlen + Equinor, commissioning 2026), Bałtyk II (720 MW, Ørsted/PGE) and Bałtyk III (720 MW). Government target: 5.9 GW offshore by 2030, 18 GW by 2040. Baltic offshore is seen as Poland's most credible path to rapid decarbonisation given wind onshore permitting constraints (10× turbine height distance regulation).
Solar: from zero to 20+ GW in 5 yearsPoland's prosumer solar boom (2019-2024) was one of Europe's fastest — driven by net metering subsidies (My Electricity programme), rising electricity prices, and EU funds. Households and SMEs installed ~15 GW; utility-scale added ~5 GW. PSE faces midday curtailment challenges as solar output exceeds demand in spring/autumn. Grid upgrades lagging behind connection requests by 2-3 years.
EU ETS: €80+/tonne pressure on coalPoland's coal power plants are the largest source of CO₂ in the EU electricity sector — ~100 Mt CO₂/year. EU ETS carbon price (€60-100/tonne in 2024-25) has made coal uneconomical vs. gas and rapidly vs. RE. PGE, Tauron, and Enea each face billions in stranded asset write-downs as coal plants approach end-of-life before being repaid. Government capacity market payments have prolonged coal life; EU is challenging some of these subsidies as state aid.
Energy security: no Russian gas dependency since 2022Poland terminated its Russian gas contract (Yamal contract with Gazprom) in 2022 — one of the first EU members to do so voluntarily. Now supplied via Baltic Pipe (Norway→Denmark→Poland, 10 BCM/year, operational Oct 2022), LNG terminal at Świnoujście (8.3 BCM), and Lithuanian LNG connection. Poland's energy security posture is now fully Western-aligned.

Coal Transition: The EU's Hardest Decarbonisation Challenge

Poland's coal sector is not just an energy issue — it is a social and political economy issue of EU-wide significance:

  • Silesian coal mining employment: ~80,000 miners employed in Upper Silesia, with 3-4× that in indirect employment. The Polish government signed the Silesian coal agreement (2021) committing to keep mines open until 2049 — later challenged by the European Commission as incompatible with EU state aid rules. The timeline was revised, but Poland's coal phase-out date remains the EU's latest.
  • Bełchatów: Europe's largest emitter: The Bełchatów lignite power complex (5,102 MW) is Europe's single largest CO₂ source — emitting ~30 Mt/year. PGE operates it; phase-out target revised to 2036-38 (previously 2049). The lignite mine feeding it will be exhausted by the early 2030s regardless — creating a forced closure timeline that market forces may accelerate.
  • Capacity market payments: Poland's capacity market, approved by the EU, makes payments to coal plants to remain available for winter peak demand. Critics argue this prolongs uneconomical coal operation; supporters argue it's necessary while nuclear and offshore wind are built. The capacity market is scheduled to end (or be reformed) by 2028-2030.
  • Just Transition Fund: Poland is the largest recipient of EU Just Transition Fund (JTF) resources — €3.85B — targeting Silesia, Łódź (Bełchatów), and Lower Silesia (Turów). These funds are intended to reskill miners, attract new industries, and rehabilitate coal-affected landscapes. Delivery progress has been slow due to governance complexity and political resistance to acknowledging that the coal era is ending.
Poland is the EU energy transition's most consequential open question. A country of 38 million that generates more electricity than the Netherlands, Belgium, and Austria combined — and still does so predominantly from coal — cannot decarbonise gradually. The EU's 2035 clean power target effectively mandates Polish coal exit within a decade. The combination of nuclear (2033+), Baltic offshore wind, and solar provides a credible technical pathway. The question is whether Poland can execute on all three simultaneously, while managing the political economy of coal communities, and within the EU's ETS and state aid framework.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Poland's energy transition is the EU's single largest decarbonisation challenge by absolute CO₂ volume — and its success or failure will materially affect whether the EU meets its 2030 and 2035 climate targets. Poland's electricity sector emits ~100 Mt CO₂/year — roughly the combined electricity emissions of France, Sweden, Austria, and Norway. A five-year delay in Poland's coal exit adds ~500 Mt to EU cumulative emissions. Brussels understands this arithmetic: Poland receives more climate transition funding than any other member state, and the European Commission has been willing to grant timeline flexibility in exchange for credible decarbonisation commitments.
2
Poland's selection of Westinghouse AP1000 for its first nuclear plant was as much a geopolitical statement as an energy procurement — and the US strategic partnership that underpins the deal is now a structural pillar of Poland's energy security architecture. Poland chose between Westinghouse (US), EDF (France), and KHNP (Korea). Choosing Westinghouse aligned with Poland's deepening US military relationship (largest US military presence in Europe), gave access to US Export-Import Bank financing, and publicly aligned Poland's energy security with Western suppliers rather than the Korean KHNP track. EDF was eliminated partly due to Flamanville's cost overruns demonstrating French nuclear execution risk.
3
Poland's solar deployment — from near zero to 20+ GW in five years — is the fastest solar scale-up in EU history relative to starting base, and demonstrates that prosumer-led RE deployment can dramatically outpace utility-scale planning timelines. Most of this growth was not planned by PSE or the energy ministry — it emerged from household investment decisions responding to net metering incentives and rising electricity prices. The grid consequence (midday curtailment, connection queue backlogs) was not anticipated. Poland's solar boom is both a success story and a warning: rapid RE deployment without concurrent grid investment creates integration problems that add cost and delay.
4
The Bełchatów lignite complex — Europe's largest single source of greenhouse gas emissions — will effectively force itself offline by the early 2030s as the Bełchatów lignite deposit nears exhaustion, regardless of government policy. This is an underappreciated fact in Polish energy debates: the forced closure of Bełchatów is a physical certainty, not a political choice. PGE must plan for the closure of 5 GW of baseload generation by ~2033-2036. Nuclear (Pomerania Unit 1 ~2033-35) is being timed partly to replace Bełchatów. If nuclear is delayed, Poland will face a significant generation gap that gas and imports would need to fill.
5
Poland's Baltic offshore wind programme — 5.9 GW by 2030, led by Orlen/Equinor Baltic Power — represents the country's fastest credible path to RE baseload, and Orlen's transformation from oil refiner to renewables developer is one of Central Europe's most significant corporate energy transitions. Orlen absorbed Energa (2020), PGNiG gas company (2022), and PKN Orlen's own refining assets to become Poland's largest company. Its offshore wind joint venture with Equinor gives it a world-class development partner; its acquisition of Energa gave it grid distribution assets; its planned green hydrogen production at refineries uses renewable offshore electricity. Orlen is, in effect, the instrument through which Poland's state will execute its energy transition — making its commercial decisions and governance as consequential as government policy itself.
