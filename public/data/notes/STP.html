Electricity Sector Overview

São Tomé and Príncipe is a small island state (~230,000 people) in the Gulf of Guinea with an electricity system characteristic of its SIDS context: modest total capacity (~40 MW), heavy dependence on imported diesel, a single state utility with financial difficulties, but a relatively high electrification rate (~75%) by Central African standards. The system comprises the main island of São Tomé and the smaller island of Príncipe, each with its own grid. Electricity generation has historically been entirely diesel/HFO, with the lone renewable exception being the Contador HPP (2 MW) — a 50-year-old run-of-river plant that is the country's sole conventional RE generation asset.

EMAE & Market Structure

EMAE (Empresa de Água e Electricidade) — the combined water and electricity utility — is the state national operator responsible for all generation, transmission, and distribution of electricity, as well as water supply. This dual mandate is characteristic of small island states where single utilities manage multiple public services.

  • EMAE: State monopoly; financially stressed by the gap between diesel generation costs and subsidised retail tariffs; grid infrastructure old and loss-prone.
  • Technical losses: Distribution losses are significant; the transmission and distribution network is ageing and poorly maintained; losses worsen EMAE's financial position.
  • SCATEC partnership: The Água Casada solar plant is structured as a lease/PPA with Norwegian IPP SCATEC — a first step toward private generation in STP. SCATEC also doubled Cameroon's Maroua and Guider solar parks in 2024, demonstrating its Africa SIDS/Sahel strategy.
  • World Bank National Energy Compact (2025): A framework for structuring donor-financed energy access and transition support; sets out STP's ambitions for renewable electricity and energy access improvement.
São Tomé and Príncipe's electricity challenge is archetypal of island SIDS: high diesel import dependence, small system scale (which makes economics difficult), a financially stretched utility, and very high solar irradiance that makes the technical case for solar transition compelling. The Água Casada solar plant — if it performs well — will validate the transition thesis and create a template for further RE procurement.

The Solar Transition

STP lies at 0°N latitude in the Gulf of Guinea — among the most consistently sunny locations in the world. The solar resource is exceptional: ~4.5–5.0 kWh/m²/day GHI, year-round. The case for solar transition is straightforward:

  • Economics: Diesel generation costs $0.35–0.50/kWh landed in STP; solar + battery storage now achieves $0.20–0.35/kWh even at small island scale. The economics strongly favour solar replacement of diesel.
  • Energy security: 100% diesel dependency means every power outage is also an import-cost crisis. A diversified solar + battery + hydro portfolio dramatically reduces this vulnerability.
  • Government target: STP has set an ambition of 100% renewable electricity — achievable in principle with solar + battery + micro-hydro + biomass; the Água Casada plant is the first step.
  • IRENA support: The International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA) has provided technical assistance for STP's renewable energy transition planning.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
The Água Casada solar project is STP's most consequential near-term energy development. Displacing diesel with solar at this scale (~25% of daytime São Tomé demand) will reduce EMAE's fuel costs, improve the utility's financial position, and demonstrate that solar IPPs can work in STP's institutional environment. Operational performance over the first 1–2 years will determine whether more projects follow.
2
STP's solar resource is among the best in Central Africa — its diesel dependence is therefore particularly costly. At equatorial latitudes with minimal cloud seasonality, STP has year-round high irradiance. Diesel at $0.40+/kWh when solar is available at $0.25/kWh represents a large and unnecessary premium that the Água Casada plant begins to address.
3
EMAE's dual water-electricity mandate and financial distress are structural sector constraints. As long as EMAE operates at a loss — selling electricity below cost — it cannot fund the network rehabilitation and capacity expansion the sector needs. A tariff reform pathway (ideally with targeted subsidies for low-income households rather than blanket suppression) is a prerequisite for sector financial sustainability.
4
Príncipe Island's energy system is a separate, smaller challenge. With ~8,000 inhabitants, Príncipe's electricity system is tiny and isolated even from São Tomé. Its near-total diesel dependence makes it a strong candidate for a 100% solar + battery off-grid island system — a project that would have both practical energy security value and significant demonstration value for SIDS globally.
5
STP's energy future is inextricably linked to its offshore oil and gas prospects. Unconfirmed offshore hydrocarbon resources in the Joint Development Zone (STP–Nigeria) have been studied for decades without major commercial discoveries. If hydrocarbons were found, they would both complicate the renewable transition narrative and potentially fund grid infrastructure. The more likely scenario — continuing without a major oil windfall — means the renewable transition path is both necessary and sufficient.
