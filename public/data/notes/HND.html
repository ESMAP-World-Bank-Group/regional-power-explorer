Generation Mix & Capacity

Honduras has approximately 3.5 GW of installed capacity with a diversified renewable portfolio: hydro (~25%), solar (~20%), biomass (~9%), wind (~7%) and geothermal (~1%), alongside fossil fuels (~38%) primarily as backup and peaking capacity. Renewable energy has grown dramatically over the last decade through a private IPP programme — Honduras was an early Central American adopter of wind and utility-scale solar. Despite this, the state utility ENEE remains mired in financial crisis, with losses of 34% and debts to private generators exceeding $655 million.

ENEE — A Utility in Crisis

The Empresa Nacional de Energía Eléctrica (ENEE) is Honduras's vertically integrated state utility and the central actor in the country's electricity sector crisis. Three decades of mismanagement have produced a deeply dysfunctional institution:

  • Debt to private generators: ENEE owes ~$655 million (17.4 billion lempiras) to private IPPs — money it cannot pay because tariff revenues are suppressed below cost-recovery levels. This debt is the primary barrier to new private investment in Honduran renewables.
  • Distribution losses: 34.3% system losses — a combination of technical losses (ageing infrastructure) and commercial losses (non-payment, theft). Even after recent improvements, Honduras loses one third of electricity produced before it is billed.
  • IMF programme: Honduras's ENEE crisis is a recurring theme in IMF Article IV consultations; reducing ENEE's fiscal drag is a structural benchmark in the current programme.
  • Privatisation debate: Multiple administrations have considered partial privatisation of ENEE's generation or distribution assets; political resistance has consistently blocked reform.
Honduras's electricity sector presents a stark paradox: one of Central America's most active private renewable energy investment markets alongside one of its most distressed state utilities. ENEE's financial crisis creates a negative feedback loop — it cannot pay IPPs, IPPs hesitate to invest, ENEE cannot improve service, customers don't pay, losses grow. Breaking this cycle requires tariff reform and institutional restructuring that have so far been politically unachievable.

SIEPAC & Regional Position

Honduras is centrally located in the SIEPAC backbone and is structurally a net importer in the MER — buying power principally from Guatemala and, in wet years, Costa Rica. ENEE's payment arrears extend to cross-border trade as well, creating periodic tensions with neighbouring system operators.

  • Honduras is the transit country for power flows between Guatemala (north) and El Salvador, Nicaragua, and beyond — making its grid physically strategic even if commercially strained.
  • The 1.5 GW storage tender (2026) could transform Honduras from a peak importer to a more self-sufficient system if storage enables dispatch of its 850+ MW of existing renewables around the clock.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
ENEE's debt crisis is the single greatest constraint on Honduras's energy transition. The $655M arrears to private generators have frozen new IPP investment precisely when Honduras needs additional capacity. No amount of renewable energy ambition (80% by 2038) matters if ENEE cannot pay for electricity it already contracted. Debt restructuring or sovereign guarantee support is a prerequisite for the sector to function.
2
Honduras has already built significant renewable capacity — the problem is system reliability, not generation portfolio. With solar, wind, biomass, hydro, and geothermal all operational, the generation mix is already over 60% renewable by capacity. The blocking factor is a dysfunctional utility that loses 34% of electricity and cannot fund grid upgrades. Investment in transmission, distribution rehabilitation, and smart metering would deliver more system benefit than incremental generation.
3
The 1.5 GW storage tender (2026) is the most ambitious energy procurement in Central American history. If successfully procured and financed, battery storage at this scale would fundamentally change Honduras's power system — allowing existing solar and wind to provide dispatchable evening and morning power, reducing thermal backup dependency, and potentially turning Honduras from an importer to a balanced position on the MER.
4
Cerro de Hula's success demonstrates Honduras's private renewable energy market potential is real. When Honduras launched IPP auctions in the 2010s, multiple wind, solar, and biomass projects were delivered on time and on budget by private developers. The investment environment can work — what broke it was ENEE's subsequent payment defaults. Restoring payment credibility would unlock rapid re-engagement by developers who proved the model.
5
Honduras's geographic position as the SIEPAC transit hub is underutilised. Sitting between Guatemala and El Salvador, Honduras's grid is the physical backbone of Central American power flows. Upgrading the domestic transmission network — which is ageing and bottlenecked — would increase regional market liquidity, allow Honduras to capture transit revenues, and reduce the cost of regional imbalances that currently require expensive emergency generation.
