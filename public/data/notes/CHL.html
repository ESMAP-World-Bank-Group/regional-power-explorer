Generation Mix & Capacity

Chile operates the most liberalised power market in Latin America and has moved faster than any of its neighbours from coal to solar. The Sistema Eléctrico Nacional (SEN) was created in 2017 by joining the northern mining grid (SING) to the central system (SIC), producing a single market stretching more than 3,000 km along the country.

The Atacama desert has the highest solar irradiance on earth, and developers built into it aggressively once auction prices collapsed after 2015. Solar and wind now supply a large and rising share of generation, and Chile has committed to retiring its coal fleet, with most units already closed or converted to reserve status well ahead of the original 2040 schedule.

The consequence is a textbook transmission problem. Generation is in the north, demand is in Santiago and the south, and the corridor between them saturates in the middle of the day. Chile now routinely records zero or negative marginal prices in the north while paying far more in the centre, with large volumes of solar curtailed. The Kimal–Lo Aguirre HVDC line, a 1,500 km, 3,000 MW link, is the designated fix.

Market Structure & Institutions

  • Ministerio de Energía: Sets policy and the long-term energy strategy, including the coal retirement schedule and the carbon neutrality pathway.
  • CNE: The Comisión Nacional de Energía sets regulated tariffs, calculates node prices and runs the supply auctions for regulated customers.
  • Coordinador Eléctrico Nacional: An independent system and market operator, created in 2016, dispatching on audited variable cost rather than on bids. Chile is therefore cost-based like Brazil, not bid-based, but with far more private contracting.
  • SEC: The Superintendencia de Electricidad y Combustibles enforces service quality and safety, and became politically prominent after prolonged storm outages in the Santiago distribution network.
  • Regulated and free customers: Consumers above 500 kW may choose their supplier; below that they are served by distributors through long-term auctions. Those auctions, held from 2015 onward, are what drove contract prices down and pulled in international renewable developers.
  • Interconnections: Chile is effectively an electrical island. Small links to Argentina in the north exist but are little used, and the long-discussed SINEA corridor toward Peru and the Andean system has not been built.
Chile shows what happens when a market liberalises early and a resource turns out to be world-class. Auction competition delivered some of the cheapest solar contracts ever signed, and the grid could not keep up. The resulting price separation between a saturated north and a tight centre is not a market failure so much as a planning lag, but it has cost developers real revenue and shifted the investment case from generation to transmission and storage.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Transmission, not generation, now determines the pace of the transition. Chile has more solar than it can move. Until Kimal–Lo Aguirre energises, additional northern capacity mostly adds to curtailment rather than to supply, and the economics of new projects depend on where they sit relative to the constraint.
2
Storage has moved from pilot to core infrastructure faster than anywhere in the region. The daily price spread created by solar saturation gives batteries a clear revenue case without a capacity payment, which is why Chile's pipeline dwarfs its neighbours'. This is the most replicable lesson Chile offers the rest of the Southern Cone.
3
The tariff debt turned a technical deferral into a political crisis. Freezing residential prices for five years suppressed a real cost that had to reappear. When it did, in a single sharp adjustment, it damaged public support for the energy transition even though the underlying generation cost had fallen. It is a cautionary case for every country tempted to freeze tariffs.
4
Coal exit has outpaced the firm capacity to replace it. Retiring coal ahead of schedule is a genuine achievement, but it removed dispatchable capacity that solar does not replace at 8pm. Adequacy now rests on gas, hydro, storage and, increasingly, on reserve contracts with units that were supposed to close.
5
Chile's isolation limits what its surplus is worth. A country generating negative-priced solar next to a Peruvian system that burns gas, and an Argentine system that is short in the evening, exports almost nothing. Interconnection would monetise curtailed energy, and its absence is the largest unexploited opportunity in the Andean–Southern Cone corridor.
