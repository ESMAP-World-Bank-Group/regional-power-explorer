Data quality and conflict status warning. Sudan has been in active civil war between the Sudan Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) since April 15, 2023. The electricity sector has been severely damaged. Khartoum — host to the main thermal plants and national grid control centre — was occupied and repeatedly contested by RSF from April 2023. All data below refers to the pre-war baseline (2022 and earlier) unless otherwise noted. Current operational status of specific plants, the national grid, and distribution infrastructure is largely unknown due to restricted humanitarian access and absent utility reporting. All figures should be treated as pre-war baselines only.

1. Energy context

Sudan entered Africa's most destructive active conflict in April 2023 when fighting erupted between the SAF and the RSF — the two armed factions that had jointly ruled Sudan since the 2021 military coup. The electricity sector has suffered catastrophic damage. The UNDP estimated total infrastructure losses — across all sectors — at over USD 3 billion by mid-2024, with the electricity system among the hardest hit. Independent assessments suggest approximately 40% of generation capacity was destroyed, damaged, or disconnected from the grid by end-2024. Satellite imagery and eyewitness reports indicate that by mid-2025 only 2–3 of Sudan's 15 major thermal power plants were reported operational, primarily those in areas outside the main conflict zone.

Prior to the war, Sudan operated a mixed system of roughly 2,800 MW installed capacity (some pre-war estimates placed installed capacity higher, at ~4,500 MW nominal, but effective capacity was already significantly below that). Hydropower — centred on the Merowe Dam (1,250 MW), Roseires (280 MW), and several smaller Nile plants — provided roughly 55–65% of generation. The remainder came from gas and oil-fired thermal plants concentrated in Khartoum. National electrification reached approximately 63.2% (World Bank 2022); rural electrification was far lower, at 30–40%.

Sudan's electricity sector was already under severe financial stress before the war: the Sudan Electricity Holding Company (SEHC) and its subsidiaries were chronically underfunded, retail tariffs were below cost recovery, and the system suffered from high losses and poor maintenance. The war has essentially destroyed what institutional and physical capacity existed. Reconstruction will require massive financing, political stabilisation, and years of work.

  • Merowe Dam (1,250 MW): Commissioned 2009 on the Fourth Cataract of the Nile; 10 × 125 MW Francis turbines. Sudan's largest power plant, contributing ~50% of pre-war hydropower output.   • Roseires Dam (280 MW, expanded): Blue Nile, near the Ethiopian border. Originally 280 MW (raised in 2013 to 560 MW, but turbines may not all have been installed — figures vary). Upstream of Khartoum; less directly affected by fighting in central Sudan.
  • Other Nile hydro (~130–200 MW): Sennar Dam (15 MW), Jebel Aulia (30 MW, near Khartoum — in contested area), Upper Atbara + Setit complex (320 MW), Atbara (81 MW). The Upper Atbara/Setit complex was China Gezhouba Group EPC, commissioned 2017–2019.
  • Thermal — Khartoum complex: Gas and oil-fired plants in Khartoum North and across the capital region (previously the economic and political heart of Sudan). Reported near-total shutdown since April 2023 as RSF occupied Khartoum. Plants include Khartoum North 1 (300 MW), Garri 1–4, Bahri, and others; total pre-war thermal capacity ~1,000–1,200 MW. Status: largely non-operational.
  • Small-scale renewables: Minimal at pre-war baseline; some small solar installations in rural areas under SREP (Scaling-up Renewable Energy Programme). Pre-war renewable share was less than 1%.
Note on war damage (as reported mid-2025): The RSF's January 2025 drone attack on Merowe Dam targeted Sudan's single most important power plant. Satellite imagery and local reports indicated damage to electrical infrastructure and potential turbine hall impact. SAF recaptured Khartoum in late April 2025, but thermal plant damage was extensive. Full damage assessment across the national electricity system has not been conducted by any international body as of mid-2026 due to ongoing insecurity.

Sudan's pre-war demand growth was moderate at ~3–4%/year, constrained by electricity access limits (63% national), high tariffs relative to incomes, and unreliable supply. Peak demand was estimated at ~3,000–3,500 MW, already exceeding effective supply and creating rotational load shedding pre-war. Industrial demand was primarily driven by Khartoum manufacturing and oil-related industry; agriculture (irrigation pumps along the Nile) was a significant rural load. Since April 2023, effective demand has collapsed along with supply — millions of Sudanese have been displaced and large parts of Khartoum are uninhabited.

3. Sector organisation and actors (pre-war structure)

3.1 Market structure

Sudan operated a vertically integrated state-owned electricity sector under the Sudan Electricity Holding Company (SEHC), which owned and operated generation (Sudan Electricity Generation and Transmission Company, SEGTCO) and distribution (multiple regional distribution companies). Sector regulation was provided by the Electricity and Cogeneration Regulatory Authority (ECRA). A 2015 sector reform law envisaged unbundling and IPP entry, but limited progress was made before the war. The sector's financial position was severely compromised by below-cost-recovery tariffs, high losses, and delayed government payments for public sector consumption.

4. Key issues and dynamics

4.1 War damage assessment — what is known

  • Khartoum thermal plants: The capital's main thermal generation was located in Khartoum North and adjacent areas — the front line of the most intense urban fighting from April 2023 through at least mid-2025. These plants were non-operational for the vast majority of this period. SAF recapture of Khartoum in late April 2025 revealed extensive physical damage to industrial and infrastructure assets.
  • Merowe Dam drone attack (January 2025): RSF drone strikes targeted the Merowe hydropower station — the most consequential single piece of electricity infrastructure in Sudan. Reports from Sudanese engineers and satellite imagery suggested damage to the electrical switchyard and transmission infrastructure; turbine damage is disputed. Even partial operational loss at Merowe removes Sudan's single largest generation resource.
  • Distribution infrastructure: Urban distribution networks in Khartoum, Omdurman, and other contested cities have been extensively looted and physically damaged. Transformers, cables, and metering equipment have reportedly been stripped for scrap. Restoration will require replacement of entire distribution system segments.
  • Total damage estimate: UNDP estimated total infrastructure damage across all sectors at USD 3+ billion by mid-2024. Electricity-specific damage has not been formally quantified but is likely USD 1–2 billion in replacement value for generating units and transmission assets alone, excluding distribution.

4.2 Pre-war sector vulnerabilities

  • Even before the war, Sudan's electricity sector was characterised by: a 63% electrification rate masking severe rural access gaps (20–30%); chronic load shedding of 6–12 hours/day in urban areas due to supply shortfall; SEHC's financial insolvency (tariffs below cost recovery, high government payment arrears); and heavy Chinese financing of Merowe and Upper Atbara/Setit complexes with repayment obligations.
  • The war has swept away whatever institutional progress existed. Reconstruction will not restore the pre-war baseline — it will require building a de facto new sector in a contested political environment.

4.3 Post-conflict reconstruction — donor and political landscape

  • Any reconstruction scenario requires political stabilisation and international recognition of a governing authority. As of mid-2026, the war is ongoing with no agreed ceasefire. The AU, IGAD, and US-led mediation (Jeddah process) have not produced a durable agreement.
  • The IMF and World Bank cannot provide new lending to a country in active conflict without a recognised civilian government. Gulf states (Saudi Arabia, UAE) are the potential bilateral financiers — but the UAE's alignment with RSF during the conflict complicates any unified reconstruction effort. Egypt (which borders Sudan and has the most direct energy interconnection interest) has maintained relations with SAF.
  • GERD's completion and operation creates a long-run opportunity: Ethiopia could theoretically supply Sudan with clean electricity via the Ethiopia-Sudan interconnection, replacing the Khartoum thermal plants that are now largely non-operational. This is a pre-conditions scenario — it requires peace first.

5. Five things to take away

1
Sudan's electricity sector has been catastrophically damaged by the civil war that began in April 2023. An estimated 40% of capacity is destroyed or non-operational; Khartoum's thermal plants were in the middle of the most intense fighting; and Merowe Dam — Sudan's largest power plant — was attacked by drone strikes in January 2025. The current operational state of the electricity system is largely unknown. All data in this brief refers to the pre-war 2022 baseline.
2
Even before the war, Sudan's electricity system was severely stressed: 63% electrification, chronic 6–12 hour daily blackouts, and an insolvent state utility (SEHC) running below cost recovery with Chinese loan obligations on Merowe and Upper Atbara/Setit. The war has destroyed the institutional and physical infrastructure that was built over the previous 15 years. Reconstruction will not be a repair — it will be a rebuild.
3
Merowe Dam (1,250 MW) is the single most important electricity asset in Sudan — it accounted for roughly 45% of pre-war generation. Its attack in January 2025 and uncertain damage status is the defining near-term electricity risk for any eventual post-conflict recovery. If the turbines are extensively damaged, replacing them would take 3–5 years and significant financing even in a stable political environment.
4
Any serious reconstruction financing (World Bank, IMF, AfDB) requires a recognised civilian government, a functioning finance ministry, and at minimum a humanitarian ceasefire. None of these conditions exist as of mid-2026. Gulf state bilateral financing is possible but depends on post-war political settlement, given the UAE's backing of RSF during the conflict. The electricity sector is a post-conflict reconstruction problem, not a development financing problem — for now.
5
Sudan's Nile hydrology is directly affected by GERD operations. Merowe and Roseires dam outputs depend on Nile flow; under contested GERD filling scenarios, reduced flow could further reduce hydro generation in an already destroyed system. Post-conflict, Sudan will need to navigate both reconstruction and the GERD water-sharing framework — the two most consequential determinants of its long-run electricity supply.
