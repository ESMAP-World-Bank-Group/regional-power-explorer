1. Energy context

Togo is one of WAPP's most import-dependent countries: with domestic generation of only 0.71 TWh against a national demand of 2.25 TWh in 2023, approximately 68% of Togo's electricity consumption is imported — primarily from Ghana's VRA/GRIDCo via the shared CEET/VRA electricity supply agreement, and partly through the Communauté Électrique du Bénin (CEB) which also manages electricity trade with neighbouring Benin. This high import dependency makes Togo extremely vulnerable to supply disruptions in Ghana — drought-induced generation shortfalls at Akosombo, financial disputes, or grid emergencies on the Ghanaian side translate immediately into load-shedding in Lomé.

Despite this vulnerability, Togo has made significant progress in electrification (~70% in 2024), surpassing many West African peers, and has built an increasingly active private sector investment pipeline in energy. The government's CIZO and SE4ALL-aligned programmes have expanded off-grid solar access significantly. The CEET utility, while still financially stressed, benefits from stronger political governance than some regional peers.

  • Gas/HFO (~73% of domestic generation): ContourGlobal Togo (previously Contour Global, now part of TotalEnergies' portfolio) operates a 92 MW HFO plant at Lomé that is Togo's main thermal generator. Additional capacity includes the Kekeli Efficient Power plant (65 MW gas/HFO, near Lomé) and smaller CEET-owned diesel units. The Kekeli FSRU LNG-to-power project (100 MW) has been in development, with CEET as offtaker.
  • Solar (~15% of domestic generation): Several solar plants in service — Blitta Solar (25 MWp, commissioned 2021, Scatec/CEET) — and a growing off-grid solar programme under the CIZO project (200,000 households targeted). Togo has one of West Africa's more ambitious solar agendas relative to its size, with targets of 500 MW RE by 2030 under the PNER-DRE programme.
  • Hydro (~11% of domestic generation): The Nangbeto dam (66 MW, on the Mono River, 180 km north of Lomé) is managed by the Communauté Électrique du Bénin (CEB) — a binational entity co-owned by Togo and Benin. Togo and Benin share Nangbeto output. A second Mono River dam at Adjarala (147 MW, Togo/Benin, CEB) has been delayed; FID and financing are pending.

3. Sector organisation

CEET — Compagnie Énergie Électrique du Togo State Utility

CEET (51% state, 49% EDF/Eranove since 2000, later majority returned to state by 2011) is the vertically-integrated electricity utility responsible for generation, bulk transmission, and distribution. CEET manages electricity import contracts with VRA (Ghana) and CEB and serves about 600,000+ connected customers. CEET's financial position is structurally stressed: import costs are the dominant expenditure, tariffs cover only ~70% of costs, and the government provides operating subsidies. CEET is technically more capable than some WAPP peers, partly due to the prior EDF/Eranove management period.

ARSE — Electricity Sector Regulator Regulator

The Autorité de Réglementation du Secteur de l'Electricité (ARSE) was established in 2000 as Togo's independent electricity regulator. ARSE oversees tariff applications, licenses new generators, and mediates utility-consumer disputes. Togo's electricity tariff (XOF 122.96/kWh, ~USD 0.22) is among the higher tariffs in West Africa, reflecting the high cost of imported power and domestic HFO generation.

CEB — Communauté Électrique du Bénin Binational

CEB is a binational institution owned jointly by Togo and Benin, responsible for the Nangbeto hydropower plant, associated transmission, and bulk electricity procurement from CIV and Ghana for resale to CEET (Togo) and SBEE (Benin). CEB manages the electricity supply agreements at the wholesale level; CEET and SBEE manage retail distribution in their respective countries. CEB has been pursuing the Adjarala hydro project (147 MW, second Mono River dam) for over a decade — financing and ESIA approvals have been challenging.

4. Key issues and challenges

  • Ghana supply vulnerability — structural import dependence: Togo's 68% import dependence on Ghana creates an inherent vulnerability: when Akosombo's reservoir drops (as in 2023), when Ghana's own load-shedding emergency takes priority, or when commercial disputes arise, Togo's supply collapses. The North Core 330 kV project is designed to provide redundant sourcing from Nigeria, but completion is several years away and Nigeria's own supply situation is uncertain.
  • Tariff among the highest in WAPP: At XOF 122.96/kWh (~USD 0.22), Togo has one of the highest electricity tariffs in West Africa. This reflects the high cost of both imported power (VRA charges) and domestic HFO generation, but it limits affordability for residential and SME customers and depresses productive electricity use. Despite high tariffs, CEET still runs a deficit due to collection efficiency issues and operational costs.
  • North Core progress and the Nigeria option: The North Core 330 kV interconnection (Nigeria–Benin–Togo–Ghana) would give Togo access to Nigerian electricity for the first time, potentially at lower prices than current Ghanaian imports. The Togo 330 kV section is under construction (AfDB-supported). Full commissioning would allow CEET to diversify its import portfolio — though Nigeria's domestic supply shortfall limits how much can realistically be exported.
  • Solar CIZO programme — regional benchmark: Togo's CIZO off-grid solar programme, targeting 300,000 rural households through solar home systems, has been one of the more successful national off-grid programmes in West Africa, with strong government commitment and results-based financing from the EU and ECOWAS. CIZO has been cited as a model for last-mile electrification in contexts where grid extension is too expensive.

5. Five key takeaways

1
Togo's 68% import dependence on Ghana is the most acute energy security vulnerability in the WAPP zone relative to country size: when Ghana manages its own supply crisis (2023 Akosombo drought), Togo immediately experiences severe load-shedding with no backup supply at comparable scale.
2
The North Core 330 kV project is Togo's most important energy infrastructure investment: it would provide a second major import source (Nigeria/Benin) and transform the country from a captive consumer of Ghana's surplus to a participant in a diversified regional market.
3
The Nangbeto/CEB framework — a binational hydro asset managed by a shared institution — is a model for WAPP shared infrastructure governance that has operated continuously for 35+ years, though the Adjarala second dam has been chronically delayed for over a decade, illustrating the difficulty of expanding shared infrastructure beyond its original design.
4
Togo's CIZO off-grid solar programme has connected 300,000+ rural households at a unit cost and speed that grid extension cannot match — a compelling case that off-grid solar and grid extension are complementary rather than competing strategies for the last mile.
5
With tariffs of USD 0.22/kWh — the highest in the WAPP core zone — Togo faces a paradox: it pays premium prices for imported power because it lacks domestic capacity, but high tariffs discourage the demand growth that would justify domestic investment, trapping the sector in a high-cost equilibrium.
