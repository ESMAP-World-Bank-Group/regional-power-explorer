Generation Mix & Capacity

Ecuador built its way out of blackouts in the 2010s and back into them in the 2020s. A large public investment programme added roughly 4 GW of hydropower, centred on Coca Codó Sinclair (1,500 MW), and raised the hydro share of generation to around 75 to 80%, displacing expensive oil-fired plants and briefly making Ecuador an exporter.

The concentration proved fragile. The new plants sit on the Amazonian slope, where flows share a common seasonality, so a dry season hits most of the fleet at once. In late 2023 and 2024, severe drought combined with deferred maintenance on thermal backup and an inability to import enough from Colombia produced scheduled national load shedding lasting many hours a day for months.

Coca Codó Sinclair has also been dogged by technical problems, including thousands of cracks in its distributors and severe regressive erosion of the Coca River that destroyed the San Rafael waterfall and threatens the intake works. The combination of hydrological, technical and fiscal stress has made emergency thermal leasing and barge generation a recurring feature.

Market Structure & Institutions

  • Ministerio de Energía y Minas: Sets policy and, through the state holding structure, controls most generation and distribution assets.
  • CENACE: Operates the national interconnected system and administers transactions, including international exchanges with Colombia and Peru.
  • ARCERNNR: The regulator for electricity and non-renewable natural resources, responsible for tariffs and technical norms.
  • Predominantly public sector: After the 2015 sector law, generation, transmission through CELEC and distribution through CNEL are largely state-owned. Private participation is limited, which ties investment capacity to the fiscal position.
  • Tariffs below cost: Regulated tariffs do not recover full cost, and the gap is covered by the state. That has constrained maintenance spending, which was a direct contributor to the thermal unavailability during the rationing crisis.
  • Interconnections: Strong links to Colombia at 230 kV, and a weak link to Peru that operates in isolated mode. Ecuador's import dependence runs almost entirely through one border.
Ecuador is the clearest recent demonstration that adding renewable capacity is not the same as adding energy security. A fleet that is 80% hydro and hydrologically correlated, with underfunded thermal backup and a single import partner facing the same drought, has no diversification at all. The 2024 blackouts were a portfolio failure rather than a capacity shortfall.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Hydrological correlation, not hydro share, caused the crisis. Plants concentrated on the same Amazonian slope fail together. Diversification across basins, or across technologies, would have prevented most of the rationing, and neither was in place.
2
Tariffs below cost eventually show up as unserved energy. Subsidised prices suppressed revenue, revenue shortfalls suppressed maintenance, and unmaintained thermal units were unavailable when hydro failed. The causal chain from tariff policy to load shedding is unusually direct here.
3
Import dependence on a single correlated neighbour is not a hedge. Colombia was Ecuador's insurance policy and was simultaneously in drought. A functioning Peru interconnection would have provided genuine diversification, and its absence turned a manageable shortfall into a national crisis.
4
Coca Codó Sinclair carries asset-specific risk on top of hydrological risk. The distributor cracking and the upstream erosion are engineering problems on the single largest plant in the country. Any derating there removes roughly a sixth of national capacity.
5
Solar and wind are almost absent despite being the obvious diversifier. Ecuador has under 200 MW of variable renewables. Even a modest solar build would be counter-correlated with the dry season and would reduce diesel imports, which makes its near-total absence the most striking gap in the country's portfolio.
