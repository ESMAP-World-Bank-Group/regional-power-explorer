Burkina Faso experienced two military coups within eight months: Lieutenant-Colonel Paul-Henri Sandaogo Damiba overthrew Roch Kaboré in January 2022, and was himself removed by Captain Ibrahim Traoré in September 2022. The Traoré-led MPSR junta expelled French Barkhane forces in 2023, terminated the MINUSMA cooperation agreement, and has deployed Russian Wagner/Africa Corps forces. Jihadist insurgency (JNIM, affiliated GSIM/Al-Qaeda) controls or contests large swaths of the north and east, with attacks on critical infrastructure including electricity pylons, fuel depots, and mine sites. Data reliability is significantly reduced; figures are estimates with meaningful uncertainty.

1. Energy context

Burkina Faso is one of West Africa's most electricity-deficient countries, with only 42% electrification and a domestic generation capacity of ~340 MW that covers less than half of national demand. The remainder — estimated at 45–50% of total consumption — is imported from Côte d'Ivoire via 225 kV interconnections, making the country deeply dependent on a single external supplier. Gold mining (Burkina is a major African producer) is a key industrial load, but most mines operate on self-generated diesel or HFO power as the national grid does not reach them reliably.

The sector is managed by SONABEL (Société Nationale Burkinabè d'Électricité), a state-owned, vertically-integrated utility operating under severe financial and operational constraints. Rural electrification is managed by ABERME, using mini-grid concessions for remote areas. The security crisis has effectively halted rural electrification expansion in northern and eastern regions and has created new demands on an already-stressed system.

  • Oil/diesel (~80% of domestic generation): SONABEL operates HFO thermal plants in Ouagadougou (Komsilga, combined 130+ MW) and diesel units in regional cities (Bobo-Dioulasso, Koudougou, Fada N'Gourma). Fuel supply is difficult due to landlocked geography — fuel arrives by truck from coastal ports (Lomé, Tema, Abidjan) and the supply chain has been disrupted by jihadist attacks on road corridors.
  • Solar (~17% of domestic generation): Zagtouli solar PV (33 MW, Ouagadougou, commissioned 2017, a €26M EU-AfDB project) is sub-Saharan Africa's largest standalone solar plant when built. Several smaller systems in regional towns and mining sites add another 20–30 MW. Solar is the most scalable clean addition: the Sahel solar resource is exceptional (>5.5 kWh/m²/day).
  • Hydropower (~3%): Bagré dam (16 MW, on the Nakambé river, 150 km SE of Ouagadougou) and Kompienga dam (14.5 MW, 225 km SE) are the only significant hydro assets. Both operate in contested security zones and their output is highly variable with rainfall. Tourni (2 MW) is a micro-hydro unit on the Comoé river.

3. Sector organisation

SONABEL — Société Nationale Burkinabè d'Électricité Financially Stressed SOE

SONABEL is a 100% state-owned, vertically-integrated utility responsible for generation, transmission, and urban distribution. Like many of its WAPP peers, SONABEL operates at a structural deficit: tariffs are below cost recovery, fuel costs are high and rising (given landlocked supply chain costs and security disruptions), and industrial customer self-generation has reduced the revenue base. Government subsidies and transfers are SONABEL's primary financial lifeline. SONABEL manages the electricity import contracts with CIE (CIV) and GRIDCo (Ghana).

ABERME — Rural Electrification Agency Off-grid / Rural

The Agence Burkinabè de l'Électrification en Milieu Rural (ABERME) manages off-grid rural electrification through solar mini-grid concessions and individual solar system programmes. Security conditions in the north and east have suspended most ABERME activities in those regions. The solar hybrid mini-grid model has worked well in politically stable zones (south, southwest), with several dozen localities connected through 10–50 kW solar-diesel systems.

4. Key issues and challenges

  • Security crisis and supply chain disruption: Jihadist control of northern and eastern corridors has disrupted fuel supply chains (diesel truck convoys require military escort), isolated communities from the main grid, and rendered rural electrification expansion effectively impossible in ~40% of the national territory. Several gold mines have paused or relocated operations due to security, eliminating key industrial electricity customers.
  • CIV import dependency — single point of failure: Burkina Faso imports ~45–50% of its electricity from Côte d'Ivoire through two 225 kV lines. This dependency is extreme: a CIV drought year, a line security incident, or a commercial dispute could immediately cause massive load-shedding in Ouagadougou. No redundant import source of comparable size exists, and domestic generation cannot cover national demand.
  • Mining sector self-generation: Several major gold mines (Semafo/Endeavour, Houndé, Karma, Bissa Gold) have shifted to dedicated HFO/diesel self-generation or solar-diesel hybrids because SONABEL grid supply is unreliable. This concentration of creditworthy demand in the self-generation segment represents a major revenue loss for SONABEL and reduces the economics of grid expansion toward mining zones.
  • Solar scale-up opportunity: Burkina Faso has exceptional solar irradiance (Zagtouli was successful), and solar is the most realistic path to both reducing import dependency and extending access in areas the grid cannot reach under current security conditions. Several IPP solar projects are in development or MOU stage, but financial close requires government guarantees and stable PPA enforcement that the current junta environment makes uncertain for foreign investors.

5. Five key takeaways

1
Burkina Faso is arguably the WAPP member most exposed to compounding crises: security (jihadist insurgency in 40%+ of territory), political instability (two coups in 2022), energy import dependency (45% from CIV), and climate vulnerability (Sahel drought) — any one of which would be a major challenge; together they constitute a structural energy emergency.
2
CIV import dependency is Burkina Faso's most acute energy security risk: a CIV drought year or a single security incident on the Ferkéssédougou–Ouagadougou corridor can cause national supply collapse, yet there is no credible alternative at comparable scale or cost.
3
Zagtouli (33 MW, 2017) proved that large-scale solar is technically viable in the Sahel and commercially bankable with EU/AfDB support — scaling this model (targeting 200+ MW) is the most feasible domestic generation expansion path that does not depend on fuel supply chains or river hydrology.
4
Gold mining self-generation is a double structural problem: it removes the sector's most creditworthy customers from SONABEL's revenue base AND it perpetuates expensive HFO consumption for mines that could be supplied cheaply from a reliable grid — breaking this equilibrium requires grid reliability that is currently impossible.
5
Under current conditions, rural electrification in conflict zones is not a utility planning challenge but a security and humanitarian one — the ~40% of territory under jihadist influence or active contestation is effectively inaccessible to any grid expansion, mini-grid deployment, or meaningful energy policy, until the security situation changes.
