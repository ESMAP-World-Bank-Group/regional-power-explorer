Generation Mix & Sector Overview

The Czech Republic has one of Central Europe's most distinctive electricity mixes: a near-equal split between nuclear power (~40%) and coal (~35%), with solar, gas, biomass, and minimal wind making up the remainder. The country has no significant wind energy due to limited resources and historically restrictive permitting — yet it is among Europe's few consistent net electricity exporters, exporting 15–20 TWh/year to Germany, Austria, Slovakia, and Poland. The dominant utility is ČEZ (Czech Energy Works), a state-70%-owned conglomerate that operates both nuclear plants, most coal plants, and is expanding renewables internationally. The TSO is ČEPS. Czech energy policy has been consistently pro-nuclear and the Czech Republic is now the EU's leading advocate for nuclear's role in the clean energy taxonomy.

Nuclear Expansion and the EU Taxonomy Debate

The Czech Republic has been the EU's most vocal nuclear advocate in Brussels, and its political investment in nuclear expansion has shaped EU energy law in ways that matter far beyond Czech borders:

  • EU Taxonomy inclusion: The Czech Republic, together with France, lobbied hard for nuclear to be included in the EU Sustainable Finance Taxonomy as a "transitional" sustainable activity. The taxonomy delegated act including nuclear was adopted in July 2022, over the objections of Germany, Luxembourg, and Austria. This decision allows ČEZ and other nuclear operators to issue green bonds backed by nuclear assets — reducing their financing costs by 50-100 basis points.
  • Loop flows and Central European congestion: Czech transmission (ČEPS) sits at a critical Central European crossroads. German RE-driven loop flows (unscheduled power flows from Germany's north coast to Austria and Italy via the Czech grid) have been a persistent grid management problem. ČEPS operates Phase Shifting Transformers (PSTs) at the German border to limit uncontrolled power flows — a technical solution that nonetheless reflects the absence of adequate EU-wide congestion management.
  • Czech nuclear export strategy: The Czech Republic is exploring becoming a nuclear technology services exporter, building on ČEZ's experience. Cooperation with KHNP on Dukovany opens potential for Czech industrial participation in Polish, Slovak, and Romanian nuclear projects — creating an industrial value chain for Czech nuclear engineering firms beyond the domestic fleet.
The Czech Republic's selection of KHNP for Dukovany 5 is one of the most consequential European nuclear procurement decisions in a generation — not because of the single unit, but because it opens the door to KHNP supplying Temelín 3&4 and potentially Polish and Slovak projects. If KHNP delivers Dukovany 5 on time and on budget (a significant if, given Western nuclear track records), Korea becomes a dominant nuclear vendor in Central Europe, replacing Russia's historical monopoly. If KHNP delays or overruns, it hands the argument back to Westinghouse and EDF — and sets back nuclear expansion across the region.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
The Czech Republic's KHNP selection for Dukovany 5 is a geopolitical as much as a commercial decision — it reflects a deliberate policy of replacing Russian nuclear influence (Soviet-era VVER technology) with Korean nuclear technology, while avoiding the delays and cost overruns associated with EDF's EPR and Westinghouse's AP1000 in Western Europe. All three VVER units at Dukovany and Temelín are Soviet/Russian designed; their long-term operation involves Russian fuel (TVEL), parts, and maintenance. Westinghouse won approval to supply alternative fuel for Czech VVERs in 2024 — diversifying fuel supply. KHNP for the new unit completes the technology transition from Russian to Western/Korean supply chains.
2
Czech wind policy is the clearest example in the EU of how sub-national permitting discretion can neutralise national energy targets — and the Czech government's failure to override municipal wind vetoes for 15 years has left the country structurally dependent on coal for RE gap-filling. With only ~400 MW of wind for a 22 GW system, the Czech Republic has no variable renewable flexibility buffer. Every MW of nuclear or coal that goes offline must be replaced by gas or imports. More onshore wind (even 3-4 GW) would dramatically improve Czech grid resilience and reduce coal's necessity as balancing generation. The 2024 EIA reform is a step toward unlocking wind permitting — but results will take 5+ years to materialise in capacity.
3
ČEZ is one of Europe's most strategically important energy companies, yet its 70% state ownership creates tension between government priorities (cheap, secure electricity for Czech consumers) and commercial objectives (RE expansion, green finance, international growth) — a tension that will intensify as coal retirement and nuclear expansion require simultaneous multi-billion-euro investments. The Czech state has historically used ČEZ as an instrument of industrial policy (keeping electricity prices low, delaying coal closures for social reasons). As Dukovany 5 construction begins (€7-9B capital commitment), the state's financing burden via ČEZ will become the central constraint on Czech energy policy execution.
4
The Czech Republic's status as a consistent net electricity exporter (~15-20 TWh/year) is a structural competitive advantage that is at risk from the country's own energy transition timeline — if Dukovany units begin closing before Dukovany 5 comes online, the Czech Republic risks becoming a net importer for the first time in decades. Dukovany's four VVER-440 units are all operational through 2045+ with planned life extensions — but extensions require regulatory approval and investment. If any two units close earlier than planned (regulatory pressure, technical issues), ~1,000 MW of baseload disappears. The "nuclear gap" between old units closing and new units opening is Czech energy policy's critical path problem for the 2030s.
5
The Czech Republic's 2033 coal phase-out target — revised under EU pressure from 2038 — is ambitious but achievable provided Dukovany 5 construction stays on schedule and onshore wind permitting reform produces at least 2-3 GW by 2033. If either nuclear slips (2036 is already optimistic by European nuclear standards) or wind remains blocked, the coal phase-out will be delayed de facto even if the political commitment holds. The Czech energy ministry's NECP modelling shows the transition is feasible with KHNP's timeline — but it is the narrowest credible scenario, with no slack for construction delay or permitting failure. European Commission oversight of Czech NECP delivery will be the key governance mechanism.
