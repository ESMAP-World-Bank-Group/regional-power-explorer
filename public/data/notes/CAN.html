Generation Mix & Capacity

Canada generates roughly 60% of its electricity from hydropower and about 15% from nuclear, giving it one of the cleanest large power systems in the world at over 80% non-emitting. That position was inherited from twentieth century hydro development rather than bought with recent policy, which shapes the whole sector: the decarbonisation problem here is not the existing fleet but the cost of the next increment of firm clean capacity.

Electricity is a provincial responsibility. There is no national utility, no national market and no national grid operator. Each province built its own system around its own resource, so Québec, British Columbia, Manitoba and Newfoundland are hydro systems, Ontario is hydro and nuclear, and Alberta and Saskatchewan are gas and coal systems in transition. Federal authority reaches the sector mainly through carbon pricing, environmental regulation, and permitting of interprovincial and international lines.

The trade pattern follows that history. Interprovincial transfer capability is thin, but north to south links into the United States are strong, and Canada is a consistent net exporter of around 50 TWh a year, overwhelmingly from Québec, British Columbia, Ontario and Manitoba. Hydro reservoirs make those exports flexible, which is worth more to New England and New York as their own variable renewable share rises.

Its export contracts with New England and New York, and the renegotiation of the Churchill Falls arrangement with Newfoundland, are the defining commercial questions in Canadian power
Ontario: nuclear refurbishmentBruce and Darlington are being refurbished unit by unit through the 2030s, and Darlington hosts the first grid-scale SMR under construction in the G7. Ontario is also the only province to have fully phased out coal, which it did by 2014
Alberta: the one liberalised marketAn energy-only wholesale market with no capacity mechanism, now coal-free ahead of schedule after conversions to gas. It carries the fastest renewable build in the country alongside the most contested provincial siting policy
East to west transfer is weakProvinces are far better connected to American neighbours than to each other. Proposals for an Atlantic Loop, or for stronger Manitoba to Saskatchewan and Québec to Ontario links, recur and stall on cost allocation between provinces with very different resource endowments
Clean Electricity RegulationsFederal rules pushing the grid toward net zero emissions by 2035, softened in consultation to accommodate gas with abatement and peaking flexibility. Market Structure & Institutions

  • Provincial jurisdiction: Provinces own and regulate their utilities and set resource policy. Most operate vertically integrated Crown corporations: Hydro-Québec, BC Hydro, Manitoba Hydro, SaskPower, NB Power and Nalcor.
  • Ontario: The IESO runs a wholesale market and centralised procurement, Ontario Power Generation owns the nuclear and large hydro fleet, and Hydro One owns transmission. It is a hybrid of market and state planning rather than either one.
  • Alberta: The AESO operates the only genuine energy-only competitive market, with a price cap, no capacity mechanism, and retail competition. Market design reform and restrictions on renewable siting are both live.
  • Canada Energy Regulator: Federal oversight of interprovincial and international power lines and of export permits. It is a permitting authority, not a market regulator.
  • Carbon pricing: The federal output-based pricing system applies to generators in provinces without an equivalent, and is the main economic driver against unabated gas. Its durability has become a political question.
  • NERC and NPCC: Canadian systems sit inside the North American reliability framework and follow NERC standards through provincial adoption, which is what makes the dense cross-border interconnection operationally straightforward.
Canada's advantage is a clean, dispatchable hydro fleet; its constraint is that the fleet is provincial. The cheapest continental decarbonisation option would pair Québec, Manitoba and British Columbia reservoirs with wind and solar across a much wider area, but almost every route for doing that crosses either a provincial boundary with no line or an international one with a better line. Whether Canada builds east to west or keeps selling north to south is the question that decides its role in the North American transition.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
The clean baseline is inherited. Over 80% non-emitting generation came from mid-century hydro investment. It gives Canada a head start on decarbonisation and very little recent institutional practice at building new firm clean capacity.
2
Growth, not replacement, is the challenge. Electrification of heat, transport and industry implies roughly a doubling of generation by 2050 on most official scenarios, a build programme without modern precedent in the country.
3
Interprovincial transmission is the missing market. Every serious least-cost study finds value in east to west transfer. None has yet found a cost allocation that provinces accept.
4
Exports are a strategic asset. Flexible hydro exported into markets with rising variable renewable shares earns a rising premium. It also ties Canadian revenue to American market design decisions.
5
Federal-provincial friction is structural. Carbon pricing and the Clean Electricity Regulations meet provincial ownership of the sector. Expect continued negotiation rather than resolution.
