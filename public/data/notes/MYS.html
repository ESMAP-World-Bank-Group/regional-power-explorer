1. Energy context

Malaysia operates three geographically separated electricity systems: Peninsular Malaysia (TNB, ~24 GW, ~75% of national demand), Sabah (SESB, ~3 GW) on the northern coast of Borneo, and Sarawak (SEB / Sarawak Energy, ~9 GW) on the rest of Malaysian Borneo. These systems have historically operated with minimal interconnection — Peninsular Malaysia is connected to Thailand (north) and Singapore (south) but not to Sabah or Sarawak. The January 2026 energisation of the Sarawak–Sabah grid interconnection (50 MW initial capacity) marks a historic step toward unifying Borneo's Malaysian grid.

Malaysia's generation mix has historically been gas and coal dominated (~75% combined), with a large-scale hydro contribution from Sarawak (~16% of national capacity from Bakun, Murum, Batang Ai, and other Sarawak dams). The sector's defining transition document is the National Energy Transition Roadmap (NETR 2023), launched by Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim in August 2023: it targets 70% renewable energy by 2050, 70% installed RE capacity by 2035, and sets out a Just Transition framework linking electrification with economic development in Sabah and Sarawak.

Malaysia is strategically important in the regional power grid as the transit country for the LTMS-PIP (Laos–Thailand–Malaysia–Singapore) pilot and as a potential exporter of Sarawak RE to Singapore. The Baleh hydropower dam (1,285 MW, under construction in Sarawak) and several utility-scale solar auctions (LSS4, LSS5 programmes) are the largest near-term capacity additions.

  • Gas (34% capacity / 45% generation): The dominant fuel in Peninsular Malaysia; ~12.2 GW of gas-fired capacity (CCGTs and open-cycle). Malaysian gas is sourced from domestic offshore fields (Petronas concessions, South China Sea) supplied via the Peninsular Gas Utilisation (PGU) pipeline. Gas capacity factors exceed coal in Peninsular Malaysia due to more favourable dispatch economics. LNG exports continue from the Bintulu terminal (Sarawak), though domestic gas allocation for power is prioritised. TNB holds long-term fuel supply agreements with Petronas.
  • Coal (24% capacity / 30% generation): Coal-fired IPPs in Peninsular Malaysia (Port Dickson, Jimah, Manjung, Tanjung Bin) and Sabah. All coal is imported (mostly from Australia, Indonesia). The NETR 2023 does not set a firm coal phase-out date but implies substantial coal displacement by 2035 and full retirement by 2050. LSS (Large Scale Solar) auctions are the primary vehicle for coal displacement.
  • Hydro (17% capacity / 12% generation): Dominated by large Sarawak dams: Bakun (2,400 MW, operational 2011, SEB-owned), Murum (944 MW, operational 2015), and Batang Ai (108 MW). The Baleh dam (1,285 MW) is under construction in Sarawak (target commissioning ~2026), which will make it the largest power plant in Malaysia. Peninsular Malaysia has smaller run-of-river hydro (~2 GW). Sarawak's large hydro is the most valuable RE asset in Malaysia; exporting it to Peninsular Malaysia requires HVDC or to Singapore via subsea cable.
  • Solar (10% capacity / 4% generation): ~3.6 GW installed by 2023, growing via the LSS programme (LSS4 awarded ~2 GW, LSS5 pipeline). Concentrated in Peninsular Malaysia. Solar capacity factors (~16–18% in Malaysia) are lower than in drier ASEAN countries, limiting generation share relative to capacity. The LSS5 programme targets a further ~2 GW; floating solar on reservoir surfaces is being explored by TNB.
  • Biomass (6% capacity / 5% generation): Malaysia has significant biomass potential from palm oil mill effluent, empty fruit bunches, and wood residue. Small IPPs supply under the Feed-in Tariff (FiT) and competitive auction schemes. Palm oil is both a major export commodity and a source of controversy over land use; biomass power generation from agricultural waste is viewed as a lower-risk sustainability pathway than dedicated energy crops.
Malaysia's three separate grid systems (Peninsular, Sabah, Sarawak) represent distinct sub-sectors: Peninsular is a competitive liberalised market with TNB as the dominant utility; Sarawak is state-controlled with SEB as the sole operator and large hydro as the primary resource; Sabah is smaller and historically underserved, now being integrated via the Sarawak–Sabah interconnection (energised January 2026, 50 MW initial capacity).

Demand growth (~2%/yr) is moderate, reflecting Malaysia's middle-income status, industrial maturity, and improving energy efficiency. The 2020 dip reflects COVID-19 lockdowns. Manufacturing (semiconductors, E&E, auto assembly) and commercial real estate are the primary demand growth drivers in Peninsular Malaysia. Sarawak's demand growth is accelerating as downstream industrial development (aluminium smelting, petrochemicals at SCORE — Sarawak Corridor of Renewable Energy) expands. Sabah has historically had limited grid access but is growing with improved transmission.

Tariffs: Peninsular Malaysia (TNB) residential: MYR 0.218/kWh for first 200 kWh/month, rising to MYR 0.571/kWh for >900 kWh — tiered structure with implicit cross-subsidy. Average residential rate ~MYR 0.35/kWh (~USD 0.075/kWh). Industrial tariffs ~MYR 0.365–0.417/kWh (medium voltage). Sarawak (SEB) domestic tariffs are significantly lower (~MYR 0.175–0.25/kWh), reflecting hydro cost advantages. Fuel cost pass-through mechanism (ICPT, reviewed semi-annually) adjusts tariffs for fuel price changes.

3. Sector organisation and actors

TNB (Tenaga Nasional Berhad) Peninsular Transmission & Dominant Utility

TNB is the listed state-controlled utility (Khazanah Nasional ~35% shareholder) responsible for transmission, distribution, and generation in Peninsular Malaysia. TNB owns ~12 GW of generation (hydro, gas, some solar) and purchases from ~20 GW of private IPPs under long-term PPAs. TNB operates the Peninsular grid under a Single Buyer model. TNB is also the single buyer for all LSS solar and FiT renewable energy. TNB's renewable energy ambitions are anchored in the NETR 2023 and include large-scale solar deployment, offshore wind feasibility, and potential hydrogen import/blending.

Sarawak Energy (SEB) & SESB (Sabah) East Malaysia Utilities

Sarawak Energy Berhad (SEB) is wholly owned by the Sarawak State Government and operates generation (Bakun, Murum, Baleh dams + coal at Sejingkat), transmission, and distribution in Sarawak. SEB is the operator of the Sarawak–Sabah interconnection (energised Jan 2026) and plans to export 30–50 MW of renewable energy to Brunei by 2030. Sabah Electricity (SESB), majority-owned by TNB (80%), operates the Sabah grid — historically isolated and diesel-heavy, now gradually integrating via the Sabah-Sarawak link.

Energy Commission (Suruhanjaya Tenaga, EC) Regulator

The EC (Suruhanjaya Tenaga) regulates the electricity sector in Peninsular Malaysia and Sabah (Sarawak falls under the Sarawak State Government via SEB). The EC issues licences, approves tariff adjustments (via the ICPT mechanism for fuel pass-through), and oversees the LSS renewable auction programme. EC has been expanding its role to include grid code development for storage, V2G, and distributed generation as the sector decarbonises.

4. Key issues and challenges

  • NETR 2023 implementation — 70% RE by 2050: The NETR roadmap is ambitious but the near-term trajectory (24 GW RE capacity by 2035 in Peninsular Malaysia vs. ~8 GW today) requires sustained acceleration in solar auctions, offshore wind development, and grid upgrades. The LSS5 programme and green hydrogen industrial zone (Pengerang, Johor) are near-term anchors. However, coal phase-out depends on PPA expirations and government willingness to incur stranded asset costs.
  • East–West grid integration: Peninsular Malaysia and Borneo (Sabah+Sarawak) operate as de facto separate energy markets. The Sarawak–Sabah interconnection (50 MW, Jan 2026) is historic but far below Sarawak's surplus capacity (~2+ GW). A Peninsular–Sarawak HVDC would transform Malaysia's energy security and export potential but requires massive capital and a clear commercial structure. Until this link is built, Sarawak's surplus hydro cannot reach Singapore or Peninsular Malaysia efficiently.
  • Gas supply security: TNB's CCGTs depend on Petronas domestic gas allocation. As offshore fields deplete, Petronas is developing new fields (Kasawari) and Malaysia is expanding LNG regas capacity for domestic use — a reversal from the historic LNG export focus. Gas prices to power generators are regulated below market, creating implicit subsidies that complicate the transition economics.
  • Data centre boom and green energy demand: Johor is experiencing a hyperscale data centre boom (Google, Microsoft, AWS, ByteDance investing USD 10B+), driven by proximity to Singapore and lower land and power costs. DC power demand in Johor alone could reach 10+ GW by 2030. The Singapore–Johor Special Economic Zone (SEZ, signed Jan 2024) is expected to shift significant DC load from Singapore to Malaysia, creating major new demand that both stresses the grid and provides off-take for RE projects.
  • Downstream SCORE industrial load in Sarawak: The Sarawak Corridor of Renewable Energy (SCORE) envisions energy-intensive industries (aluminium smelting, silicon production, petrochemicals) absorbing Sarawak's surplus hydro. While some smelters operate (Press Metal aluminium, ~1.5 GW load), the full SCORE vision has been slower to materialise than planned. SEB's Baleh dam (1,285 MW) adds to surplus that needs industrial or export off-take.

5. Five key takeaways

1
Malaysia's three separate grid systems (Peninsular, Sabah, Sarawak) are at very different development stages — the January 2026 Sarawak–Sabah interconnection is a historic step toward a unified Borneo grid, enabling Sarawak's large surplus hydro to supply Sabah and potentially Brunei and Singapore.
2
The NETR 2023 (70% RE by 2050) is Malaysia's most ambitious energy transition plan; near-term delivery depends on the LSS5 solar auction programme and the pace of coal PPA expirations — coal phase-out by 2050 requires firm retirement schedules not yet mandated.
3
Malaysia is the transit country for the LTMS-PIP (Laos hydro to Singapore) and exports 50 MW of renewable energy to Singapore (ENEGEM, Dec 2024) — demonstrating its pivotal role in the regional power grid and its potential as a green energy corridor.
4
The Johor data centre boom (USD 10B+ committed by Google, Microsoft, AWS, ByteDance) creates a massive new RE demand source that could accelerate the LSS programme — or strain the grid if not matched by generation and transmission investment.
5
A Peninsular–Sarawak HVDC link remains the most transformative but also most expensive and complex infrastructure investment in Malaysia's energy future — enabling Sarawak's surplus hydro to reach Singapore and Peninsular markets but requiring multi-billion USD capital and a credible commercial structure.
