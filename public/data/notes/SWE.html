Generation Mix & Sector Overview

Sweden operates one of Europe's cleanest and most stable electricity systems — nearly 99% of generation comes from clean sources in 2025, with fossil fuels accounting for only ~1% (primarily backup gas). The mix is dominated by a trio: hydropower (~40%), primarily large river systems in the north; nuclear (~25%), from six reactors at Forsmark and Ringhals; and wind (~25%), with 17 GW of onshore capacity and rapid offshore expansion underway. Sweden is a structural net electricity exporter within the Nordic market (Nordpool), with transmission links to Norway, Finland, Denmark, Poland, Lithuania, and Germany.

The Electrification Demand Surge

Sweden faces a uniquely ambitious challenge: electrifying not just transport and heating (already advanced) but its core heavy industries. The scale of projected electricity demand growth is unlike anything in Europe:

  • HYBRIT / SSAB green steel: Hydrogen-based steel production replacing coking coal; pilot plant operational in Luleå; full SSAB transformation requires ~15 TWh/year of clean electricity — equivalent to adding a major nuclear unit's output.
  • Northvolt battery gigafactory: Skellefteå; the largest single new electricity consumer in northern Sweden; 2–4 TWh/year of demand; facing financial difficulties in 2024 but strategically important for European battery supply chain.
  • Data centres: Sweden's cheap, clean electricity and cold climate make it a preferred European data centre location; Google, Meta, Microsoft all expanding; each hyperscale campus adds 0.5–1 TWh/year of demand.
  • Total demand forecast: Swedish Energy Agency forecasts electricity demand doubling to ~300 TWh by 2045, from ~163 TWh today. This requires adding nuclear, offshore wind, and significantly more grid capacity — especially north-to-south transmission through Norrland.
Sweden's electricity system is the cleanest large economy in Europe — 99% fossil-free in 2025 — yet faces its most complex infrastructure challenge in decades. The north-south transmission bottleneck (Norrland has surplus clean power; southern Sweden and industry have demand) cannot be resolved without either new high-voltage lines (politically and permitting-wise very slow in Sweden) or distributed generation and storage in the south. This internal congestion is Sweden's primary electricity system constraint and the key risk to its industrial decarbonisation ambitions.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Sweden's reversal on nuclear power in 2022 — after 40 years of a phase-out political consensus — reflects a pan-European reassessment of nuclear's role in deep decarbonisation. The original 1980 referendum decision to phase out nuclear was driven by safety concerns post-Three Mile Island and Chernobyl. By 2022, the security-of-supply concerns post-Ukraine invasion, combined with the electrification demand surge, made maintaining and expanding nuclear the pragmatic choice. Sweden now aims for 2.5 GW of new nuclear by 2035 and is considering SMRs. This policy reversal has been replicated in Finland, France, Poland, the Netherlands, and the UK.
2
The north-south grid transmission constraint is Sweden's most binding infrastructure bottleneck — more limiting than generation capacity itself. Sweden's hydro and wind wealth is in Norrland (north), while industrial and population demand centres are in the south (Stockholm, Gothenburg, Malmö). The SE3-SE4 price areas regularly trade at significant premiums over SE1-SE2. Building new high-voltage transmission through Swedish forests and fjälls requires 10–15 years of permitting and political consensus that is currently absent. This internal grid constraint limits Sweden's ability to monetise its clean energy surplus for decarbonising southern industries.
3
Sweden's offshore wind potential is enormous — 100+ GW in proposed projects — but the regulatory and military exclusion zone constraints mean less than 20 GW is realistically available by 2035. Swedish military authorities have blocked or limited several offshore wind zones for radar and defence reasons. The concession process is also slower than Denmark's or the UK's. The gap between pipeline (100 GW) and deliverable capacity (20 GW by 2030–35) reflects a planning and permitting problem, not a resource or cost problem.
4
Vattenfall's European footprint makes it a geopolitically significant energy company — its decisions in Germany (lignite exit, offshore wind) and the Netherlands (offshore wind pause) shape European energy policy beyond Sweden. As a state-owned company, Vattenfall's strategic choices are partly political. Its 2024 decisions to pause UK offshore wind projects and retreat from the Aberdeen offshore development reflect the broader cost inflation crisis in offshore wind — with implications for UK and European supply chains that go beyond Swedish interests.
5
Sweden's industrial electrification wave — green steel, batteries, hydrogen — represents the leading edge of what deep industrial decarbonisation looks like in practice, and its success or failure will set expectations for the rest of Europe. If HYBRIT-model steel and Northvolt-model battery manufacturing succeed at scale in Sweden, they validate the full electrification pathway for hard-to-abate industries. If they fail (as Northvolt's 2024 financial difficulties suggest is a real risk), it will cause a fundamental rethink of industrial decarbonisation timelines across Europe.
