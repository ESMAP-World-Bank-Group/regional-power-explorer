Generation Mix & Capacity

Mexico's power system runs on gas. Combined cycle plants supply roughly 60% of generation, and about 70% of the gas that feeds them arrives by pipeline from Texas. That combination gives Mexico cheap fuel and a deep external dependence, which is simultaneously its main cost advantage and its main security exposure. The remainder is hydro, a large but ageing fuel oil fleet, wind and solar built mostly between 2016 and 2020, one nuclear station at Laguna Verde, and geothermal at Cerro Prieto.

The institutional history matters more here than in most systems. The 2013 and 2014 reform unbundled CFE, created a wholesale market run by CENACE, and ran three long-term auctions that delivered some of the lowest solar and wind prices recorded anywhere at the time. From 2019 that direction reversed: auctions were cancelled, permitting slowed, the 2021 electricity law gave CFE dispatch priority, and constitutional and secondary reforms completed in 2024 and 2025 re-established CFE as a state public company with a majority share of generation as a planning objective.

The result is a market that still exists but no longer allocates investment. Short-term energy and ancillary markets continue to operate, and private plants under legacy permits and self-supply arrangements continue to run, but new capacity is planned by the state and built largely by CFE or through mixed schemes with a state majority. Reserve margins in the central and north eastern regions have tightened to the point of repeated operating alerts.

Market Structure & Institutions

  • CFE: The state utility, now constitutionally defined as a public company rather than a state productive enterprise. It dominates generation, owns transmission and distribution as a legal monopoly, and supplies the large majority of retail customers.
  • SENER: The energy ministry sets sector planning through PRODESEN, the national development programme for the electricity system, and since the 2025 secondary laws holds authorities previously distributed to independent regulators.
  • Wholesale market (MEM): Day-ahead and real-time energy, ancillary services and a capacity market continue to clear. What has ended is the long-term auction mechanism that previously drove private renewable investment.
  • Private generation: Legacy self-supply and independent power producer contracts remain in force and account for a significant share of industrial supply. New private projects proceed mainly through mixed vehicles in which the state holds a majority.
  • Transmission: The national grid is legally a state monopoly. Expansion is funded from CFE and federal budgets, which is why the northern reinforcement programme and the Baja California link move at the pace of fiscal allocation rather than of demand.
  • Cross-border links: Modest ties to Texas and California, plus links to Guatemala and Belize. Transfer capability is small relative to system size, so Mexico is effectively an electrical island while importing at scale the gas that runs it.
Mexico is the clearest case in the hemisphere of policy reversing direction within a decade. A reform that produced record-low renewable prices was replaced by a model in which the state plans and builds. The question is not whether that model can operate the system, which it does, but whether public balance sheet capacity can add generation and transmission fast enough for industrial load growing at the pace nearshoring implies.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Gas dependence is the defining exposure. Roughly 60% of generation from gas, and roughly 70% of that gas imported by pipeline from a single neighbour. Price and supply risk both sit outside Mexican policy control.
2
Capacity is the binding constraint on industry. Grid connection and reserve margin, not labour or logistics, increasingly determine where nearshoring investment can locate.
3
Renewables stalled, but not on cost. Mexico has among the best solar resource of any large economy and cleared auction prices near 20 dollars per MWh in 2017. The slowdown since is institutional.
4
Two grids, one country. Baja California's synchronisation with the United States rather than with Mexico is a standing anomaly and a live infrastructure question.
5
Regional integration is shallow. Interconnection with the United States, Guatemala and Belize is small relative to system size, so Mexico captures little of the diversity value that stronger ties would offer in either direction.
