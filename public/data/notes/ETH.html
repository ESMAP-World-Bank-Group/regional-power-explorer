1. Energy context

Ethiopia's electricity sector is defined by a single extraordinary fact: the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (GERD), completed and officially inaugurated on September 9, 2025, is the largest hydropower plant in Africa. At 5,150 MW across 13 turbines on the Blue Nile, GERD more than doubled Ethiopia's installed capacity and delivered the country's long-held ambition to become a continental electricity exporter. In the Ethiopian fiscal year 2024/25, EEP generated ~29.5 TWh — a near-doubling from ~15 TWh seven years earlier — almost entirely from renewable sources.

The power sector is structured around two vertically separated state utilities: Ethiopian Electric Power (EEP) handles all generation and the national transmission grid, while Ethiopian Electric Utility (EEU) manages distribution and retail for over 5.2 million customers. IPP participation remains minimal — flagship solar projects signed in 2019 (ACWA Power's 2 × 125 MW, Enel's 100 MW) never reached financial close, largely due to foreign exchange convertibility constraints and tariffs that were among the world's lowest until the September 2024 reform raised them 122% in the first step of a 2024–2028 phase-in. Despite this, roughly 60 million Ethiopians still lack electricity access, and the country's revised Mission 300 energy compact targets 75% national electrification by 2030.

The sector's two defining tensions sit side by side: a world-class renewable resource now being systematically developed, and a distribution system (grid reliability, outages, rural access) lagging far behind the generation leap.

  • Hydropower (93.8% capacity / ~95% generation): The backbone of the system. GERD (5,150 MW, 13 × 400/375 MW turbines, fully operational September 2025) contributes roughly 51% of national supply. The Gibe cascade adds another ~2,500 MW: Gibe I (184 MW, 2004), Gibe II (420 MW, 2010), Gibe III (1,870 MW, 2015). Koysha Dam (Gibe IV, ~1,800 MW) is 65% complete as of March 2025 with a $950 million Chinese loan secured in August 2024. Older plants include Tekeze (300 MW), Tana Beles (460 MW), and Fincha (134 MW).
  • Wind (~5.8% capacity / ~4-5% generation): Adama I (51 MW, 2012) and Adama II (153 MW, 2014) operate reliably; Ashegoda (120 MW, Tigray) was damaged during the 2020–2022 Tigray war and remains offline. Aysha I (~100 MW, Somali region) came online February 2023. Wind underperforms relative to capacity due to Ashegoda's outage.
  • Geothermal (0.1% / <0.3% generation): Only Aluto Langano (7.3 MW) is grid-connected — negligible at system scale. Ethiopia's estimated potential is 5,000–7,000 MW. Tulu Moye (50 MW Phase 1, Meridiam) and Corbetti (500 MW long-term, Reykjavik Geothermal/Meridiam) are in advanced development but have faced repeated delays. A World Bank $350 million geothermal project is de-risking exploration.
Carbon intensity of the Ethiopian grid: approximately 24 gCO₂eq/kWh — one of the lowest in Africa and comparable to nuclear-heavy grids in Europe. This low-carbon profile makes Ethiopian electricity an attractive export for industries with decarbonisation commitments.

Per-capita consumption remains very low at ~188 kWh/person/year (EEP 2024/25 brief) despite the generation leap. The distribution network has not kept pace with generation growth: EEU has 5.2 million customers but grid reliability is poor — the Energy for Growth Hub estimates ~39 outages and ~21 hours of downtime per month, one of the worst reliability profiles in Africa. Industry bears enormous costs: outages have been estimated to cost ~3.1% of GDP annually.

Tariff reform (September 2024): A four-year phased tariff increase (2024–2028) raised residential rates by ~122% in the first step. Pre-reform, the lowest residential tier was ~$0.002/kWh — among the world's lowest. By September 2025, average residential tariff reached ~ETB 0.924/kWh (~$0.006/kWh), still very low by international standards but moving toward cost-recovery as required under the IMF Extended Credit Facility programme.

2.3 Grid and interconnections

EEP operates the national high-voltage grid. Ethiopia has been the most active power exporter in EAPP, earning $118.1 million in FY 2024/25 from exports to Kenya ($86.3 million), Djibouti ($30.9 million), and Sudan ($0.9 million). Grid reliability and the transmission-distribution interface remain the binding constraints on domestic service quality.

2.4 Export ambitions and the $1 billion target

Ethiopia as Africa's power exporter Strategic

Ethiopia's official energy strategy positions GERD's ~15,700 GWh/year output as the foundation for continental power export. Current exports (~2.1 TWh/year, $118 million in FY 2024/25) are a fraction of the $1 billion/year target. The Kenya HVDC line is the near-term priority corridor; an Ethiopia–Tanzania link would unlock the second export axis. The IMF's parallel conditionality — raise domestic tariffs — creates an alignment of incentives: as domestic tariffs rise toward cost-recovery, export revenue increasingly subsidises the domestic system's financial sustainability.

GERD dispute — Nile tensions without resolution Unresolved

The GERD fills 74 billion m³ of the Blue Nile — 85% of Egypt's Nile flow. Egypt withdrew from African Union-mediated talks in early 2024, brought the dispute to the UN Security Council in September 2024, and maintains a military threat posture. GERD's fifth and final reservoir fill was completed October 2024; the dam was officially inaugurated September 9, 2025. No binding water-sharing agreement exists. Long-term risk: reduced Nile flows to Egypt and Sudan in drought years; upstream-downstream tensions will remain a feature of the Ethiopia–Egypt relationship for decades.

3. Sector organisation and actors

3.1 Market structure

Ethiopia's sector was unbundled in 2013 when the former EEPCO was split into EEP (generation/transmission) and EEU (distribution/retail). Both remain fully state-owned. The Ethiopian Energy Authority (EEA) is an independent regulator responsible for licensing, standards, tariff methodology, and the November 2020 mini-grid directive. EEP holds 24 power plants (16 hydro) with a combined 8,115.84 MW of installed capacity per its 2024/25 brief.

Private sector participation is limited. All three Scaling Solar projects (ACWA Power 2 × 125 MW, Enel 100 MW, signed 2019) failed to reach financial close, primarily due to foreign exchange convertibility and offtake bankability issues. The July 2024 birr float (part of IMF ECF) and the September 2024 tariff reform are intended to address both barriers and unlock a new wave of IPP investment. EEA has issued mini-grid licences under the 2020 directive, enabling a growing off-grid solar market.

4. Key issues and dynamics

4.1 GERD — a dam and its consequences

  • Official inauguration: September 9, 2025 (Ethiopian New Year), following the fifth and final reservoir fill in October 2024 (74 billion m³, target elevation ~640 m). All 13 turbines operational. Annual output target: ~15,700 GWh/year.
  • GERD now supplies approximately 51% of Ethiopia's national electricity and generated ~18 TWh in EFY 2024/25 alone (out of total 29.5 TWh). It is the clearest single explanation for Ethiopia's near-doubling of generation in one fiscal year.
  • The Nile dispute has not been resolved and is structurally likely to remain unresolved: Egypt's 1959 water agreement allocates 55.5 BCM/year to Egypt, zero to Ethiopia. Ethiopia rejects this colonial-era framework. Egypt withdrew from AU mediation in early 2024 and brought the case to the UN Security Council. Diplomatic and military tensions persist without near-term resolution.

4.2 Distribution reliability — the missing half of the system

  • Ethiopia's grid outage problem is severe and well-documented: ~39 outages and ~21 hours of downtime per month per connection (Energy for Growth Hub, 2023). This is independent of generation capacity — it reflects the state of transmission and distribution infrastructure, not fuel or plant availability.
  • A nationwide blackout in March 2024 plunged most of the country into darkness. Of 144 transformer stations, only 95 are connected to the national control centre; most communication is by radio or telephone.
  • Outage costs: manufacturing firms lose an average of ~$977/month — ~9 times their monthly electricity bill and ~2.2% of monthly sales. Outages are estimated to cost ~3.1% of GDP annually.
  • AFD is financing a grid control system upgrade; AfDB is funding the Eastern Ethiopia Grid Reinforcement Project. These are necessary but not sufficient — the distribution upgrade backlog is large.

4.3 Rural electrification — 60 million still unconnected

  • NEP 2.0 (2019) targeted 100% access by 2025 through 8.2 million new grid connections plus 6 million off-grid households. The target was not met. National access remains ~55% (World Bank 2023).
  • The 2025 Mission 300 Energy Compact sets revised targets: 75% electrification by 2030; 14,000 MW installed capacity; 58% clean cooking access. World Bank ELEAP and ADELE projects contributed over 1.6 million on-grid connections and ~8 million people reached.
  • For households more than 25 km from the grid, the strategy relies permanently on off-grid solar home systems. EEA's 2020 mini-grid directive has enabled private mini-grid operators but scale remains limited.

4.4 IPP blockage and the path to private capital

  • The three Scaling Solar projects (ACWA Power Gad 125 MW + Dicheto 125 MW at record $0.0253/kWh; Enel Metahara 100 MW) were signed in 2019 and achieved the world's lowest solar PPA prices at the time — but none reached financial close. PPAs for Gad and Dicheto were reportedly cancelled.
  • Root cause: Ethiopia's managed exchange rate (pre-July 2024 float) made USD-denominated returns unrepatriable. The July 2024 IMF-backed birr float and the September 2024 tariff reform together address both the currency and offtake-bankability barriers.
  • The 2026–2030 period may see the first wave of viable IPPs in solar and geothermal — if the tariff reform path holds and EEP/EEU financial sustainability improves under the IMF programme.

5. Five things to take away

1
GERD (5,150 MW, inaugurated September 2025) is the most consequential energy infrastructure event in modern Ethiopian history. It pushed national generation to 29.5 TWh in EFY 2024/25 — a doubling in seven years — and positions Ethiopia as the natural hub of East African power exports. Future additions (Koysha ~1,800 MW, Gibe V ~560 MW) could double the system again by 2030.
2
The grid reliability crisis is the critical bottleneck: ~39 outages/month, costs of ~3.1% of GDP annually, and a distribution network that has not kept pace with generation. Generating world-class electricity means little when transmission losses, transformer failures, and poor control infrastructure prevent it from reaching consumers reliably.
3
The September 2024 tariff reform (122% increase in step 1 of a 2024–2028 phase-in) is the most important sector policy shift since unbundling. Combined with the July 2024 birr float, it creates the conditions for IPP investment and EEU financial sustainability for the first time. Implementation discipline is the key variable.
4
GERD's Nile dispute with Egypt and Sudan has no binding resolution and is likely to persist for decades. Egypt's military posture and diplomatic escalation (UN Security Council, September 2024) reflect existential stakes. The absence of a water-sharing agreement is a chronic source of regional tension in the Northeast Africa geopolitical landscape.
5
Ethiopia's export ambition ($1 billion/year target) depends on three infrastructure bets: the Kenya HVDC line continuing to scale toward its 2,000 MW rated capacity, an Ethiopia–Tanzania HVDC link progressing to construction, and domestic tariff reform creating a creditworthy EEU as an offtaker. Any slippage on the tariff path threatens the entire export-finance model.
