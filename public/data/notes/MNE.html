Generation Mix (2024)

Montenegro is a small system — approximately 396,000 customers and ~3,000 GWh annual demand — that oscillates between net exporter and net importer depending on hydrological conditions. In 2024, unfavourable rainfall reduced hydro output significantly: total generation fell 15% to 3,447 GWh. In a good hydro year, hydropower can supply 60–70% of generation; the sole coal plant (Pljevlja, 225 MW) provides thermal baseload, and wind and small hydro contribute the rest.

Market Structure & Key Actors

Montenegro's electricity sector is centred on EPCG, the state-controlled utility, with Italian utility A2A holding a significant minority stake. The sector has been formally unbundled with CGES as independent TSO, and Montenegro operates within the Energy Community framework as an EU accession candidate.

  • Regulator: REGAGEN (Energy Regulatory Agency) — sets tariffs, issues licences, oversees Energy Community compliance; reports to parliament.
  • EPCG (Elektroprivreda Crne Gore): State-controlled utility (~55% state, ~45% A2A Italia); owns Piva, Perućica, Pljevlja, and most small hydro; also the main supplier.
  • CGES: Montenegrin Transmission System Operator — state-owned; manages 220/400 kV grid; operates the Monita interconnector and all cross-border lines; ENTSO-E full member.
  • A2A (Italian utility): ~45% stake in EPCG; provides operational expertise, access to Italian and Central European market connections, and is a key stakeholder in any Monita cable commercial arrangements.
  • Wholesale market: Montenegro has a functioning bilateral OTC market; CGES operates the balancing mechanism; market coupling with Serbia and the broader SEEPEX platform is evolving.
Montenegro's partnership with A2A and the Monita undersea cable together represent a coherent strategy: sell hydro-surplus green electricity to Italy (and the broader EU) via a dedicated transmission link, while using Pljevlja as backup for dry years. If Monita reaches full capacity (2 × 500 MW), Montenegro could become a net electricity exporter to the EU for much of the year.

The Monita Undersea Cable — A Strategic Asset

The Monita interconnector is Montenegro's most strategically significant energy infrastructure project. The submarine HVDC cable crosses the Adriatic Sea to connect the Montenegro transmission grid (at Lastva Grbaljska) to the Italian grid (at Villanova, Puglia), enabling direct electricity trade between the Balkans and Italy — the first such link of its kind in the Adriatic.

  • Cable 1: 500 MW capacity; operational. The first cable enabled market coupling between Montenegro and Italy and provided a route for Western Balkans RE exports to reach Italy's premium-price market.
  • Cable 2: A deal to install the second cable within the Monita interconnector (adding another 500 MW, for 1,000 MW total) was signed in October 2025. The second cable would double export capacity.
  • Commercial logic: Montenegro's hydropower is dispatchable and in principle "green"; Italian power prices are historically higher than Balkan markets. CGES and EPCG/A2A can monetise surplus hydro into the Italian market at premium prices.
  • Regional significance: Monita is not just a Montenegro project — it creates a transit pathway for other Western Balkans RE (Serbian wind, Albanian solar) to reach EU markets, subject to transmission capacity on the overland links feeding into Montenegro.
Monita is the Western Balkans' most important electricity export infrastructure and a proof of concept for the region's green electricity export thesis. With Cable 2 under development, Montenegro has a credible path to becoming a net green exporter to the EU, provided Pljevlja coal can be replaced by 2035 without a supply gap.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Monita is Montenegro's most consequential energy asset — and the Western Balkans' best export route to the EU. With Cable 2 under development, Montenegro has a credible path to exporting 1,000 MW of green electricity directly into the Italian market. This changes the economics of any RE investment in the country — and potentially in neighbouring Albania and Serbia as well.
2
The Pljevlja coal phase-out is the defining domestic energy decision of the next decade. The plant is essential for dry-year security and north Montenegro employment, but incompatible with EU accession environmental obligations. A managed closure — with a replacement (wind + storage, or a modest gas bridge) in place — is achievable but requires political commitment not yet visible.
3
Montenegro's hydrology is its greatest asset and greatest risk. The 2024 drought cut generation by 15% and pushed the system toward imports. Climate projections for the Dinaric Alps suggest declining average rainfall. Diversifying beyond hydro — particularly into wind in the northern highlands — is a climate adaptation imperative, not just an energy transition aspiration.
4
The A2A partnership gives EPCG access to Italian market expertise and capital. A2A's ~45% stake aligns Italian commercial interests with Montenegro's grid development — particularly relevant for Monita commercial operations, RE project finance, and market coupling. This is one of the most productive foreign utility partnerships in the Western Balkans.
5
Montenegro is disproportionately important to Western Balkans regional integration. As the only country in the region with a direct HVDC link to the EU (Italy), Montenegro's transmission capacity decisions ripple through the entire regional grid. CGES's role as a transit hub — for Albanian and Serbian power toward Italy, and for EU market price signals flowing east — will grow as the region integrates.
