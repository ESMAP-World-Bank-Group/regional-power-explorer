Generation Mix & Sector Overview

Dominica, the "Nature Isle of the Caribbean," is a rugged volcanic island of ~750 km² with one of the Eastern Caribbean's most dramatic geothermal resources — and one of its most diesel-dependent current electricity systems. The island's approximately 85 MW of installed generation is operated by DOMLEC (Dominica Electricity Services Ltd.), a subsidiary of Fortis Inc. (Canada), with approximately 78% coming from diesel and HFO at the Fond Cole Power Station and satellite plants. Small run-of-river hydro stations on the Trafalgar, Padu, and Laudat rivers contribute ~14%. A modest solar fleet (~6%) has been added post-2018. Peak demand is only ~25-30 MW — making this one of the Caribbean's smallest grids in absolute terms. The defining feature of Dominica's energy future is not its current mix but its untapped geothermal potential: 150-200 MW in the Wotten Waven / Laudat geothermal field — more than five times the island's peak electricity demand. If developed, Dominica could supply 100% of its own electricity from geothermal, retire all diesel, and export surplus electricity to neighbouring Martinique and Guadeloupe (French overseas territories) via submarine cable — transforming from one of the region's most fuel-import-dependent islands to a clean energy exporter.

Wotten Waven geothermal: more energy than Dominica needsThe Wotten Waven / Laudat geothermal field in Dominica's volcanic interior is one of the Eastern Caribbean's most promising geothermal resources. Surface manifestations: boiling springs, fumaroles, mud pools at Wotten Waven village and Titou Gorge area; subsurface temperature gradient drilling (IDA-financed, 2012-2014) confirmed viable resource at ~2,000m depth. Estimated resource: 150-200 MWe; Dominica's peak electricity demand: ~27-30 MW. Geothermal capacity factor ~85-90% (baseload, unlike solar or wind). Development history: multiple exploration permits; Ormat Technologies (US, Israel) undertook initial exploration 2015-2018; IDA/ESMAP funded phases 1-2. Hurricane Maria 2017 caused significant delay (exploration camp damaged; equipment destroyed). Current status (2026): Phase 3 exploration ongoing; commercial FID expected 2026-2027; first commercial production target: 2028-2030. Export scenario: 50-60 MW domestic use + 80-100 MW export via submarine cable to Martinique / Guadeloupe. France (as Martinique/Guadeloupe's sovereign) has formally expressed interest in the export option.
Hurricane Maria 2017: worst natural disaster in Caribbean history per capitaHurricane Maria (Category 5, 257 km/h sustained winds) made direct landfall on Dominica on 18 September 2017. Damage: ~90% of buildings damaged or destroyed; 65% loss of roof cover island-wide; total economic damage ~US$1.3B (~225% of GDP); 31 confirmed deaths. DOMLEC grid: complete destruction — all distribution lines down, substations flooded, generating equipment damaged. Power restoration took 4-5 months for most of the island (some communities waited 6+ months). The Maria experience validated PM Roosevelt Skerrit's "Climate Resilient Island" strategy — all post-Maria reconstruction (schools, hospitals, roads, electricity infrastructure) was built to hurricane-resistant standards. DOMLEC's post-Maria grid rebuild used underground cabling in vulnerable areas, elevated substations, and hurricane-rated switchgear. The rebuild cost (IDA/IDB/EU funded): ~US$70M for electricity infrastructure alone.
DOMLEC and Fortis: same model as BelizeDominica Electricity Services Ltd. is 71% owned by Fortis Inc. (St. John's, Newfoundland), with the Government of Dominica holding 21% and the public ~8%. The DOMLEC-Fortis relationship mirrors Belize's BEL-Fortis structure: a small Caribbean utility within a large North American investor-owned utility conglomerate. Fortis provides management and technical expertise; the regulatory framework (Dominica NTRC, National Telecommunications Regulatory Commission) sets tariffs. Post-Maria, Fortis has increased investment in grid resilience but faces the same tension as in Belize: generating a regulated return while the government pushes for rapid RE transition investment that may dilute near-term returns.
Citizenship by Investment (CBI): funding the energy transitionDominica's Citizenship by Investment Programme (CBI) — one of the Caribbean's most active, generating ~US$100-150M/year — has been a significant source of government investment capital for post-Maria reconstruction and energy infrastructure. CBI funds have contributed to: the Fond Cole Power Station resilience upgrades; rooftop solar grants for households; road infrastructure enabling access to geothermal sites. The CBI-to-energy pipeline is unusual among SIDS — most rely exclusively on MDB borrowing. Dominica's CBI revenue provides a degree of fiscal flexibility for energy investment that most OECS peers lack. The sustainability of CBI revenue (given scrutiny from EU/US of Caribbean CBI programmes) is a medium-term risk to this financing model.
Export scenario: Dominica as the Caribbean's green energy hubIf the Wotten Waven geothermal field is developed to 100+ MW, Dominica would need only ~30 MW domestically, leaving 70-80 MW for export. A submarine cable to Martinique (~35 km southeast, French territory, ~400,000 people, heavily diesel-dependent grid) would allow France to count this clean energy toward its RE targets for overseas territories. A further extension to Guadeloupe (~100 km north, ~400,000 people) would complete an Eastern Caribbean clean energy triangle. The IDB has funded a cable feasibility study (2022-2023); results were positive technically, though finance structuring between three jurisdictions (Dominica, France/Martinique, France/Guadeloupe) remains complex. The export revenue — potentially US$20-30M/year at European electricity prices — would transform Dominica's national income.

Post-Maria: Building Back Better or Just Building Back?

Dominica's post-Maria reconstruction offers the Caribbean's most instructive case study in resilient rebuilding:

  • What worked: Underground cabling in Roseau (the capital) dramatically reduced the time to restore electricity post-Maria vs. equivalent overhead line areas — undergrounded areas were back online in days, not months. Hospital and emergency services with backup generation survived the storm operationally. The IDA-funded grid resilience project (2018-2022) has now completed much of the island's distribution upgrade.
  • What was delayed: The geothermal development — which Maria disrupted — remains the critical long-term item. Each year of delay costs Dominica an estimated US$15-20M in avoided diesel fuel costs. The exploration-to-commercial-production timeline has slipped repeatedly; a 2026-2027 FID now looks the earliest realistic date for commercial geothermal.
  • The counterfactual risk: Without geothermal coming online, Dominica's 100% RE by 2030 target is unachievable. Solar alone cannot provide the baseload reliability that geothermal would supply, and Dominica's small land area (only ~750 km²) with rugged topography limits utility-scale solar siting. The 100% RE target is effectively conditional on geothermal — which is conditional on a multi-year commercial development process outside DOMLEC or the government's direct control.
Dominica's energy situation is uniquely paradoxical: an island with enough geothermal energy in its rocks to supply itself and two neighbouring islands five times over, currently burning diesel for 78% of its electricity. The barrier is not resource availability, not technology, not even fundamentally finance — it is the timeline of commercial geothermal development, which requires 8-12 years from exploration to commercial operation even with adequate funding, and which was set back 3-5 years by Hurricane Maria. The Caribbean's most energy-rich small island is also, for now, one of its most fossil-fuel-dependent.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Dominica's geothermal resource (150-200 MW estimated) exceeds its domestic electricity demand (~30 MW) by a factor of five — making it the only SIDS in the Eastern Caribbean with a credible pathway to becoming a net clean energy exporter, and the decision whether and how to develop geothermal for export is the most consequential energy policy choice the island will make in the next decade. The domestic-only scenario (30 MW geothermal for 100% RE) and the export scenario (100+ MW with submarine cable to Martinique/Guadeloupe) have fundamentally different implications for Dominica's economy, fiscal position, and political relationships with France. The export scenario would generate national income equivalent to doubling tourism revenues — but requires France's sustained political commitment to a long-term RE import arrangement from a non-EU territory, which carries geopolitical complexity.
2
Hurricane Maria's destruction of DOMLEC's grid and the subsequent IDA-funded rebuild represents the Caribbean's most advanced implementation of "build back better" for electricity infrastructure — and its lessons (underground cabling priority, elevated substations, hurricane-rated switchgear, backup generation at critical facilities) should be systematically applied across all Eastern Caribbean grid rebuilds rather than reinvented after each storm. The DOMLEC post-Maria rebuild was not perfect — geothermal was delayed, some communities waited 6+ months for power — but it produced a measurably more resilient grid than existed before Maria. Restoration after a subsequent Category 4 storm in 2021 (Tropical Storm Elsa impacts) was demonstrably faster in areas rebuilt post-Maria. The "resilient rebuild" premium (estimated +15-20% capital cost vs. pre-Maria standard) is worth paying as an infrastructure insurance premium in the Caribbean context.
3
The DOMLEC-Fortis ownership structure, which provides management competence and access to Fortis' capital markets but also requires a regulated return that inflates tariffs, becomes an acute problem in the geothermal development context — because Fortis' regulated return model is not designed for the pre-revenue exploration risk phase of geothermal development, creating a structural mismatch that requires concessional public capital (IDA, IDB) to fill the exploration phase before private commercial capital can take over for the production phase. This is why geothermal development globally (including in Dominica) requires a public-risk-taking / private-return structure: governments and MDBs absorb the ~US$20-40M exploration drilling risk; private developers (Ormat, Cyrq, others) then bid competitively for the production phase once the resource is confirmed. The Dominica geothermal finance architecture (IDA exploration → commercial PPA for production) is conceptually sound but took a decade to reach the PPA negotiation stage.
4
Dominica's CBI-funded government fiscal space — unusual among OECS micro-states — has been essential for maintaining RE transition investment momentum between MDB disbursements and between hurricane reconstruction cycles, and demonstrates that diversified non-tax revenue sources (tourism levies, CBI, REDD+ where available) can materially accelerate SIDS energy transitions when directed toward the energy sector by capable governments. Most OECS states depend almost exclusively on MDB project loans for energy infrastructure investment; Dominica's CBI income (~US$100-150M/year, 15-20% of GDP) provides the government budget flexibility to co-invest in energy projects, accelerate regulatory approvals, and maintain grid investment even when MDB disbursement cycles are slow. The CBI sustainability risk (EU and US scrutiny of Caribbean passport programmes) means this financing model cannot be assumed permanent — an argument for accelerating geothermal development while CBI revenues remain strong.
5
Dominica's "Climate Resilient Island" branding — initiated by PM Roosevelt Skerrit immediately after Hurricane Maria — has been more than rhetorical: it has attracted disproportionate climate finance attention, secured a dedicated IDA climate resilience facility, and kept Dominica in international climate conversations far above what a 72,000-person economy would normally achieve, demonstrating that credible national climate narrative combined with demonstrated post-disaster action can generate real financial and diplomatic returns for micro-states. The Climate Resilient Island commitment includes binding climate-proofing standards for all new public construction, a National Resilience Development Strategy, and explicit linkage of CBI investment to resilience infrastructure. Whether this brand promise is being consistently delivered (some infrastructure rebuild has used non-resilient standards due to cost pressure) is debated internally. But the international attention it has generated — including dedicated IDA and EU trust fund windows — has mobilised capital that would not otherwise have reached a 72,000-person island in the Eastern Caribbean.
