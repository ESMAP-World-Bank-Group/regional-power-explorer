1. Energy context

Uganda operates one of East Africa's cleanest electricity systems: approximately 2,048 MW of installed capacity (December 2024) of which over 95% is renewable, overwhelmingly from Nile-source hydropower. The system is entirely domestic — no imported electricity — and has historically been the regional surplus producer, exporting to Kenya, Rwanda, Tanzania, and the DRC. The commissioning of Karuma Hydropower Station (600 MW) in September 2024 — after a 4.5-year delay largely attributable to Chinese equipment certification disputes — doubled effective supply and has pushed total installed capacity above 2 GW for the first time.

Yet Uganda faces a paradox of abundance: with 1,063 MW reportedly idle or dispatched below capacity in late 2024 due to a domestic demand deficit, only 20–25% of the population has a grid connection. Overall electrification reaches ~52% only when off-grid solar (PAYGo lanterns, SHSs) is included. The government's Electricity Access Scale-Up Project (EASP, World Bank USD 500 million) and a network of rural electrification initiatives are working to close the access gap, but grid extension economics remain challenging in low-density, low-income rural areas.

A second structural disruption occurred in March 2025: the 20-year concession of Umeme Limited (Kampala-listed, Eaton Capital majority) expired and was not renewed. The government chose to retake distribution under the state-owned Uganda Electricity Distribution Company (UEDCL), completing a full vertical renationalisation of the Ugandan electricity value chain. The transition's operational implications — for billing, maintenance, and commercial efficiency — are actively monitored.

  • Bagasse/biomass (~8% of capacity): Kakira Sugar (50 MW), Kinyara Sugar, SCOUL — captive industrial cogeneration, partially grid-connected under ERA licensing. Seasonal output variation.
  • Distributed solar (~4% of capacity): Growing mix of smaller solar PV plants — Soroti Solar (10 MW, 2016), Tororo Solar (10 MW), Bulyansali (5 MW), and a pipeline of auction-awarded projects reaching ~100–150 MWp. Uganda has among East Africa's highest solar irradiance (~5.5 kWh/m²/day).
  • Thermal (emergency / <2% of capacity): Thermal plant at Namanve (~50 MW diesel, emergency reserve). High tariff (~USD 0.20/kWh), dispatched only during extreme shortfalls. The government aims to exit thermal entirely as surplus hydro grows.
Surplus paradox (2024–2025): Following Karuma commissioning, Uganda's dispatchable capacity significantly exceeds domestic grid demand. An estimated 1,063 MW of available capacity was reported idle or contracted but undispatched in late 2024. This structural surplus makes new generation investment commercially unattractive and creates payment tensions in existing PPAs — particularly for Karuma and Isimba, whose government-backed offtake obligations weigh on UETCL's financial position.

Per capita electricity consumption (~70 kWh/year) remains among the lowest in Sub-Saharan Africa, reflecting low incomes, high connection costs (~USD 500+ per household), and 75–80% rural population share with limited load density. Industrial demand (manufacturing, agro-processing, mining) is the most price-elastic segment and has grown with new Special Economic Zone development. Uganda's population growth rate (~3.5%/year) will drive substantial incremental demand as connection expands, but the demand-supply gap takes years to close given the economics of rural distribution.

2.4 Regional trade

Export strategy — monetising the Karuma surplus Strategic

With domestic demand insufficient to absorb current generation capacity, Uganda's energy strategy is explicitly pivoting toward electricity exports. The Kenya interconnection (and via Kenya to Ethiopia via the HVDC project) is the primary commercial corridor. The UETCL–KETRACO 400 kV upgrade would add ~600 MW export capacity, allowing Uganda to sell into East Africa's growing demand centres. EAPP's Vision 2035 Master Plan identifies Uganda as one of the primary surplus exporters for the region.

South Sudan represents an untapped near-term export market: the 220 kV Nimule–Juba line (NELSAP-funded, under construction) would bring grid electricity to Juba (currently diesel-dependent) while generating commercial revenue for Uganda. Progress has been slowed by South Sudan's political instability and financing constraints.

UEDCL transition — distribution renationalisation March 2025

Uganda's 20-year electricity distribution concession held by Umeme Limited expired on March 31, 2025, and was absorbed by the state-owned UEDCL. Umeme had been listed on the Uganda Securities Exchange, had secured the concession from the government in 2005, and at its peak served ~1.4 million metered customers. The government declined to extend the concession, citing a desire for greater state control and lower electricity tariffs. UEDCL took over approximately 33 substations, 34,000 km of distribution lines, and 1.5 million customers. The transition raises questions about whether UEDCL has the operational capacity to maintain Umeme's service quality and billing efficiency.

3. Sector organisation and actors

3.1 Market structure

Uganda has a fully unbundled electricity sector, restructured under the Electricity Act 1999 and subsequent legislation. Generation, transmission, and distribution are separate legal entities. The Electricity Regulatory Authority (ERA) issues licences and sets tariffs independently. The sector operates a single-buyer model: UETCL (transmission) acts as the single buyer, purchasing all generation under PPAs and dispatching to distribution companies and bulk consumers. Since March 2025, the value chain is fully state-owned from generation through transmission to distribution.

The Electricity Market Code provides for bilateral contracts and exports. ERA issues generation licences to IPPs (Bujagali Energy Ltd, various solar developers) on standard PPA templates with government guarantee support. The Rural Electrification Agency (REA) co-funds rural grid extension and off-grid solar, operating the Rural Electrification Fund.

4. Key issues and dynamics

4.1 Karuma: finally commissioned, but straining the grid

  • Karuma (600 MW, Sinohydro, Victoria Nile, USD 1.7 billion, China Exim loan) was commissioned September 26, 2024 — 4.5 years after the original 2020 target. Delays involved equipment certification disputes (Chinese vs. European standards) and significant cost overruns, raising questions about the value-for-money of Chinese EPC+finance packages in African infrastructure.
  • The commissioning roughly doubled Uganda's effective hydro capacity in one step. While a transformational achievement for energy security, the near-term consequence is a structural dispatch surplus: domestic grid demand (~650–700 MW peak in 2024) cannot absorb 2,048 MW of available capacity. UETCL carries contractual payment obligations for Karuma's capacity even at low dispatch — a fiscal liability of ~USD 120–150 million/year.
  • The government is urgently pursuing new industrial consumers (mining, data centres, aluminium smelting), export contracts, and demand stimulation through aggressive rural electrification. The EASP (World Bank, USD 500 million) is the primary vehicle for the latter.

4.2 Distribution renationalisation — UEDCL vs. Umeme

  • Umeme's concession was notable as one of Africa's few commercially successful private distribution utilities: it reduced system losses from ~40% (2005) to ~16% (2023), grew the customer base from 290,000 to 1.5 million, and returned consistent dividends to shareholders. The government nonetheless declined to renew, citing tariff levels and the desire to retain distribution rents within the state.
  • UEDCL is a relatively new entity with limited operational track record at distribution scale. The critical risks are: (a) loss of Umeme's institutional memory and commercial discipline; (b) political pressure to suppress tariff increases; (c) transition-year operational disruption affecting billing and maintenance.
  • The World Bank EASP has structured significant technical assistance to UEDCL as a condition of continued lending — an implicit safeguard for operational standards.

4.3 Access gap — 52% and the rural last mile

  • Uganda's ~52% electrification rate (including off-grid) conceals a grid access rate of only 20–25%. The country has among the world's highest electrification affordability barriers: a new connection costs USD 500–1,000 (>4 months' median household income), tariffs average USD 0.18–0.22/kWh (above the regional average for income levels), and rural population density is too low for grid extension economics without heavy subsidy.
  • The EASP and REA programmes target a grid access rate of ~50% by 2027, requiring ~600,000 new household connections per year — a rate Uganda has never previously achieved at scale. Off-grid solar (primarily PAYGo systems from BBOXX, d.light, Fenix International) serves ~2–3 million households but at very low kWh consumption levels.

4.4 Nile basin politics and long-run hydro risk

  • Uganda sits upstream of all other Nile-dependent states. Its hydropower is drawn primarily from the Victoria Nile (between Lake Victoria and Lake Albert), which makes it highly water-secure relative to Nile flow variation — unlike downstream Egypt or Sudan. However, GERD's completion and operation create uncertain multi-year filling scenarios that affect lower-basin flows, with secondary effects on Lake Victoria hydrology debated among researchers.
  • Uganda's Electricity Generation Master Plan identifies ~4,200 MW of additional untapped hydro potential — sufficient to make Uganda a major regional exporter in an EAPP interconnection scenario. The major near-term project is Ayago North (840 MW, pre-feasibility complete), though financing remains uncertain.

5. Five things to take away

1
Uganda now has 2,048 MW of installed capacity — nearly 100% renewable (hydro) — following Karuma's long-delayed commissioning in September 2024. The capacity surplus relative to domestic demand (~700 MW peak) means Uganda's near-term challenge is not supply but finding enough demand to absorb it commercially through rural electrification, export contracts, and industrial growth.
2
The UEDCL takeover of distribution (April 2025, ending Umeme's 20-year concession) completes a full renationalisation of the sector. Umeme's commercial track record was respectable by regional standards; whether UEDCL can maintain service quality, billing efficiency, and loss levels while under political pressure to hold tariffs down is the defining operational question for 2025–2028.
3
Grid electricity reaches only 20–25% of Ugandans, despite near-100% renewable supply and no fuel import dependency — one of the starkest access-capacity paradoxes in Sub-Saharan Africa. The economics of rural last-mile distribution (high capex, low load, low income) require sustained public subsidy or innovative models; the World Bank EASP (USD 500 million) is currently the primary vehicle.
4
Uganda's electricity export strategy — selling surplus hydro into Kenya, Rwanda, Tanzania, DRC, and eventually South Sudan — is a viable commercial opportunity that the EAPP Master Plan supports. The Nimule–Juba 220 kV line (South Sudan) is the highest-impact untapped corridor: it would connect a diesel-dependent capital city to clean Nile hydro while generating revenue for Uganda. South Sudan's political trajectory is the binding constraint.
5
Karuma's troubled delivery — 4.5-year delay, certification disputes, cost overrun — is a cautionary case study for Chinese EPC+finance packages in Sub-Saharan Africa. The project is operational and the 600 MW will be transformational in the long run, but the contracting and risk allocation lessons are being studied regionally. China Exim Bank debt repayments (~USD 1.7 billion) will constrain Uganda's fiscal space for new energy investments for over a decade.
