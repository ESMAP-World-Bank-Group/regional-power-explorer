Generation Mix & Sector Overview

The Netherlands reached a historic milestone in 2024: 50.4% of electricity consumption came from renewables, the first time the majority of Dutch electricity was clean. Wind leads with 11.7 GW total (6.97 GW onshore + 4.75 GW offshore), supplemented by a rapidly growing solar fleet (~22 GW). However, natural gas still provides roughly 40% of electricity generation, reflecting the Netherlands' deep historical reliance on its own Groningen gas field (now closed) and its role as Europe's gas trading hub (TTF). The country has also announced plans for two new nuclear reactors at Borssele, potentially delivering ~2.5 GW by 2035. The single operational reactor (Borssele, 485 MW) continues to run.

The Grid Congestion Emergency

The Netherlands is experiencing one of Europe's most acute electricity grid congestion crises — a bottleneck that is actively slowing its energy transition:

  • Scale of the problem: TenneT reports that as of mid-2025, nearly 50,000 connection requests are pending, ranging from household solar installations to multi-GW industrial electrolysers. The grid simply cannot accept the electricity that solar panels, wind farms, and industrial consumers want to exchange.
  • Root cause: Decades of under-investment in grid infrastructure, combined with a faster-than-anticipated energy transition (solar from 0 to 22 GW in 10 years), overwhelmed a system designed for centralised, predictable fossil fuel generation.
  • Economic impact: Renewable curtailment costs, connection delays, and blocked industrial investments are estimated to cost the Dutch economy €2–4B per year. Companies including major electrolysers for green hydrogen have delayed investment decisions due to grid uncertainty.
  • Resolution timeline: TenneT's €40B+ grid investment programme will take 10–15 years to fully execute. The government has introduced emergency permitting measures, but the backlog will persist through at least 2030.
The Netherlands' grid congestion crisis is a cautionary tale for every country rushing to deploy renewable energy: the generation side of the transition moved far faster than the network infrastructure side. Solar deployment (0 to 22 GW in a decade) outpaced grid investment by a decade. The resulting bottleneck is more expensive to fix than it would have been to prevent — and serves as the primary lesson for emerging markets planning rapid RE deployment: grid investment must lead generation deployment, not follow it.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
The Netherlands demonstrates that crossing the 50% renewable electricity threshold in an advanced economy is achievable — but grid infrastructure, not generation technology, becomes the binding constraint at that scale. Dutch solar and wind are cost-competitive and technically proven. The transition stalled not because of technology or policy failure but because the grid was not built to handle bi-directional, distributed power flows at scale. This resequencing lesson — build the grid before the generation — is the most important lesson from the Dutch experience for developing country planners.
2
The closure of the Groningen gas field and the retention of the TTF gas hub represent a calculated bet: eliminate domestic gas production while remaining Europe's gas price-setter. The Netherlands ended its own gas extraction (for domestic safety and political reasons) while Rotterdam's LNG infrastructure makes it the primary European gas import hub. TTF prices drive European energy markets. This gives the Netherlands significant geopolitical influence over European energy pricing while eliminating the local seismic and political risks of domestic extraction — a structurally advantageous position.
3
The offshore wind target revision (50 GW by 2030 → 30–40 GW by 2040) reflects a European-wide recalibration of offshore wind ambitions driven by supply chain costs, not political will. Cost inflation in offshore wind (steel, cables, foundations, installation vessels) raised levelised costs 40–60% between 2020 and 2023. Several European governments overestimated how fast costs would continue to fall; the Dutch revision is an honest acknowledgment. The 35 GW by 2035 target remains extremely ambitious — it requires roughly 6-7× the current installed capacity in 9 years.
4
TenneT's role as TSO for both the Netherlands and a large portion of Germany makes it one of Europe's most systemically important energy infrastructure companies — and its €40B+ investment need has become a European financing challenge. TenneT's capital requirements are so large that the Dutch government briefly sought a German partner for its German operations (2023-24) before ultimately deciding to sell the German grid to a consortium. The scale of transmission investment required for European energy transition exceeds most TSOs' balance sheets — requiring new financing structures involving public banks, EC support, and private capital.
5
The Dutch new nuclear decision (2 reactors at Borssele) signals a fundamental European policy shift: nuclear is back as a mainstream option even in countries that successfully deployed large-scale renewables. The Netherlands has 22 GW of solar and 12 GW of wind — a credible RE programme. The nuclear decision is not a fallback but a complement: providing firm, dispatchable, low-carbon baseload for periods when wind is low and solar is dark. Combined with hydrogen storage ambitions, the Dutch are explicitly planning for a mixed-technology zero-carbon grid — not a 100% variable RE future.
