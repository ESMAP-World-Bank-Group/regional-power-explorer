Generation Mix & Sector Overview

Tonga's electricity system serves approximately 100,000 people across five island groups — Tongatapu (main island, Nuku'alofa capital, ~70% of population), Vava'u, Ha'apai, 'Eua, and the Niuas — each with a separate grid operated by Tonga Power Limited (TPL), the state utility. Tonga has been one of the Pacific's RE pioneers: the Maama Mai Solar Farm (6 MW, Tongatapu, ADB-financed, commissioned 2012) was among the earliest utility-scale solar projects in the Pacific — predating Fiji's solar boom by nearly a decade. Since 2012, Tongatapu has added 8 MW of additional solar (Atele Solar Farm), wind turbines at 'Eua and Tongatapu (combined ~5-6 MW), and a 3 MW BESS (2018) for solar firming — making Tongatapu's grid approximately 60% RE in 2024, the highest of any Tongan island group. The outer island groups (Vava'u: ADB solar hybrid; Ha'apai: partial solar; 'Eua: wind + diesel) are less RE-advanced. The defining event of recent Tongan energy history: the Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha'apai underwater volcanic eruption (15 January 2022) — the largest volcanic eruption recorded globally in 2022, generating a Pacific-wide tsunami, ash fall blanketing Tonga's islands, and severing the TONGA cable (international internet connection) for weeks. Volcanic ash coated solar panels across all Tongan islands, reducing PV output to near-zero for several days.

Maama Mai 2012: Pacific RE pioneerThe Maama Mai Solar Farm ("Maama Mai" = "a new light" in Tongan) was commissioned in March 2012 — one of the first utility-scale solar projects in the entire Pacific island region, predating Fiji's Ba Solar Farm (2021) by 9 years and establishing Tonga as a Pacific RE leader. ADB financing: US$10M loan + US$1M grant. Design capacity: 6.25 MWp (1,320 polycrystalline panels, 20 × 0.3 MW inverters). Performance: ~9,000 MWh/year (~1,450 kWh/kWp — excellent for a Pacific site). The project demonstrated that utility solar was commercially viable in the Pacific at a scale comparable to comparable Caribbean projects; it became the reference design for ADB's subsequent Pacific solar programme (Fiji, Samoa, Vanuatu, Solomon Islands, Marshall Islands, Kiribati). Maama Mai's commissioning in 2012 — when utility-scale solar was still expensive globally — required ADB concessionality to bridge the LCOE gap; the same project today would be commercially viable without grant support.
Hunga Tonga eruption 2022: when a geological event meets an RE gridThe Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha'apai underwater caldera eruption (15 January 2022) was the largest recorded volcanic event since Pinatubo 1991 in terms of stratospheric aerosol injection and pressure wave propagation (the pressure wave circled the Earth multiple times, detectable on global weather stations). For Tonga specifically: tsunami up to 15 m on northern Tongatapu; volcanic ash fall covering Tongatapu, Ha'apai, and Vava'u 1-5 cm depth; international internet cable (TONGA cable, 827 km to Fiji) severed, leaving Tonga without internet connectivity for ~5 weeks; 3 deaths in Tonga from tsunami. Energy sector impact: volcanic ash coating solar panels across all Tongan islands reduced PV output to near-zero for 3-5 days; panel cleaning required significant labour mobilisation; Maama Mai and Atele solar farms required full panel cleaning and safety assessment before resuming operation. Unlike La Soufrière (St. Vincent), where the eruption was on the island itself and evacuation was required, Tonga's ash came from an offshore submarine eruption 65 km from Tongatapu — the ash hazard was severe but the structural damage to solar infrastructure was minimal (no lava, no pyroclastic flows).
TPL: a small but improving state utilityTonga Power Limited (state-owned) has made consistent progress in both RE integration and operational efficiency: billing collection improved from ~80% to ~93% (2015-2024); technical losses reduced from ~19% to ~14%; diesel plant maintenance improved (Popua Power Station, Nuku'alofa — refurbished 2019). TPL's BESS deployment (3 MW / 1.5 MWh, Tongatapu, 2018) was among the Pacific's first utility-scale battery storage systems — providing solar firming and frequency regulation. TPL's procurement process is constrained by Tonga's small economy (TPL's annual revenue ~US$35-40M, limiting commercial borrowing capacity) — ADB and WB concessional lending is the primary capital source. TPL's financial model: break-even at ~60% RE share on Tongatapu; at 70%+, fuel savings create operational surplus enabling reinvestment.
Outer island groups: variable RE progressTonga's outer island groups have very different RE trajectories: Vava'u (population ~15,000, tourism-oriented, main town Neiafu): ADB solar hybrid (4 MW solar + 1.5 MW BESS, 2020) — approximately 50% RE share; good tourism-sector demand driving commercial solar adoption on resorts. Ha'apai (population ~12,000, flat coral islands, most cyclone-exposed): partial solar at Pangai; Cyclone Harold 2020 damaged infrastructure; rebuilding to Category 4 standards. 'Eua (population ~5,000, elevated volcanic island): wind turbines (600 kW, ADB, 2010 — one of Pacific's early island wind projects) + diesel; ~25% RE share. The Niuas (Niuatoputapu + Niuafo'ou, population ~2,000 total, extremely isolated): diesel mini-grids; SHS for most households; no formal grid RE to date.
70% RE target and the wind-solar complementTonga's 70% RE by 2030 target for Tongatapu (currently ~60%) requires approximately 5-8 MW additional RE. Options: additional utility solar (Tongatapu has some suitable flat land) or wind additions (Trade Wind corridors are consistent, 7-8 m/s annual average at suitable sites). Wind and solar are complementary in Tonga: trade winds are strongest May-October (when solar irradiance is lowest), creating a natural seasonal complement that reduces BESS requirements compared to solar-only RE deployment. TPL's modelling indicates a 60% solar / 40% wind RE mix requires approximately 30-40% less BESS than 100% solar for the same RE share. The planned additional 5 MW wind expansion (Tongatapu, ADB financing under discussion 2025-2026) would bring Tongatapu to ~70% RE with the existing BESS fleet — achievable by 2028.

The 2022 Eruption: Tonga's Energy Resilience Test

The Hunga Tonga eruption uniquely combined volcanic, tsunami, and telecommunications disaster in a single event — testing TPL's resilience across multiple dimensions simultaneously:

  • Electricity infrastructure survived: Unlike the Caribbean's La Soufrière (which is on a populated volcanic island), Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha'apai is 65 km offshore — ash fell but no lava or pyroclastic flows reached inhabited islands. Solar panels at Maama Mai and Atele required cleaning but were not structurally damaged. TPL's Popua diesel plant (which does not depend on solar) maintained supply for critical services during the ash-cleaning period.
  • Internet outage amplified energy sector impact: The 5-week internet cable outage disconnected TPL from its remote monitoring and cloud-based SCADA systems. TPL's grid management fell back on manual operations for the most critical period — demonstrating that energy sector digitalisation without local backup systems creates resilience risk in cable-dependent SIDS.
  • Recovery pace: Solar panel cleaning was completed island-wide within approximately 5-7 days using TPL teams and community volunteers. Full solar output restored within 10 days of the eruption. The resilience of Tonga's solar infrastructure to this unprecedented event strengthened confidence in RE investment across the Pacific.
Tonga's Maama Mai Solar Farm — commissioned in 2012 when Pacific utility solar was a demonstration project — is now simply part of the background infrastructure of a grid that has evolved to 60% RE in 12 years. The 2022 Hunga Tonga eruption, which temporarily disabled every solar panel on the island, validated rather than undermined this investment: panels cleaned, grid restored, and diesel backup bridged the gap without prolonged outage. The Pacific's RE transition has a resilience track record, not just a financial one.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Tonga's Maama Mai Solar Farm (2012) established the Pacific's first utility-scale solar proof-of-concept — demonstrating at a commercially meaningful scale, in a real Pacific grid, with real grid management challenges, that solar could reach 30-40% penetration without grid instability — and its 12-year operating track record is the most credible evidence base for Pacific utilities still hesitant about high solar penetration in isolated island systems. ADB and WB staff consistently cite Maama Mai's operational data (availability, generation profile, grid frequency records, maintenance cost) when briefing Pacific utility executives who are uncertain about solar at scale. The farm has operated without a major failure for 12 years in a salt-air tropical environment — dispelling the concern that Pacific climates are uniquely hostile to solar equipment. The 12-year operational record is more persuasive than any number of feasibility studies.
2
The complementarity of solar and wind in Tonga — where trade winds are strongest in the dry season (May-October) when solar irradiance is lower, and solar is highest in the wet season (November-April) when winds are lighter — is a design principle that reduces battery storage requirements by 30-40% compared to solar-only RE portfolios, and Tonga's wind-solar hybrid Tongatapu grid is the Pacific's best operational demonstration of this complementarity benefit. Pacific energy planners frequently default to solar-only utility RE procurement because solar has lower capital risk and well-established Pacific operational precedents. But the wind-solar complementarity in the trade wind belt (Tonga, Samoa, Kiribati, Marshall Islands) means that including wind reduces the system's BESS requirement — and BESS is currently the most expensive component of Pacific island 100% RE systems. The cost saving from wind-solar complementarity can be significant enough to make a mixed portfolio cheaper than solar-only at 70%+ RE penetration.
3
The Hunga Tonga eruption's 5-week internet outage — which forced TPL to manage a 60% RE grid manually without cloud-based SCADA, real-time monitoring, or remote support from ADB technical assistance teams — revealed that Pacific island grid digitisation without local backup (offline SCADA, satellite-linked secondary monitoring, locally trained grid control operators) creates a resilience gap that is invisible during normal operations but acute during telecommunications disruptions. Every Pacific SIDS relying on undersea cable for internet connectivity faces this risk — cable breaks from seismic events, anchors, or fishing vessels happen periodically across the Pacific. TPL's response — manual grid operations by trained local staff — worked because TPL had experienced operators who could manage the grid without real-time digital support. Pacific utilities that have digitalised without maintaining manual operations capability are more vulnerable. Satellite internet (Starlink in particular, now available across the Pacific) is reducing this risk but has not yet been universally adopted by Pacific utilities for backup grid management.
4
Tonga's outer island group energy inequality — particularly the contrast between Tongatapu (60% RE, reliable supply) and Ha'apai (partial solar, Cyclone Harold-damaged, less reliable) — reflects a systematic pattern in Pacific archipelago states where the capital island's RE progress masks continuing energy poverty in outer island groups, and TPL's capital allocation should explicitly require Ha'apai's energy infrastructure rebuild to meet the same RE standards as post-2022 Tongatapu infrastructure. Ha'apai's flat coral atoll topography makes it ideal for solar — no terrain constraints, excellent irradiance, manageable peak demand (~10 MW). The barrier is not technical but financial: post-Harold reconstruction has been slow (aid funds competed across multiple Pacific countries also affected) and Ha'apai's population decline (outmigration to Tongatapu and New Zealand) reduces the apparent priority of the investment. A 5 MW solar + 2 MW BESS addition on Ha'apai would bring it to near-100% RE — the logical next capital investment for TPL after Tongatapu's 70% target is reached.
5
Tonga's experience with back-to-back extraordinary events — Cyclone Harold (2020) and Hunga Tonga eruption/tsunami (2022) — within 18 months demonstrates that for Pacific SIDS, resilience planning must account for the compound risk of multiple simultaneous or sequential disasters rather than designing for individual events, and that this compound risk justifies higher infrastructure standards (and higher capital cost) than single-event analysis would imply. Standard infrastructure risk analysis designs for a 1-in-50-year or 1-in-100-year event. Tonga experienced two extraordinary events within 18 months — not because Tonga is uniquely unlucky but because Pacific SIDS face multiple independent risk types (cyclone, earthquake, volcanic, tsunami, drought) that compound over any 20-year planning horizon. Tonga's electricity infrastructure must be designed for the compound scenario: Category 5 cyclone followed by volcanic ash fall 18 months later. This compound resilience standard has higher capital cost but is appropriate given the demonstrated frequency of compounding events.
