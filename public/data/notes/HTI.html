Sector Overview

Haiti's electricity sector is in a state of acute, multi-dimensional crisis that defies conventional energy sector analysis. Électricité d'Haïti (EDH) — the state utility founded in 1971 — has seen its operational capacity collapse over 2022–2025 as gang violence made fuel deliveries, maintenance, and collections impossible in large parts of Port-au-Prince and regional cities. Generation from EDH plants (mostly HFO-fired turbines and diesel sets at Varreux and Cap-Haïtien) operates at a fraction of nominal capacity. The 2010 earthquake destroyed significant infrastructure; rebuilding has been repeatedly disrupted by political instability, the 2021 assassination of President Moïse, and gang takeover of major urban areas. The practical reality for most Haitians: electricity is primarily provided by individual diesel gensets, solar lanterns, and off-grid solar home systems — not by a functional grid. The formal electricity sector is a shell institution managing what remains of grid infrastructure.

EDH: institutional collapseÉlectricité d'Haïti; state monopoly; ~500 employees (many unpaid); Varreux thermal plant (Port-au-Prince, ~230 MW nominal, heavily degraded); Cap-Haïtien thermal (northern Haiti); Péligre hydroelectric (54 MW, Artibonite River — Haiti's largest single power asset; intermittently operational). EDH billing collection rate: estimated <20% in areas under gang influence. Fuel theft at Varreux forced repeated shutdowns 2023-2025. International donors (USAID, IDB) providing emergency fuel and maintenance — but delivery logistics compromised by gang roadblocks on key fuel routes.
Gang control: the electricity security crisisG9 and Viv Ansanm gang coalitions control Port-au-Prince's Cité Soleil, Martissant, and key road corridors to Varreux power plant. Gangs extract payments from fuel trucks, steal electrical cables from distribution lines, and have attacked EDH substations. Metropolitan Port-au-Prince — when receiving grid power — typically gets 4-8 hours/day during periods of relative stability; 0-2 hours during acute crises. Cap-Haïtien (north) and Jacmel (south coast) are relatively more stable. Security normalisation is a prerequisite for any electricity sector recovery.
Solar mini-grids: the humanitarian access pathwayInternational donors (WB, IDB, USAID, AFD) and NGOs (EarthSpark International, ENDEV) are financing solar mini-grids and solar home systems (SHS) in areas where grid restoration is infeasible. EarthSpark's Les Anglais mini-grid (southern Haiti) serves 3,000+ households with 24/7 power — becoming a SIDS humanitarian energy model. SHS distribution: sub-$100 solar lanterns and portable solar kits reaching rural households with no formal electricity. The solar SHS market in Haiti operates largely outside formal energy policy framework, driven by NGO procurement and remittance-funded household purchases.
Péligre hydro: strategic but vulnerablePéligre dam and powerhouse (54 MW, Artibonite River, central Haiti); commissioned 1971; Haiti's only significant hydro asset; feeds Artibonite valley agricultural areas as well as Port-au-Prince via 138 kV line. Dam and powerhouse infrastructure aging; rehabilitation needed. Access road security compromised. Artibonite River water levels seasonal. Péligre can supply ~200-300 GWh/year in good conditions — disproportionately important given EDH thermal plant failures. Protecting and rehabilitating Péligre is the highest-value existing electricity asset available to Haiti.
Remittance economy and energyHaiti receives ~US$4B+/year in remittances (2023-24) — exceeding all other income sources combined. Remittance-receiving households disproportionately purchase private diesel gensets, solar lanterns, and solar home systems. The informal energy economy (private gensets, neighbours paying informal distributors, SHS) serves perhaps 20-25% of the population that the formal grid does not. Remittances are the de facto electricity financing mechanism for middle-income urban Haitians.

Context: Compound Crisis and Energy Poverty

Haiti's electricity crisis cannot be understood outside its compound political and humanitarian context:

  • 2010 earthquake (magnitude 7.0): Killed ~220,000 people, destroyed Port-au-Prince's Varreux substation and distribution infrastructure; $8B in aid pledged but disbursement slow; EDH rebuilt with IDB/WB support over 2010-2015 — but to lower capacity than pre-earthquake.
  • 2021: President Moïse assassination + August earthquake: July 2021 assassination of President Jovenel Moïse triggered political vacuum; August 2021 southern earthquake (M7.2) destroyed Cap-Haïtien and Les Cayes distribution infrastructure. Compound shock overwhelmed EDH's institutional capacity.
  • 2022-2025: gang state: Gang federations (G9, Viv Ansanm) progressively took control of Port-au-Prince's key arteries and fuel logistics infrastructure. Prime Minister Henry resigned March 2024 under gang pressure; Presidential Transitional Council established with Kenya-led Multinational Security Support Mission deployed 2024-25 to restore state authority. As of mid-2025: security improving in Port-au-Prince corridor but significant gang-controlled areas remain.
  • Electricity access by wealth quintile: Top 20% of Haitian households: ~70-80% grid access; Bottom 40%: <5% grid access, primarily served by solar lanterns and community gensets. Haiti's electricity inequality is extreme even by SIDS standards.
Haiti's energy situation requires a different analytical framework than any other SIDS case. This is not a country facing a difficult energy transition — it is a country where the energy sector has effectively ceased to function as a state-provided service for most citizens. The relevant questions are not about RE targets or grid decarbonisation timelines, but about how energy access can be delivered in a context of state fragility: through solar mini-grids, solar home systems, community energy cooperatives, and remittance-financed informal energy markets. Haiti's energy recovery is inseparable from its political and security recovery.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Haiti's electricity challenge is fundamentally a state fragility problem, not an energy technology problem — and no amount of solar panel procurement, grid rehabilitation, or tariff reform will resolve the electricity crisis without a prior restoration of physical security that allows fuel delivery, infrastructure maintenance, and bill collection to function. Every electricity intervention in Haiti over the past decade has underperformed projections because the energy sector analysis was technically sound but the security and governance baseline was not achievable. Solar mini-grids in gang-controlled urban areas have been vandalized; distribution cables have been stolen; fuel trucks have been blocked. Security restoration is the pre-condition for every other energy investment — yet energy investments are often presented as contributing to security. The sequencing problem is real and acknowledged too rarely in energy access programming.
2
EarthSpark International's Les Anglais mini-grid — 24/7 solar-powered community electricity in southern Haiti — is the most important SIDS energy access demonstration project of the past decade, because it proves that community-owned, solar-powered mini-grids can deliver universal electricity access in a fragile state context when designed with genuine community ownership and appropriate cost recovery mechanisms. Les Anglais (pop. ~12,000) receives round-the-clock solar electricity managed by a community energy cooperative. The key design elements: community equity stake, tiered pricing with cross-subsidy for poorest households, local technician training, smart metering for pre-payment. Operating since 2016; survived multiple political crises because the community has ownership and motivation to protect the system. This model is directly replicable across Haiti's 570+ rural communes.
3
The informal energy economy — private diesel gensets, neighbourhood distribution rings, solar home systems bought with remittances — serves more Haitians than the formal EDH grid and should be recognised, supported, and regulated rather than treated as a temporary gap to be eventually displaced by grid extension. Haiti's informal energy market has price discovery, customer service quality competition, and payment innovation (mobile money for SHS payments via Digicel/Natcom) that EDH's formal grid cannot match. Formalising the informal energy economy (quality standards for solar products, safety regulations for gensets, consumer protection for pay-as-you-go SHS) would improve outcomes for millions of Haitians faster than any grid rehabilitation programme.
4
Péligre hydroelectric dam (54 MW) is Haiti's most critical energy infrastructure asset — technically operational, geographically remote from urban gang violence, and capable of supplying 200-300 GWh/year of clean electricity — making its rehabilitation and protection the highest-return existing energy investment available to Haiti and to international donors. A $50-80M Péligre rehabilitation programme (turbine overhaul, transmission line protection, operations staff security) would restore 40-50 MW of firm clean hydro to Haiti's grid — equivalent to adding 200 MW of solar in terms of actual reliable supply. Péligre's remote Artibonite location provides more security than urban thermal plants. The dam was funded by the US in the 1970s and represents the one piece of large-scale energy infrastructure Haiti still has functional.
5
Haiti's ~2,000 kWh/m²/year solar irradiance — among the Western Hemisphere's highest — combined with falling solar home system costs means that universal basic electricity access (lighting, phone charging, small appliances) is achievable within 5-7 years through SHS deployment alone, independent of grid recovery, if a scaled national SHS programme is funded and implemented through community channels. A SHS delivering 50-100 Wp of solar panel, 50Ah battery, LED lights, and USB charging costs $80-150 landed in Haiti. At $100/unit × 3 million unserved households = $300M total — equivalent to two years of current international aid to Haiti's energy sector, deployed differently. A nationally coordinated SHS programme (WB, IDB, AFD, diaspora finance) with local assembly, quality certification, and mobile payment integration could achieve near-universal basic electricity access independent of EDH grid recovery — creating the energy foundation on which economic activity can eventually rebuild.
