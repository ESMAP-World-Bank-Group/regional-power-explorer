Generation Mix & Capacity

Iceland has the highest electricity consumption per capita in the world and generates effectively all of it from renewables. Hydropower supplies roughly 70% and geothermal the remainder, with no fossil generation of any consequence outside small island back-up sets.

The system exists largely to serve industry. Around 80% of demand comes from power-intensive users, overwhelmingly aluminium smelters at Grundartangi, Straumsvík and Reyðarfjörður, plus ferrosilicon and, more recently, data centres. Long-term contracts with those users, historically indexed to aluminium prices and now increasingly to market benchmarks, are what underwrite generation investment.

Iceland is an electrical island. The IceLink subsea cable to Great Britain has been studied for decades and never built, partly on cost and partly because domestic opinion is divided on exporting a resource seen as the basis for domestic industry. Without an interconnector, surplus in wet years is spilled and dry years are managed by curtailing interruptible industrial load.

Market Structure & Institutions

  • Orkustofnun: The National Energy Authority licenses projects and regulates network tariffs.
  • Landsvirkjun: State-owned and dominant in generation; its industrial contracts effectively set the country's wholesale price level. Reykjavík Energy and HS Orka make up most of the balance.
  • Landsnet: The transmission system operator, and Iceland's representative in ENTSO-E despite the absence of any physical link to the continent.
  • No wholesale spot market of consequence: With a handful of generators and a few very large buyers on long contracts, price formation is bilateral rather than exchange-based. A small internal market platform exists but carries limited volume.
  • Master Plan for nature protection and energy: A parliamentary framework classifying potential hydro and geothermal sites into protected, on hold, or available. It is the main constraint on new development and is politically renegotiated each cycle.
  • ENTSO-E membership without interconnection: Landsnet participates in pan-European network planning and codes, which matters mainly for standards and for keeping the IceLink option procedurally alive.
Iceland is the only fully renewable, fully isolated system in ENTSO-E. Its problem is not decarbonising generation, which is finished, but deciding what its clean energy is for: domestic heavy industry at contracted prices, data centres and hydrogen, or exports through a cable that would expose domestic consumers to continental prices. That is a political question, not a technical one.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
An industrial system, not a consumer system. With 80% of demand in a handful of plants, Icelandic electricity policy is industrial policy. Contract renegotiations with smelters move the sector's economics more than any generation project.
2
Isolation is a deliberate position, not an accident. IceLink is technically feasible and has been studied repeatedly. It has not been built because exporting would raise domestic prices toward European levels and erode the comparative advantage that attracted the smelters in the first place.
3
Hydrological risk is real even at 100% renewable. Low-inflow winters have forced curtailment of interruptible industrial load. A system with no thermal backstop and no interconnector has only demand-side flexibility to fall back on.
4
New generation is limited by planning, not by resource. The Master Plan process is where projects live or die. Remaining hydro and geothermal potential is substantial, and access to it is a recurring parliamentary fight over landscape and conservation.
5
Data centres and hydrogen are the proposed next demand, with the same debate attached. Both would absorb energy domestically rather than export it, which is politically easier than a cable. Whether they deliver comparable value per megawatt-hour to aluminium is unproven.
