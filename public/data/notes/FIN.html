Generation Mix & Sector Overview

Finland's electricity sector underwent a structural transformation with the full commissioning of Olkiluoto 3 (OL3) — at 1,600 MW, Europe's largest nuclear reactor — in 2023. Nuclear's share of Finnish electricity rose from 28% in 2022 to 39% in 2024, with Olkiluoto alone producing 23.26 TWh in 2025 (25% of national consumption). Wind power surged in parallel, from 14% in 2022 to 24% in 2024, and is continuing to grow rapidly. Finland's total generation mix in 2025 is approximately: nuclear 37%, wind 26%, hydro 20%, biomass/CHP 12%, and negligible fossil fuels. The country is a net electricity exporter within the Nordic Nordpool market. A historic milestone was reached in February 2025, when the Baltic states (Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania) and Finland desynchronised from the Russian BRELL grid and synchronised with the European continental grid.

The Fennovoima Cancellation and Strategic Recalibration

Finland's energy strategy was transformed by two events: the completion of Olkiluoto 3 and the cancellation of the Fennovoima nuclear project:

  • Fennovoima cancelled (May 2022): A third nuclear project at Pyhäjoki, originally planned with Rosatom (Russian state nuclear company) as the reactor supplier, was cancelled following Russia's invasion of Ukraine. The project lost its raison d'être — its main industrial backer (Fortum-linked consortium) and its technology supplier simultaneously became politically untenable. Finland lost ~1.2 GW of planned new nuclear capacity.
  • New nuclear search: Finland is now evaluating alternative new nuclear projects — potentially with Rolls-Royce SMRs, Westinghouse AP1000, or Korean APR-1400 technology. Multiple sites under consideration. Timeline: earliest 2035.
  • Russian grid severance: Finland historically imported electricity from Russia via a 1,600 MW connection. This was cut in May 2022 (Russia cut off exports). The subsequent grid desynchronisation from BRELL in February 2025 represents the definitive elimination of this energy security dependency.
  • Estlink vulnerability: The Estlink 1 submarine cable (350 MW, Finland-Estonia) suffered suspected sabotage in December 2024, cutting one of the key links in the Baltic electricity exchange. Investigation ongoing; highlights hybrid warfare risk to critical energy infrastructure.
Finland's Olkiluoto 3 experience is the most important lesson for countries planning new large nuclear: the project took 18 years from decision to full commercial operation (2005–2023), cost roughly 3× the original budget (€3B estimated → ~€11B final), and required a complete legal and financial restructuring of the contractor (Areva went bankrupt during construction). Yet it is now operational, reliable, and produces a quarter of Finland's electricity cleanly. The lesson is not that nuclear is easy — it is that nuclear completion timelines and costs must be planned with extreme caution, and that "first-of-a-kind" projects carry existential financial risks for contractors.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Olkiluoto 3's completion — after 18 years and a 3× cost overrun — is both a vindication of nuclear persistence and a cautionary tale for new nuclear projects everywhere. The reactor is now one of Europe's most productive, generating 25% of Finnish electricity. But the journey involved Areva's bankruptcy, TVO's lawsuit against Areva (settled for €450M in 2018), Finnish taxpayers absorbing cost risk, and nearly a decade of delays. Countries planning new nuclear must price in this construction risk explicitly — not assume it will be better managed next time without institutional reforms.
2
The Baltic–Finnish desynchronisation from the Russian BRELL grid (February 2025) is the most consequential European energy security infrastructure achievement since Russian gas supply was cut after 2022. For 50+ years, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, and Finland operated power systems technically controlled by Moscow — Russia could manipulate frequency, cause outages, and threaten grid stability as geopolitical leverage. The synchronisation with ENTSO-E eliminates this entirely. It required 15 years of planning, €1.6B of EU investment, and precise technical execution. It is a model for how energy security can be achieved through infrastructure rather than conflict.
3
Finland's wind energy acceleration — from 14% to 24% of generation in just two years — demonstrates that rapid scaling of variable RE in a high-latitude, forested country is technically and economically feasible. The common argument that northern countries face unique challenges for renewables (low solar, challenging terrain, cold supply chains) is contradicted by Finland's wind buildout. Finnish onshore wind resources are excellent; permitting reforms in 2022-23 accelerated project timelines; and the combination with Olkiluoto 3's firm baseload gives the system robustness that pure-wind systems lack.
4
The Estlink sabotage incident (December 2024) signals that submarine electricity cables are the next frontier of hybrid warfare targeting energy infrastructure in Europe. Following the Nord Stream pipeline explosions (2022) and Baltic data cable cuts (2023-24), the Estlink cable damage fits a pattern of infrastructure targeting. For Finland and the Baltics — which depend on submarine cables for electricity trade with the Nordic market — this represents a new dimension of energy security risk that requires physical protection, redundancy planning, and rapid-repair capabilities that did not exist in previous energy security frameworks.
5
Finland's energy story — successful nuclear, fast wind growth, Russian grid decoupling, and industrial clean energy demand — makes it a more instructive reference case for emerging markets than Germany or France. Finland is a smaller, colder, resource-constrained country that nonetheless achieved a clean, secure, affordable electricity system through technology diversity and pragmatic policy. For Bank clients in Eastern Europe, Central Asia, or high-latitude developing countries, Finland's approach (nuclear baseload + wind growth + regional integration) offers a more replicable model than the German Energiewende or the French near-100% nuclear model.
