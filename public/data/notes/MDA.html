Generation Mix & Capacity

Moldova's electricity question is geopolitical before it is technical. For decades the country bought the large majority of its power from the Cuciurgan (Moldavskaya GRES) plant, a roughly 2,500 MW gas-fired station located in Transnistria, the breakaway region on the left bank of the Dniester, running on Russian gas supplied without payment to Chișinäu.

That arrangement made cheap electricity a lever. When supply was reduced or priced up, as during the 2022 energy crisis and again during the gas cut-off at the start of 2025, Moldova had to buy emergency volumes from Romania at far higher cost, with immediate fiscal and political consequences.

The structural response has been integration westward. Moldova and Ukraine synchronised with the Continental European grid in March 2022, an emergency operation completed in weeks rather than the years it normally takes, and Moldova joined ENTSO-E's observer arrangements. The Vulcänești–Chișinäu 400 kV line and its back-to-back converter, the first real import capacity from Romania, is the physical expression of that shift.

Market Structure & Institutions

  • Ministry of Energy: Created as a standalone ministry in 2023, a sign of how far energy security rose up the political agenda after 2022.
  • ANRE: The National Agency for Energy Regulation sets tariffs and licenses market participants. Its independence has been contested and reinforced in successive reform rounds tied to EU accession commitments.
  • Moldelectrica: The transmission system operator, responsible for the synchronisation with Continental Europe and for the Romanian interconnections.
  • Energocom: The state trader, which became the central procurement vehicle during the crises, buying on regional markets when Cuciurgan supply was interrupted.
  • Energy Community and EU accession: Moldova is an Energy Community contracting party and an EU candidate, so market design, unbundling and network codes are converging on the EU acquis under a fixed timetable.
  • Transnistria: The left bank operates its own arrangements, consumes gas it does not pay for, and hosts the dominant plant. Any durable Moldovan energy settlement runs through that unresolved status.
Moldova is the clearest European example of electricity as an instrument of influence. A single plant in a breakaway region, fuelled by unpaid gas, supplied most of the country's power. Synchronisation with Continental Europe and the Romanian interconnection do not remove that dependence overnight, but for the first time they give Chișinäu an alternative it controls.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Synchronisation was a security decision executed at emergency speed. Joining the Continental European system in March 2022 took weeks instead of years. It is the strongest demonstration available that grid integration timetables are political constraints as much as technical ones.
2
Import capacity is worth more than domestic capacity here. Moldova cannot realistically build its way to self-sufficiency at this scale. The Romanian interconnections change its bargaining position more than any plant it could construct.
3
Dependence on Cuciurgan is a governance problem, not a market one. No tariff design fixes a supply relationship that runs through an unrecognised authority and unpaid gas. Diversification is the only available answer, and it is being pursued.
4
EU accession is driving the sector's rulebook. Unbundling, regulator independence and market coupling are being adopted on an accession timetable rather than on a domestic one, which accelerates reform but also imports design choices made for much larger systems.
5
Renewables are small, growing and strategically useful. Wind and solar are a few hundred megawatts, but every domestic megawatt-hour substitutes for an import bought under pressure. Their value to Moldova is measured in leverage, not only in cost.
