Generation Mix & Sector Overview

Slovenia operates one of the EU's most compact electricity systems (2 million people, ~14 TWh annual consumption) with a notably clean mix: nuclear ~37%, hydro ~35%, and the remainder split between gas and declining lignite. The nuclear share comes entirely from Krško Nuclear Power Plant (688 MW PWR, Westinghouse design, commissioned 1983) — jointly owned 50/50 with Croatia, meaning Slovenia's effective share is 344 MW. The Sava, Drava, and Soča rivers provide excellent run-of-river hydro. Lignite from the Šoštanj complex has historically provided baseload, but the Šoštanj coal plant is being phased out by 2033. The major utilities are GEN energija (nuclear + gas) and HSE (Holding Slovenske Elektrarne, hydro + coal). The TSO is ELES. Slovenia and Croatia jointly approved a second Krško unit (Krško 2, 1150 MW) in 2024 — the most significant energy investment decision in Slovenia's independent history.

Krško 2: Small Country, Enormous Infrastructure Bet

Slovenia's decision to build Krško 2 — jointly with Croatia — represents an infrastructure commitment equivalent to roughly 40% of Slovenia's GDP over the construction period. This is extraordinary for a country of 2 million people:

  • Financing challenge: GEN energija (Slovenia's nuclear operator) is a relatively small utility — it cannot finance €4-5B (Slovenia's 50% share of Krško 2) without significant state support, EU green bonds, and international financing. The EU taxonomy inclusion of nuclear (2022) is directly relevant: it allows Krško 2 bonds to qualify as sustainable investments, reducing financing costs. EIB (European Investment Bank) participation is being sought — EIB has historically avoided nuclear financing but is under pressure to review this post-taxonomy.
  • Energy security argument: Slovenia's case for Krško 2 rests on energy security: replacing ~600 MW of lignite (Šoštanj closing 2033) and eventually Krško 1 (licence to 2043) requires large baseload addition. Solar and wind alone cannot provide the winter firmness that a Central European grid requires without enormous storage investment that Slovenia cannot afford. Krško 2 provides 60 years of firm low-carbon baseload — the most capital-efficient solution available for a small landlocked country without significant wind or seasonal hydro storage.
  • Croatia's co-investment: Croatia's participation is equally motivated by Šoštanj replacement logic — Croatia uses its Krško 1 share as a reliable low-cost baseload source. Krško 2 gives both countries a clean baseload anchor through 2095 for a jointly managed €8-10B investment. Croatia's HEP utility is more financially robust than GEN energija and may carry a disproportionate share of financing burden.
Slovenia's energy story is quietly remarkable: a country of 2 million that operates a nuclear plant, manages four river cascade systems, and is now planning a second nuclear reactor — all while decarbonising a Soviet-era lignite sector. The Krško 2 decision will define Slovenia's energy future for the next 70 years. If it is built on time and budget (historically very difficult for European nuclear), Slovenia and Croatia will have the cleanest electricity grids in Central Europe. If it is delayed or cancelled, Slovenia faces a critical supply gap in the 2030s when Šoštanj closes and before sufficient RE is deployed.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Slovenia's decision to build Krško 2 — jointly with Croatia — is the most consequential energy infrastructure decision made by any EU member state of its size in the past two decades, representing a capital commitment equivalent to ~40% of GDP for a country of 2 million people. This is not a routine infrastructure project — it is a generational bet on nuclear technology, Westinghouse or equivalent Western vendor execution, EU financing support, and Croatia's long-term partnership. For comparison, this would be equivalent to a country like New Zealand committing to a €50B infrastructure project. The risk appetite required is extraordinary, and the political consensus across multiple Slovenian and Croatian governments required to sustain it over 12+ years of construction is not guaranteed.
2
The Šoštanj lignite Unit 6 is one of the EU's most striking stranded asset stories: a €1.4B power plant financed partly by EU loans, commissioned in 2016 (the year the Paris Agreement was signed), and already scheduled for closure in 2033 — giving it a useful life of less than 20 years of its intended 40-year design life. Šoštanj Unit 6 was EU-funded as part of Slovenia's "clean coal" modernisation — a rationale that was already questionable at FID in 2008-09 and was unambiguously wrong by commissioning in 2016. The EU funding decision for Šoštanj Unit 6 is among the clearest examples in Europe of public infrastructure investment in assets that were near-stranded at commissioning. The EU Just Transition Fund now partially compensates for this policy error.
3
Krško's Westinghouse PWR design — unlike VVER plants in the region — provides fuel supply independence from Russia, and is directly relevant to Central European energy security post-2022 as the region seeks to eliminate Russian nuclear dependencies. Krško runs on Westinghouse fuel. This means Slovenia and Croatia have no Russian nuclear fuel exposure — unlike Czech, Slovak, Hungarian, Bulgarian, and Finnish VVER operators. This is a genuine competitive advantage in the post-2022 energy security landscape. Krško 2 will also be a Western-vendor plant (Westinghouse, EDF, or KHNP), maintaining this independence. Slovenia's nuclear fleet is effectively Russian-supply-free — a distinction worth emphasising in EU energy security discussions.
4
Slovenia's Drava River cascade — shared with Austria and jointly operated with Verbund — is a model of transboundary hydro cooperation that predates EU energy market integration and demonstrates that cross-border electricity infrastructure partnerships can be durable, technically successful, and commercially equitable. The Drava cascade produces ~1,200 MW across both banks of the Slovenia-Austria border; output is split by agreed bilateral formula; operations are jointly coordinated. This arrangement has functioned since the Yugoslav-Austrian period and survived Slovenia's EU accession, market liberalisation, and Verbund's privatisation. It is a template for how the EU should structure cross-border RE projects — revenue sharing, joint operations, shared maintenance costs, and agreed dispatch priority.
5
Slovenia's solar and wind acceleration (post-2022) is necessary but cannot substitute for Krško 2 in providing the winter baseload security that Slovenia's climate and geography require — making Krško 2 not a political choice but a technical necessity for a landlocked, alpine country with no significant wind resource and no inter-seasonal storage. Slovenia's solar irradiance is good (1,250 kWh/m²/year) but peaks in summer when demand is lowest; winter solar output is minimal. Wind resources are limited by the Alps and Dinarics — exposed ridgelines exist but are few. Without large-scale seasonal battery storage (not commercially viable at the required scale before 2040), Slovenia's winter peak demand will require either nuclear, gas imports, or a power purchase arrangement with Austria or Croatia. Krško 2 is the only self-sufficient option.
