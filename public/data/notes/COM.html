Generation Mix & Sector Overview

The Comoros — three main volcanic islands (Grande Comore/Ngazidja, Anjouan/Nzwani, and Mohéli/Mwali) at the northern entrance to the Mozambique Channel, plus the contested island of Mayotte (a French overseas department) — is one of the most energy-impoverished SIDS in the Indian Ocean region. With a per-capita GNI of approximately US$1,400 and a population of approximately 900,000, Comoros is among Africa's poorest countries. Its electricity sector reflects this depth of structural underdevelopment: the state utility MA-MWE (Maji na Meme, Comorian for "Water and Electricity") is technically incapable, financially insolvent, and politically compromised — delivering electricity in Moroni (Grande Comore capital) for only 6-12 hours per day, with load shedding of 12-18 hours daily that has persisted for over a decade. On outer islands and rural areas, electricity access rates drop significantly below the official national figure (~75%).

Each of the three main islands operates as an independent grid. Grande Comore (Moroni, ~400,000 people): diesel thermal (~8 MW effective, chronically underpowered relative to ~15 MW peak demand), small solar (~1 MW ADB), chronic fuel shortages that trigger extended blackouts. Anjouan (~250,000 people): better situated — several small rivers enable run-of-river hydro (~4-6 MW, seasonal), supplemented by diesel and small solar; Anjouan generally has 12-18 hours of supply, better than Grande Comore. Mohéli (~50,000 people): the smallest and most rural island; diesel mini-grid in Fomboni (capital), solar home systems elsewhere; supply typically 8-12 hours. A GEF-funded Mohéli solar hybrid project (2020) has improved supply reliability on this island significantly.

Data quality caveat: MA-MWE does not publish reliable annual reports, and generation data for Comoros is estimated from ADB, IsDB, and World Bank project documentation rather than official utility statistics. Capacity figures (variously reported as 60-120 MW) conflate nameplate capacity of aging, partially non-functional plant with effective available generation. The analysis below uses donor programme estimates, which are more conservative and more credible.

Mt. Karthala geothermal: 50+ MW beneath the world's most active volcanoMt. Karthala — Grande Comore's shield volcano (2,361 m, last major eruption 2005-2007) — sits atop one of the most active volcanic systems in the Indian Ocean region. Geothermal surveys (UNDP 2014-2018, French geological surveys) have identified hydrothermal systems at approximately 2,000-3,000 m depth with reservoir temperatures estimated at 200-250°C — sufficient for high-temperature geothermal generation. Estimated developable resource: 50-70 MW, which would supply approximately 3-5× Grande Comore's current effective generation capacity. The development barrier is not geological but institutional and political: geothermal development requires (1) 3-5 years of exploratory drilling (approximately US$20-30M) before resource confirmation; (2) a utility operator capable of managing a complex, capital-intensive resource; (3) political stability allowing long-term concession agreements; and (4) development finance willing to take exploration risk in a politically unstable, low-income country. None of these conditions is currently met. If Comoros achieves political stabilisation, Karthala geothermal is the transformational option — but it requires a decade-long development commitment that current governance doesn't support.
Political instability: 20+ coups, donor fatigue, and the governance-energy trapSince independence from France in 1975, Comoros has experienced more than 20 coup attempts or successful changes of government by extra-constitutional means — the world's highest per-capita coup rate. The political cycle has direct energy sector consequences: each change of government disrupts ongoing utility reform processes, cancels or reverises energy policies, and triggers renegotiation or abandonment of donor-financed projects. MA-MWE has undergone multiple restructuring attempts (1995, 2003, 2009, 2016, 2022) — each time producing a new organisational chart and a new management team without fundamentally improving operations. Donor fatigue is real: IDA (WB), ADB, and IsDB have all financed Comoros energy projects that underperformed due to governance collapse mid-implementation. The "governance-energy trap" (poor governance → poor utility → poor electricity → poor economic growth → poor revenue → poor governance) is difficult to break from outside — but each successful small project (the Mohéli solar hybrid, the IsDB Grande Comore solar farm) creates demonstrated alternatives that build political pressure for reform.
IsDB (Islamic Development Bank): the dominant energy donor in a Muslim-majority SIDSComoros is an Islamic republic (Sunni Muslim majority, constitutionally enshrined). The Islamic Development Bank (IsDB, Jeddah) has been Comoros' most consistent energy donor — financing diesel plant rehabilitation (2012), solar farm construction (2019-2021, ~1.5 MW Grande Comore), and energy sector capacity building. IsDB's engagement in Comoros reflects its member country focus (Comoros is an OIC member) and its development mandate in fragile Islamic states. For energy planning: IsDB's concessional lending terms (0.9-1.5% interest, 25-30 year tenure) are among the most favourable available to Comoros and are the primary vehicle for energy capital investment. ADB's Pacific Pacific Energy programme analogue for the Indian Ocean (including Comoros) is comparatively limited. The EU and France (bilateral) are secondary energy funders focused on rural electrification and solar. Saudi Fund for Development has financed grid infrastructure on Grande Comore.
Remittances and France diaspora: energy purchasing power beyond GDPComoros receives remittances approximately equal to 25-30% of GDP — one of the highest remittance-to-GDP ratios in Africa — primarily from the large Comorian diaspora in Marseille, France (estimated 300,000+ Comorians in the greater Marseille area, more than 30% of the home country's population). These remittances fund household consumption including private generators, solar panels, and mobile phone charging — a significant informal energy economy that sits alongside and partially substitutes for the failing MA-MWE grid. The scale of private household energy investment (generators, batteries, solar) in Moroni is a direct consequence of MA-MWE's unreliability: Comorian households self-supply approximately 30-40% of their electricity need through informal means, which reduces the political pressure for MA-MWE reform by providing a workaround — while perpetuating MA-MWE's low-volume, high-cost spiral.

Structural Reform: Breaking the Governance-Energy Trap

Comoros' electricity sector cannot be transformed without governance improvement — and governance improvement is not an electricity sector intervention. The most realistic near-term pathway is not grand reform but incremental resilience: island-by-island solar + battery hybrid systems that reduce the consequence of MA-MWE dysfunction (as demonstrated on Mohéli), combined with targeted support for Anjouan's hydro systems (which work and provide a better-performing model for the main grid). For Grande Comore and Moroni specifically, the IsDB solar + BESS expansion programme (2024-2027) offers the best near-term impact: 5-8 MW additional solar + 3 MW BESS would reduce Moroni's load shedding from 12-18 hours to 6-8 hours — a meaningful improvement even without fundamental MA-MWE reform.

Comoros is the Indian Ocean AIMS region's most acute energy access failure — a country where political instability has repeatedly derailed utility reform, where the world's most accessible geothermal resource (Karthala) sits undeveloped above 400,000 people living in near-constant darkness, and where remittances from the Marseille diaspora fund private generators that paper over a structural electricity failure. The path forward is incremental and island-specific: Mohéli's solar hybrid model, Anjouan's hydro extension, and Grande Comore's IsDB solar programme are achievable without political transformation and build demonstrated success that changes the political economy of energy reform over time.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Mt. Karthala's 50+ MW geothermal potential is Comoros' most transformational long-term energy asset, and the international geothermal community (GDF — Geothermal Development Facility, managed by AfDB; IRENA; WB) should finance a structured Karthala geothermal exploration programme now — beginning with surface surveys, then exploratory drilling — precisely because exploration risk financing during a period of relative political stability (2019-2026 under President Azali) maximises the probability that resource data survives the next political transition and creates the technical foundation for development regardless of which government eventually commits to it. Geothermal development has a 10-15 year lead time; starting exploration now means development is possible by 2035-2040. If exploration is delayed until political stability is guaranteed, it will never start — because Comoros has not had sustained political stability in 50 years. The exploration data itself (drilling results, reservoir temperature profiles) is durable across governments; it does not disappear with a coup. Financing exploration as a public good (GDF grant-financed, with data shared publicly) is the appropriate vehicle — not tied to a concession agreement that a future government might cancel.
2
Anjouan's small hydro system (4-6 MW run-of-river, seasonal) is the only subsystem of MA-MWE that functions reasonably well and provides a model for what Comorian electricity can look like — and investing in Anjouan hydro maintenance, rehabilitation, and expansion (additional run-of-river sites exist on the island's rivers) would improve electricity reliability on Anjouan while demonstrating to Grande Comore's political class and population that the electricity problem is solvable, creating demand for equivalent solutions on the main island. Anjouan's hydro provides 10-18 hours of supply on a good day — significantly better than Moroni's 6-12 hours. The contrast is politically relevant: Anjouanais point to their functioning hydro as evidence that Comorian electricity can work when infrastructure is maintained. ADB's Indian Ocean energy programme (which covers Comoros alongside Mauritius, Seychelles, and Maldives) should include an Anjouan hydro rehabilitation component as a quick-win demonstration alongside the longer-term Karthala geothermal exploration programme.
3
The informal private generator and solar panel economy in Moroni — where households and businesses have self-financed approximately 30-40% of their electricity supply through private backup generation rather than relying on MA-MWE — represents a demonstrated willingness to pay for reliable electricity that MA-MWE's tariff and service quality do not capture, and a formal solar + battery retail programme targeted at this existing private energy spend could redirect household investment from diesel generators to solar systems with immediate emissions and cost benefits. Comorian urban households that can afford a diesel generator (typically US$500-1,500 capital cost + ongoing fuel) are paying US$0.50-1.00/kWh effective for private backup power — 3-5× MA-MWE's tariff. Redirecting this household spending toward solar + battery systems (US$1,000-2,500, but zero fuel cost, 20-year life) would provide better reliability than a generator at lower long-run cost. A France Développement (AFD) or IsDB consumer solar finance programme — offering 18-24 month household loans for solar + battery home systems — would meet demonstrated demand while reducing Moroni's generator diesel consumption and noise pollution.
4
Comoros' classification as both a SIDS and an LDC (Least Developed Country) — one of very few countries to hold both designations simultaneously — entitles it to the most concessional tiers of climate and development finance (IDA grants, GCF lowest-income windows, LDC Fund), but these financing mechanisms remain underutilised in Comoros because MA-MWE lacks the project preparation and fiduciary capacity to absorb grant financing, making WB and UN investment in Comorian energy project preparation capacity (the "upstream" of financing) more valuable than the grants themselves. GCF (Green Climate Fund) has approved very limited funding for Comoros relative to its entitlement under LDC+SIDS provisions — not because the climate need is absent (it is acute) but because Comoros' government does not have the project preparation capacity to submit bankable GCF proposals. A dedicated "energy project preparation unit" (EPPU) embedded in the Comorian Ministry of Energy — staffed with two international technical advisors for 3-5 years, funded by UNDP/GCF Readiness — would produce multiple bankable GCF and IDA proposals. The cost (~US$1.5M) is modest relative to the mobilisation potential (US$50-100M in GCF grants).
5
Mayotte — a French overseas department administratively separate from Comoros but geographically identical (it is the fourth Comorian island, retained by France in the 1974 independence referendum) — operates a French-standard electricity grid (EDM, Électricité de Mayotte, partly RTE/EDF) with French tariff subsidies, 230V/50Hz, and near-100% access, providing a directly adjacent reference point for what Comorian electricity could look like under adequate institutional capacity and financing — and Comoros-France development dialogue should explicitly use the Mayotte electricity standard as the service quality target for donor-financed Comorian grid improvement. The Mayotte-Comoros electricity contrast is stark and politically sensitive: residents of Moroni (12-18 hour blackouts) can see Mayotte's lights across 70 km of water — a constant visual reminder of the governance and development gap between a French department and an independent state with a 50-year coup history. For donor messaging: using Mayotte's electricity quality as the peer benchmark (rather than Sub-Saharan African averages) sets a higher ambition for Comorian energy reform and reflects the legitimate aspiration of Comorians who compare their situation to French-administered Mayotte.
