1. Energy context

Kenya is one of the world's most renewable-intensive electricity systems, with approximately 84–90% of generation from renewables — a figure driven by the East African Rift Valley's exceptional geothermal resource and a cascade of hydro plants on the Tana River. The system is not a function of recent energy transition policy; it is a structural legacy of geology. Kenya sits atop one of the world's most productive geothermal corridors, and KenGen has developed the Olkaria complex (Naivasha) into the largest geothermal generating site in Africa, approaching 1 GW.

The country's two structural challenges sit at opposite ends of the value chain. On the supply side, Kenya Power (KPLC) — the sole transmission and distribution licensee — accumulated seven consecutive years of negative working capital through FY2022/23, driven by USD-denominated PPA obligations against a depreciating shilling, high system losses (~23%), and unpaid government debts (~KSh 26 billion). A cost-reflective tariff reform in April 2023 turned the company around (FY2023/24 profit: KSh 43.7 billion before tax), but the underlying structural issues — loss levels, IPP cost burden, low grid reliability — persist. On the demand side, 21% of the population remains without electricity, concentrated in arid northern counties and remote rural areas reachable only by off-grid solar.

Kenya's electrification rate rose from 37% in 2013 to 79% in 2023 — one of the fastest sustained expansions globally — largely through the Last Mile Connectivity Programme. The Ethiopia–Kenya HVDC interconnector (commercial operations December 2023) is the most important regional infrastructure development, adding cheap Ethiopian hydro to the Kenyan mix and positioning Kenya as a transit hub within EAPP.

Kenya's geothermal potential is estimated at 10 GW.
  • Hydro (24% capacity / 21% generation): Seven Forks cascade on the Tana River (~600 MW: Masinga 40 MW, Kamburu 94 MW, Gitaru 225 MW, Kindaruma 72 MW, Kiambere 168 MW). Subject to drought-year output drops. High Grand Falls (700 MW, lower Tana) is being revived under a PPP framework; transaction advisor RfP issued March 2026.
  • Wind (12% capacity / 16% generation): Lake Turkana Wind Power (310 MW, Loiyangalani, Vestas V52) — Africa's largest single wind farm, generating ~1,367–1,481 GWh/year. USD-denominated PPA adds forex cost to KPLC.
  • Solar (12% capacity / 4% generation): ~440 MW grid-connected by end-2023. First utility-scale solar auctions under the Renewable Energy Auction Policy (REAP), replacing feed-in tariffs from 2021. Kenya is Africa's leading off-grid solar market — birthplace of PAYGo financing (M-Kopa, 2012).
  • Thermal (19% capacity / 10% generation): Diesel/HFO peakers and IPP thermal plants (Rabai 90 MW, Thika 87 MW, Tsavo 74 MW, Iberafrica ~108 MW). High operating cost (~$150–250/MWh) means dispatch only for peak and emergency. Senate has called for renegotiating thermal IPP PPAs.
Kenya's renewable share (~84–90%) is among the highest nationally-weighted figures in sub-Saharan Africa. This is structural, not aspirational — it reflects the geology of the Rift Valley and the Tana River, not a recent policy choice. The system operates on near-zero-carbon baseload from geothermal, with hydro and wind as complements and thermal as expensive backup.

Peak demand reached a new record of 2,316 MW in February 2025 — growth driven by Last Mile connections, ICT sector, and EV charging. System losses remain stubbornly high at 23.47% (EPRA benchmark: 18.5%), driven by commercial losses (meter tampering, theft, billing irregularities) as much as technical losses. Grid reliability: SAIDI 9.42 hours/customer/month, SAIFI 3.57 outages/month — both missing EPRA benchmarks and constraining industrial investment.

Tariffs (2024): Residential lifeline ~KSh 12/kWh (~$0.09/kWh); standard domestic KSh 26–31.75/kWh (~$0.20–0.24/kWh); large industrial KSh 13.90–16.48/kWh (~$0.10–0.13/kWh). Kenya's electricity is among the most expensive in sub-Saharan Africa relative to GDP per capita — a persistent competitiveness constraint for industry. Multiple levies (fuel cost adjustment, forex adjustment, RELevies, VAT) add to the base tariff.

2.4 Kenya Power — from near-collapse to turnaround

Financial distress and the April 2023 reform Structural

Kenya Power (KPLC) posted a net loss in FY2022/23 (KSh 3.2 billion), its seventh consecutive year of negative working capital. The auditor raised going-concern doubts. Root causes: USD-denominated PPA obligations (including Lake Turkana's dollar-indexed contract) while revenues are in KES; ~KSh 26 billion in unpaid debts from government agencies; commercial losses at 23.5% of units purchased. The April 2023 cost-reflective tariff reform turned the company around dramatically: FY2023/24 profit before tax was KSh 43.7 billion. But the structural issues — forex mismatch, system losses, IPP cost burden — remain. The FY2024 "profit" also incorporated significant reversal of previously unrealized forex losses as the KES stabilised in H2 2024 rather than representing purely operational improvement.

Ethiopia imports — strategic shift Growing

Ethiopian hydro via the HVDC interconnector is structurally cheaper than Kenyan thermal peakers (~$30–40/MWh vs. $150–250/MWh). Kenya imported 751.95 GWh in H2 2024 alone and plans to double contracted volumes to ~400 MW by 2026. This import strategy reduces thermal dispatch and lowers KPLC's average purchase cost — but creates a new strategic dependency on Ethiopian generation reliability and the HVDC line's availability. It also positions Kenya as a transit hub if the Tanzania link is completed, enabling arbitrage between Ethiopian hydro and SAPP markets.

3. Sector organisation and actors

3.1 Market structure

Kenya's electricity sector is formally unbundled. KenGen (74.1% government-owned, publicly listed) generates ~75% of dispatched electricity. IPPs contribute ~33% of installed capacity (~1,163 MW). KETRACO (100% government) owns and operates new high-voltage transmission assets. Kenya Power (KPLC) is the single buyer of bulk power and sole retail distributor (~50.1% government, publicly listed). EPRA is the independent regulator. REREC manages rural electrification and off-grid programs (replacing REA under the Energy Act 2019).

In practice the sector retains a single-buyer structure: EETC/KPLC purchases all power and sells to end-users. The 2019 Energy Act introduced bilateral PPA rights for large industrial consumers (≥1,500 kVA demand), but the framework is nascent. KPLC is restructuring its balance sheet by transferring KSh 70.4 billion in legacy transmission assets to KETRACO to deleverage.

4. Key issues and dynamics

4.1 Geothermal — Africa's deepest competitive advantage

  • Kenya's geothermal resource is unique in Africa: ~985 MW installed across Olkaria alone, a national potential of ~10 GW, and a geological corridor (Rift Valley) extending north toward Baringo, Silali, Korosi, and Paka — sites GDC is actively drilling. Geothermal produces ~47% of Kenya's electricity while consuming no fuel and emitting near-zero carbon.
  • Menengai (GDC steam-sales model) broke new ground in August 2023 when Sosian Energy commissioned the first 35 MW IPP unit using GDC-supplied steam. The remaining two Menengai units (2 × 35 MW) are in development. Olkaria VII (80.3 MW, KenGen) has been approved.
  • The binding constraint on geothermal expansion is not geological potential but drilling cost (~$5–7 million/well) and the capital-intensive surface infrastructure. GDC's steam-sales model de-risks exploration for private IPPs — replicating this at Baringo-Silali is the key near-term ambition.

4.2 Kenya Power — structural repair underway but incomplete

  • The April 2023 tariff reform delivered a dramatic financial turnaround for KPLC (from KSh 3.2 billion net loss to KSh 43.7 billion PBT). But ~KSh 20+ billion of that swing reflects accounting reversal of unrealised forex losses as the KES stabilised — not operational improvement.
  • System losses at 23.47% remain far above the EPRA benchmark of 18.5%. The 5% gap represents roughly 625 GWh/year of electricity stolen or unbilled — equivalent to the output of a mid-sized power plant. Reducing this is the single most impactful operational improvement available to KPLC.
  • IPP cost burden: IPPs generate ~30% of electricity but receive ~52% of total KPLC payments, reflecting legacy contracts with high capacity payments. Senate pressure to renegotiate or terminate expensive thermal IPP PPAs is growing. The Lake Turkana dollar-indexed PPA is Kenya's most expensive renewable contract on a per-kWh basis given KES depreciation.

4.3 Last-mile electrification and off-grid

  • Kenya's electrification rate rose from 37% (2013) to 79% (2023) — a 42-percentage-point increase in a decade — driven by the Last Mile Connectivity Programme (LMCP) and the Jubilee government's Big 4 agenda. LMCP has added ~1.55 million households since 2015 by subsidising connection costs for households within 600 m of an existing transformer.
  • The remaining ~21% unconnected population is concentrated in northern Kenya (Turkana, Marsabit, Mandera, Wajir counties) — arid, dispersed communities where grid extension costs exceed $10,000/household. Off-grid solar (solar home systems and mini-grids) is the only cost-effective solution.
  • Kenya is Africa's largest off-grid solar market and the birthplace of PAYGo financing (M-Kopa, 2012). KOSAP (World Bank $150 million) targets 250,000 SHS and 137 solar mini-grids. IEA projects Kenya on track for universal access by 2030 under current trajectories.

5. Five things to take away

1
Kenya runs one of Africa's most renewable electricity systems (~84–90%) not because of recent policy but because of geology: the East African Rift Valley hosts world-class geothermal and hydro resources. Geothermal at Olkaria (~985 MW) is the backbone, producing ~47% of generation with zero fuel cost and near-zero carbon intensity.
2
Kenya Power's financial crisis (seven consecutive loss years through FY2022/23) was structural — a mismatch between USD-denominated PPA obligations and a depreciating shilling. The April 2023 tariff reform resolved the immediate crisis; the structural issues (system losses at 23.5%, IPP cost burden, grid reliability) persist and require continued management.
3
The Ethiopia–Kenya HVDC interconnector (commercial December 2023, 2,000 MW rated capacity) is the most strategically significant regional infrastructure development in EAPP. It brings cheap Ethiopian hydro into Kenya, reduces thermal dispatch, and positions Kenya as a transit hub as the Tanzania link completes. Managing this import dependency on Ethiopia's generation reliability is the key operational risk.
4
Electrification from 37% to 79% in a decade is a genuine achievement, driven by Last Mile Connectivity. The remaining 21% unconnected population is in remote northern counties where grid extension is uneconomic — requiring off-grid solar at scale. Kenya's PAYGo solar market (M-Kopa, SunKing, others) is the commercial mechanism to close this gap, but reaching the poorest households requires continued results-based financing.
5
Kenya's geothermal frontier — Baringo-Silali, Menengai expansion, Olkaria VII — is the most cost-competitive generation pipeline in East Africa. GDC's steam-sales model (drill and sell steam to private IPPs) reduces IPP risk and is proving commercially viable at Menengai. Replicating this at scale across the northern rift corridor is Kenya's most compelling long-run energy investment case.
