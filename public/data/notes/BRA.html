Generation Mix & Capacity

Brazil runs the largest and most institutionally developed power system in the southern hemisphere. Roughly 200 GW of installed capacity serve a continental system operated as a single synchronised machine by the Operador Nacional do Sistema Elétrico (ONS), tied together by one of the longest high-voltage transmission networks in the world, including ±800 kV HVDC links carrying Amazonian hydropower more than 2,000 km to the load centres of the southeast.

The defining shift of the last decade is compositional rather than one of scale. Hydropower, long above 80% of generation, has fallen to roughly 55–60% as wind and solar have grown explosively, concentrated in the Northeast and South. Brazil now generates about 83% of its electricity from renewables, a share few large economies approach, and gas and coal function largely as dry-season and reliability backstops rather than baseload.

That success has produced its own problem. Wind and solar were built faster than the transmission to evacuate them, and since 2023 the Northeast has seen recurrent curtailment of renewable output, with generators disputing compensation for constrained-off energy. Transmission auctions have responded with record volumes, but the lag between generation and grid remains the system's binding constraint.

Market Structure & Institutions

  • MME and EPE: The Ministry of Mines and Energy sets policy; the Empresa de Pesquisa Energética does the planning, publishing the ten-year expansion plan (PDE) that frames auction volumes.
  • ANEEL: The regulator, responsible for tariffs, concessions, quality standards and the auction rules for both generation and transmission.
  • ONS: Dispatches the national interconnected system (SIN) centrally on a cost-based merit order computed by hydrothermal optimisation models, not by generator bids. This is the structural feature that most distinguishes Brazil from the bid-based markets of Chile or Colombia.
  • CCEE: Clears and settles contracts, and runs the short-term market at the PLD settlement price.
  • Regulated (ACR) and free (ACL) markets: Captive consumers buy from distributors through regulated auctions; large consumers buy bilaterally in the free market. Eligibility has been progressively lowered and, from 2026, all high-voltage consumers may migrate, with an extension to low-voltage consumers under debate.
  • Interconnections: Converter stations at Garabi and Uruguaiana link Brazil to Argentina, Rivera and Melo to Uruguay, and Itaipu's 50 Hz half to Paraguay. Brazil's grid is 60 Hz, and its neighbours 50 Hz, so every link is a back-to-back or HVDC conversion rather than a synchronous tie.
Brazil is the anomaly among large power systems: it decarbonised first and is industrialising second. Its challenge is not how to add renewables but how to absorb them, through transmission, storage and flexible demand, while managing a hydro fleet whose reservoir levels increasingly behave like a national fiscal variable. The 2021 water crisis, which forced emergency thermal dispatch and a surcharge on every bill, showed how quickly a dry year converts into an inflation problem.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Curtailment is now the central commercial risk for renewable developers. Constrained-off energy in the Northeast has moved from an operational footnote to a material revenue loss, and the allocation of that cost between generators, consumers and the transmission planner is unresolved. Investors are pricing it, which raises the cost of the next wave of capacity.
2
The end of hydro dominance changes how the system must be operated. Centralised hydrothermal optimisation was designed for a fleet whose storage could absorb variability. As the hydro share falls and the variable share rises, the system needs fast reserves, storage and demand response, none of which the current market design remunerates well. Capacity and reserve auctions are being reworked to fill that gap.
3
Full market opening is the largest regulatory change since the 2004 model. Extending free-market access to every consumer raises questions the sector has avoided: who bears legacy contract costs, how the subsidies embedded in the CDE account are recovered, and what a distributor is for once it no longer buys energy for most of its customers.
4
Itaipu's Annex C renegotiation is a regional, not bilateral, question. With the construction debt retired, the tariff can fall sharply, and what Paraguay may do with its unused half determines whether roughly 7 GW of firm, low-cost hydro stays inside Brazil or becomes tradable in the Southern Cone. Few single decisions matter more to regional integration.
5
Brazil's clean grid is becoming an industrial policy instrument. Green hydrogen, low-carbon steel and data centre investment are all being marketed on the back of an 83% renewable grid. Whether that translates into industrial capacity depends on delivered cost to the consumer, which Brazilian industry consistently reports as high despite cheap generation, because of taxes, sectoral charges and transmission tariffs.
