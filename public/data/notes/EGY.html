1. Energy context

Egypt operates Africa's largest electricity system — roughly 60 GW of installed capacity and ~221 TWh of annual generation (FY2023/24), serving 42 million customers at near-universal connection rates. The system is built on natural gas, which fuels approximately 75–81% of generation, complemented by the Aswan High Dam hydro complex (~2.8 GW) and, since 2019, a rapidly growing renewable portfolio anchored by the Benban Solar Park (1.65 GW AC, one of the world's largest) and the Gulf of Suez wind corridor. A 4 × 1,200 MW Russian VVER-1200 nuclear plant at El Dabaa is under construction on the Mediterranean coast, with Unit 1 reactor pressure vessel installed in November 2025 and commercial operations now expected ~2028.

Despite its enormous installed base, Egypt entered a severe electricity crisis in summer 2023 and into 2024: rolling blackouts of up to 4+ hours per day, driven by a collision of record heatwave demand, declining output from the Zohr offshore gas field (whose reserves were revised steeply downward), foreign currency shortages that impaired fuel imports, and a political decision to prioritise LNG export revenue over domestic gas supply. The crisis has catalysed a package of IMF-backed subsidy reforms — residential tariffs were raised 16–50% in 2024 — and a structural reassessment of Egypt's gas resource base.

Longer term, Egypt is positioning itself as a green energy export hub: exceptional solar irradiance (>2,000 kWh/m²/year), the Gulf of Suez wind corridor, and Suez Canal geography underpin a stated ambition to supply 5–8% of global green hydrogen trade by 2030–2040 via a USD 40+ billion project pipeline. Execution remains early-stage, but the first green hydrogen export (Scatec, 100 MW) shipped in January 2026.

Egypt's 2030 renewable target: 42% of generation from renewables. From ~12% today (including hydro), reaching 42% requires roughly quadrupling renewable output in six years — an extremely ambitious target given current trajectory. Wind and solar are growing but the Zohr decline is simultaneously raising gas generation's share in the near term.

Peak demand reached a record 34.65 GW in summer 2023 — a 3% increase year-on-year — driven by extreme heat and rising air conditioning load. Egypt's population of ~107 million and rapid urbanisation sustain a long-run demand growth rate of ~3.5–4.5%/year. At ~221 TWh/year the system is already one of Africa's largest; by 2030 demand could approach 280–300 TWh without efficiency measures, requiring ~10–15 GW of new capacity.

Tariff reform (2024): IMF Extended Fund Facility (USD 8 billion, 2024) conditionalities require energy subsidy elimination. Household tariffs were raised 16–26% in January 2024 and a further 40–50% in August 2024. Full subsidy removal was targeted for December 2025 with partial slippage expected. Pre-reform retail tariffs were among the world's lowest (~$0.01–0.02/kWh residential). Post-reform (2025): still heavily subsidised by international standards. Total energy subsidies were ~20% of GDP and one-third of government revenues in 2024.

2.4 Nuclear and green hydrogen — the long-horizon bets

El Dabaa Nuclear Plant — Rosatom VVER-1200 Under construction

El Dabaa (320 km northwest of Cairo, Mediterranean coast) is Egypt's first nuclear plant. Four VVER-1200 reactors (the same design as Leningrad-2 and Novovoronezh-2, each 1,200 MW) will add 4.8 GW of zero-carbon baseload. Russia finances 85% via a USD 25 billion state loan (total project cost USD 28.75 billion); Egypt contributes 15%. Unit 1 reactor pressure vessel (330 tonnes) was installed in November 2025; Units 2–4 are at various construction stages. Commercial operations target for Unit 1: ~2028 (slipped from the original 2026 target). At full output, El Dabaa would produce ~35–40 TWh/year — reducing gas burn and potentially enabling Egypt to end domestic electricity shortfalls permanently.

Green hydrogen — USD 40 billion pipeline, early execution Nascent

Egypt's National Low-Carbon Hydrogen Strategy (August 2024) targets 5% of global commercial hydrogen trade by 2030, with a USD 18 billion GDP contribution and 100,000+ jobs by 2040. The Suez Canal Economic Zone (SCZone) has signed MoUs worth ~USD 40 billion with EDF, Scatec, SK Ecoplant, AMEA Power, Masdar, and others. As of end-2025, only ~13% of 39 pipeline projects have binding contracts. Scatec's 100 MW pilot began first exports in January 2026 — a proof of concept. Structural challenges: electrolyser capex, water scarcity, EU certification requirements, and long-term LNG contracts that may conflict with green hydrogen gas supply. The pipeline is real but execution remains early-stage.

3. Sector organisation and actors

3.1 Market structure

The Egyptian Electric Holding Company (EEHC) remains the dominant state entity, owning ~90% of generation capacity and all distribution assets via nine regional distribution companies (42.1 million customers). The 2015 Electricity Law (No. 87/2015) introduced an IPP framework, gave EgyptERA full autonomy as an independent regulator, and formally separated EETC (Egyptian Electricity Transmission Company) from EEHC. In practice the sector operates as a single-buyer model: EETC purchases all output and sells to distribution companies.

Renewable IPPs operate under the Feed-in Tariff (FiT) programme (Benban, 32 PPAs at ~USD 8.4 cents/kWh) or subsequent competitive auctions (solar reaching USD 2 cents/kWh, wind USD 3 cents/kWh). Large consumers can now access bilateral PPAs via EETC wheeling. The government is expanding renewable auctions under a 2030 RE target framework.

4. Key issues and dynamics

4.1 The 2023 energy crisis — overcapacity on paper, blackouts in practice

  • Despite ~60 GW of nominal installed capacity against a peak demand of ~34.65 GW in summer 2023, Egypt experienced systematic load shedding of up to 4+ hours per day. The paradox is explained by a convergence of factors: the Zohr gas field's accelerated decline (reserves revised from 30 TCF to under 10 TCF; seawater infiltration damage), foreign exchange shortages preventing LNG and fuel imports, and a policy choice to prioritise LNG exports for foreign exchange over domestic supply.
  • Aging thermal plants with high forced-outage rates also contributed — many Siemens plants operate below rated capacity due to insufficient maintenance funding. The effective dispatchable capacity was substantially below the nameplate figure.
  • Crisis mitigation: Algeria provided 265 MW emergency supply in late 2023. Egypt accepted IMF conditions for subsidy reform and accelerated renewable procurement. The 2024 tariff increases reduced demand growth but also triggered social friction given high inflation and KES devaluation.

4.2 Gas field decline and the LNG export dilemma

  • Egypt became a major LNG exporter from 2018, earning ~USD 10 billion in gas export revenues in 2022. The Idku and Damietta liquefaction terminals rely on offshore gas from Zohr, Atoll, and other Mediterranean fields. But Zohr's rapid production decline (from ~2.7 BCM/year peak to falling volumes by 2023) has created a structural gas supply squeeze.
  • Egypt is in a dilemma: gas exports earn critical foreign exchange but divert supply from domestic power generation, creating blackouts. The IMF programme and subsidy reform implicitly require a rebalancing toward domestic use, but this reduces export revenues that service Egypt's substantial sovereign debt burden.
  • New gas exploration is underway (Mediterranean and Nile Delta blocks) but time-to-production is 5–7 years. El Dabaa nuclear (~35–40 TWh/year when complete) is the structural solution to reduce gas dependence for power generation.

4.3 Subsidy reform and the political economy challenge

  • Egypt's energy subsidies were approximately 20% of GDP and one-third of government revenues in 2024 — among the highest in the world as a share of the economy. Eliminating these subsidies is a condition of the IMF USD 8 billion Extended Fund Facility, the UAE Ras El Hekma USD 35 billion development deal (February 2024), and Egypt's broader external financing strategy.
  • Tariff increases of 16–26% (January 2024) and 40–50% (August 2024) have been implemented. Full subsidy removal by December 2025 was the target; political economy suggests partial slippage. Without a functional social protection system, rapid price increases hit the poorest households hardest — Egypt's Takaful and Karama cash transfer programme is the partial compensating mechanism.

4.4 GERD and Nile water security

  • Egypt derives over 97% of its freshwater from the Nile; GERD's 74 BCM reservoir can reduce Nile flow to Egypt during drought periods. The Aswan High Dam generates ~7 TWh/year in normal hydrology — a 10–25% reduction in that output under stressed filling scenarios would be materially significant for Egypt's power balance.
  • Egypt withdrew from AU-mediated tripartite talks in early 2024 and raised the dispute at the UN Security Council in September 2024. No binding tripartite agreement on the dam's filling and operation has been concluded. No binding water-sharing agreement exists, and the GERD was officially inaugurated September 9, 2025. This remains the defining bilateral tension in Egyptian energy and water security.

5. Five things to take away

1
Egypt operates Africa's largest electricity system (~60 GW, ~221 TWh) at near-100% electrification — built almost entirely on natural gas. The 2018 Siemens 14.4 GW CCGT commissioning (the world's fastest large-scale capacity addition) represented a generational leap in supply but also a deepening of gas dependence that became a vulnerability when Zohr output declined.
2
The 2023 energy crisis (rolling blackouts despite nominal 60 GW capacity) exposed the gap between nameplate capacity and dispatchable capacity. Declining Zohr gas, aging plants, FX constraints on fuel imports, and LNG export prioritisation all contributed. The crisis has forced a structural reckoning with Egypt's gas resource base and accelerated both subsidy reform and nuclear investment.
3
El Dabaa nuclear (4 × 1,200 MW VVER-1200, Rosatom, ~USD 29 billion, Unit 1 ~2028) is Egypt's most consequential long-horizon energy bet. At full output (~35–40 TWh/year), it would eliminate domestic power shortfalls and free gas for export — but the project runs on a 85% Russian state loan and involves technology transfer from a geopolitically complex partner.
4
The green hydrogen pipeline (USD 40+ billion, SCZone) is Egypt's long-run play for the European clean energy market. The resource case is excellent (Saharan solar, Gulf of Suez wind, Suez Canal logistics). The execution gap is large: only ~13% of pipeline projects have binding contracts as of end-2025. First export (Scatec, January 2026) is a proof of concept, not a commercial scale deployment.
5
The GERD dispute with Ethiopia is not resolvable through energy sector policy alone — it is a sovereign water rights conflict with existential stakes for Egypt. Egypt's position (water security at risk, military posture maintained, UN Security Council engaged) reflects a country that sees GERD as a strategic threat. Energy planners must treat Aswan hydro output as structurally uncertain for planning horizons beyond 2030.
