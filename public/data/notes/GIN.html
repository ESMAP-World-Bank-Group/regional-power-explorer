1. Energy context

Guinea has one of the most paradoxical electricity situations in West Africa: it sits on one of the continent's largest technically exploitable hydropower potentials (estimated at 26,000 MW, including the extraordinary Fomi cascade on the Niger River and the Konkoure, Bafing, and Tinkisso basins), yet only 47% of its population has electricity access and the national utility EDG (Electricité De Guinée) is chronically dysfunctional. The commissioning of Kaléta (240 MW, 2015) and Souapiti (450 MW, 2021) — both Chinese-financed and constructed — has transformed the country's generation base, yet access, reliability, and financial sustainability remain deeply problematic.

Guinea is also critical to regional connectivity: it is the terminal point of the CLSG interconnection (Côte d'Ivoire–Liberia–Sierra Leone–Guinea), and its Kaléta dam provides the electricity backbone for the OMVG (Organisation pour la Mise en Valeur du fleuve Gambie) interconnection with Senegal, Gambia, and Guinea-Bissau. Despite this potential, EDG's operational failure means that Conakry and most provincial capitals continue to face daily outages of 6–12 hours even with the new hydro capacity in place.

  • Hydropower (~74% of generation): Kaléta (240 MW, China Three Gorges/CWE, commissioned June 2015) was the first major hydro addition in decades and immediately transformed Conakry's electricity supply. Souapiti (450 MW, China Three Gorges, commissioned 2021) is the current flagship — it is the largest power plant in Guinea and explains the generation doubling from 2020 to 2021 visible in the historical trend. Both are run-of-river plants on the Konkoure River and face dry season output reduction. Older plants at Kindia (Grandes Chutes, 75 MW) and Garafiri (75 MW) add further capacity.
  • Oil/diesel (~25% of generation): EDG's diesel fleet (Tombo, Kaloum thermal stations in Conakry) provides backup and seasonal peaking when hydro output is low. HFO imports are expensive and the fuel bill is a major EDG financial burden. Some industrial consumers (bauxite mines) operate captive diesel plants.
  • Solar (~1%): Small solar systems in provincial towns and off-grid sites. A larger utility-scale solar programme is aspirational — Guinea's solar resource is good (4.5–5 kWh/m²/day) but grid integration capacity limits utility-scale addition without transmission upgrades.

3. Sector organisation

EDG — Electricité De Guinée Operationally Dysfunctional

EDG is the vertically-integrated state utility. Despite 690 MW of modern hydro capacity (Kaléta + Souapiti), EDG delivers unreliable supply — often 6–12 hours of daily outages in Conakry — due to: extreme transmission and distribution losses (40–50%); inability to bill and collect from government entities (the largest consumers); lack of metering on ~30% of connections; and weak technical capacity. EDG has had multiple donor-supported reform programs (World Bank, AfDB) without sustained improvement. The military authorities has not yet delivered the EDG structural reform that donors have consistently demanded as a condition for further investment.

China Three Gorges / CWE — Project Developers Chinese State Enterprise

China Three Gorges (CTG) and its subsidiary CWE financed and constructed both Kaléta (2015) and Souapiti (2021) under EPC+finance agreements with the Guinean state. Repayment is structured as concessional loans backed by bauxite export revenues. CTG has also been involved in initial discussions for the Amaria cascade (further downstream on the Konkoure). China's willingness to finance and build at scale, on terms that the World Bank's environmental and social standards cannot match, has made it the dominant infrastructure partner in Guinea's hydro sector.

4. Key issues and challenges

  • Kaléta and Souapiti — capacity without reliability: Guinea has commissioned 690 MW of modern hydro in 10 years, more than tripling its generation base. Yet Conakry still faces 6–12 hours of daily outages. The constraint is not generation but transmission and distribution: the 110 kV transmission network has insufficient capacity to wheel all available hydro output; distribution networks are saturated, lossy, and unmaintained; and EDG's billing and collection are non-functional. Generation capacity is a necessary but not sufficient condition for reliable supply.
  • Bauxite mining as anchor demand: Guinea has 60% of the world's bauxite reserves (Sangaredi, Boké region) and is the world's second-largest producer. Major operations include CBG (Boké, Alcoa/Rio Tinto JV), SMB-Winning (China), and GAC (smaller operators). Most mines self-generate on dedicated HFO plants because EDG cannot provide reliable supply. Winning EDG's mining customers would transform the utility's revenue — but requires grid extension to Boké (200 km from Conakry) and supply reliability that EDG cannot currently provide.
  • Dry season hydrology risk: Kaléta and Souapiti are run-of-river plants sensitive to rainfall on the Konkoure watershed. During the dry season (November–April), output can fall to 30–40% of installed capacity, requiring diesel backup. Guinea's longer-term hydro development (Fomi reservoir dam on the Niger) would address seasonality by providing storage regulation, but Fomi is years from completion.
  • OMVG and regional export potential: The Kaléta–OMVG interconnection reaching Gambia and Guinea-Bissau (late 2023) positions Guinea as a hydro exporter to the smallest and most oil-dependent countries in WAPP. This role — analogous to CIV's for the coastal region — is strategically valuable for Guinea's economy (export revenues) and for regional energy security. Maximising OMVG offtake requires financial and commercial arrangements that are still being negotiated.

5. Five key takeaways

1
Guinea's electricity paradox — enormous hydro potential, 690 MW of modern commissioned capacity, yet persistent 6–12 hour daily outages in Conakry — demonstrates that generation capacity alone cannot deliver reliable electricity: transmission, distribution, metering, billing, and utility governance are equally critical.
2
Kaléta (2015) and Souapiti (2021) have been transformational projects, tripling Guinea's generation base — but both were financed by Chinese concessional debt backed by bauxite revenues, creating a commodity-linked debt structure that makes Guinea vulnerable to bauxite price downturns or supply disruptions.
3
Winning Guinea's bauxite mines as EDG customers would be the single most impactful commercial transformation available: mining is the country's largest creditworthy electricity consumer, currently self-generating on expensive diesel — converting mines to grid supply would fundamentally change EDG's revenue base and viability.
4
The OMVG Kaléta–Gambia–Guinea-Bissau interconnection, partially operational since late 2023, positions Guinea as a hydro anchor for the Atlantic mini-grid sub-region: it brings the cheapest and most reliable electricity available to Gambia and Guinea-Bissau for the first time — a genuine regional public good that Guinea can leverage diplomatically and commercially.
5
The CLSG ring's completion (the Sierra Leone section remains the blocking gap) would complete a 225 kV mesh from CIV through Guinea, connecting West Africa's hydro-rich interior to its most electricity-deprived coastal frontier — Guinea's role as the terminal CLSG node makes it an irreplaceable link in WAPP's Atlantic architecture.
