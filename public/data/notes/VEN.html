Generation Mix & Capacity

Venezuela has one of the largest hydroelectric endowments in the world and one of the least reliable power systems. The Guri dam (10,235 MW) on the Caroní River, together with Caruachi and Macagua, forms the Lower Caroní complex that supplies the large majority of national electricity. On paper the country has roughly 35 GW of capacity against a demand that has fallen sharply.

In practice available capacity is a fraction of nameplate. Years of underinvestment, deferred maintenance, loss of technical staff and a collapse in domestic refining have left much of the thermal fleet inoperable and the transmission network fragile. The March 2019 national blackout, which left most of the country without power for days, was the most visible episode in a pattern of rolling outages that continues in much of the interior.

Access statistics remain nominally near universal because connections exist. That figure is misleading: the operative constraint is hours of supply and voltage quality, not connection. Many states outside Caracas experience daily scheduled and unscheduled interruptions, and self-generation by those who can afford it has become widespread.

Market Structure & Institutions

  • CORPOELEC: The Corporación Eléctrica Nacional, created in 2007 by merging the previously separate utilities including EDELCA and EDC, is a single vertically integrated state monopoly covering generation, transmission and distribution.
  • No wholesale market: There is no competitive market, no independent system operator and no independent regulator in any functional sense. Dispatch, tariffs and investment are administrative decisions.
  • Tariffs: Regulated tariffs have been nominal for years, recovering a negligible share of cost. Without cost recovery, maintenance and network investment depend entirely on fiscal allocation, which has not been available at the scale required.
  • Sanctions and equipment: International sanctions and the country's financial position have complicated procurement of turbines, transformers and control systems, compounding the maintenance backlog.
  • Interconnections: Links exist to Colombia at Cuestecitas–Cuatricentenario and to Brazil at the 230 kV line from Guri to Boa Vista in Roraima. The Brazil link, which supplied Roraima for years, was interrupted and Roraima now relies on its own thermal generation.
Venezuela is the region's cautionary case. Hydroelectric resources that should make electricity abundant and clean sit behind an institutional and maintenance collapse that no amount of installed capacity compensates for. Recovery is primarily a question of governance, cost recovery and technical capacity rather than of new generation, and any rehabilitation programme would start with transmission and thermal availability.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Available capacity, not installed capacity, is the only meaningful metric here. Standard databases record roughly 35 GW. Operational reality is far below that, so any planning or comparison that uses nameplate figures for Venezuela will be wrong by a wide margin.
2
Extreme concentration on one river makes the system structurally fragile. With the large majority of generation on the Lower Caroní and a single high voltage corridor carrying it, both drought at Guri and a fault on the 765 kV lines translate directly into national outages.
3
The access statistic conceals the actual service deficit. Near-universal connection alongside daily multi-hour interruptions means conventional access indicators do not describe this country. Reliability metrics, where they exist, are the relevant measure.
4
Rehabilitation would begin with transmission and thermal availability, not new build. The cheapest megawatts available are the ones already installed and not running. Restoring thermal availability and network integrity would deliver more energy per dollar than any greenfield project.
5
Regional reintegration would be consequential but requires institutional recovery first. A restored Venezuelan system with functioning Caroní output could supply Colombia and northern Brazil. Until cost recovery and technical capacity are rebuilt, interconnection adds little, because there is no reliable surplus to trade.
