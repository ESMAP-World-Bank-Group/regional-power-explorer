Generation Mix (2024)

North Macedonia is a structurally import-dependent country — domestic generation covered approximately 89% of electricity needs in 2024 (6,129 GWh produced vs. ~6,900 GWh consumed). Coal accounted for roughly 58% of domestic generation, drawn from two ageing lignite plants; hydro provided ~25%; a gas-fired combined heat and power (CHP) plant in Skopje about 13%; and wind/solar the remainder. The grid depends on imports from Serbia, Bulgaria, and Greece to cover the structural deficit.

Market Structure & Key Actors

North Macedonia has a partially liberalised power sector with the key distinction of Austrian utility EVN operating the distribution network and Bogdanci wind farm — giving foreign capital an established presence in the system.

  • Regulator: ERC (Energy Regulatory Commission) — sets tariffs, licences generators and distributors; oversees Energy Community compliance obligations.
  • ESM (Elektrani na Severna Makedonija): State-owned generator; operates REK Bitola, REK Oslomej, and most hydropower plants (~1,400 MW combined); also the dominant wholesale supplier.
  • MEPSO: Macedonian Energy Transmission System Operator — state-owned TSO; manages 110/220/400 kV grid and cross-border connections; ENTSO-E observer member.
  • EVN Macedonia: Austrian utility EVN's Macedonian subsidiary; operates the distribution network (serving ~900,000 customers) and owns Bogdanci wind farm and the TE-TO Skopje CHP plant; present since 2006 concession award.
  • Market liberalisation: Eligible large consumers can switch supplier; wholesale bilateral market exists; North Macedonia participates in the regional SEEPEX day-ahead platform.
North Macedonia's structural electricity import dependency — covering 10–15% of consumption from neighbours — creates exposure to regional price spikes and supply disruptions. This dependency will worsen as REK Bitola closes unless sufficient RE and interconnector capacity is developed first. The Coal Transition Investment Plan (CIF-ACT) funding approved in 2024 is a positive signal but needs to translate into projects quickly.

REK Bitola — The Coal Phase-Out Challenge

REK Bitola is North Macedonia's dominant generation asset and its most difficult energy challenge. The three-unit lignite plant — located near the Bitola coalfield in southwest Macedonia — produces roughly 58% of domestic electricity despite ageing equipment and repeated Energy Community enforcement proceedings for exceeding emission limits.

  • Technical condition: The plant's boilers were partially refurbished in 2017, but its core equipment remains outdated. Unit availability is chronically below design capacity; forced outages are frequent.
  • Closure plan: The government has announced a gradual phase-out of REK Bitola, but no binding unit-by-unit closure dates have been legally enacted as of 2026.
  • Employment impact: The Bitola complex — plant and associated lignite mines — employs several thousand workers in a region with limited economic alternatives. Just transition planning under the CIF-ACT framework is ongoing but early-stage.
  • CIF-ACT Investment Plan: The Climate Investment Funds' Accelerating Coal Transition (CIF-ACT) investment plan for North Macedonia, approved in 2024, provides concessional financing for RE, grid modernisation, and economic diversification in the Bitola region.
If REK Bitola's ~670 MW of coal capacity is closed without adequate replacement, North Macedonia's structural import deficit would deepen from ~10% to potentially 40–50% of consumption. The window to build replacement RE and upgrade interconnectors before closure is narrow — and the CIF-ACT programme needs to move from investment plans to commissioned projects rapidly.

Renewables & Interconnections

North Macedonia has meaningful solar and wind potential in its southern lowlands and eastern plateaus. RE deployment is accelerating from a low base, supported by competitive auctions introduced under Energy Community obligations. The grid connects to four neighbours, all in the ENTSO-E Continental Europe synchronous area.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
North Macedonia's structural electricity import dependency is its most acute energy security problem. Covering 10–15% of consumption from neighbours is manageable today; if REK Bitola closes without replacement, that dependency could triple. The sequencing of coal closure and RE buildout will determine whether the transition is manageable or destabilising.
2
REK Bitola's closure is necessary but the plan is vague. Energy Community obligations, emission standards, and EU accession pressure all point toward closure in the late 2020s or early 2030s. But without a credible unit-by-unit retirement schedule with parallel RE commissioning and interconnection upgrades, the headline commitment is not yet an operational plan.
3
EVN's 20-year presence makes it the most operationally capable actor in the sector. As DSO, CHP operator, and wind farm developer, EVN has built institutional knowledge and capital discipline that state operators lack. Any large RE project or grid modernisation programme would benefit from EVN's participation — or at minimum, from applying the operational standards it has demonstrated.
4
CIF-ACT funding is a real opportunity — but implementation speed is everything. The 2024 Accelerating Coal Transition Investment Plan provides concessional finance for RE and just transition in the Bitola region. If projects can be structured and permits issued quickly, this funding can bridge the gap between coal closure and RE commissioning. If it moves at the pace typical of regional energy planning, the supply gap will arrive before the replacement does.
5
North Macedonia's grid position makes interconnection upgrades essential for RE integration. Surrounded by five neighbours, MEPSO can leverage multiple import routes for security of supply and multiple export routes if RE surpluses develop. But current NTC capacity on several borders is constrained; investment in cross-border lines is a prerequisite for both import security and future export potential.
