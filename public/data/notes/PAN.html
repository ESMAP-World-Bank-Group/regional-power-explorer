Generation Mix & Capacity

Panama is the largest electricity market in Central America by installed capacity, with approximately 5.1 GW total — nearly double Costa Rica's. Hydropower remains the backbone at 1.85 GW (36% of capacity), generating 6.91 TWh in 2024 (up from 6.06 TWh in 2023). Renewables accounted for 73% of generation in 2024, though this share fluctuates significantly with hydrology — swinging between 45% and nearly 70% since 2019. Natural gas capacity has expanded substantially alongside hydro, providing firm dispatchable generation during dry-season hydro shortfalls. Solar and wind are growing but from a smaller base.

Market Structure

Panama has the most commercially developed electricity market in Central America, with liberalised generation, a state transmission monopoly, and private distribution concessions:

  • ETESA (Empresa de Transmisión Eléctrica S.A.): State-owned; sole owner and operator of the national transmission system; manages Panama's SIEPAC border points with Costa Rica; publishes the 15-year Power Development Plan (PDP).
  • ASEP (Autoridad Nacional de los Servicios Públicos): Multi-sector regulator including electricity; sets distribution tariffs; oversees concessions.
  • CND (Centro Nacional de Despacho): System operator under ETESA; manages real-time dispatch and economic merit-order.
  • AES Panama: Largest single generator; operates Changuinola, Estí, Bayano hydro plants plus gas peakers; US-listed AES Corporation subsidiary; has been in Panama since 1999.
  • NATURGY / Gas Natural: Operates the Bahía Las Minas coastal gas plants; Gas Natural Atlántico operates the LNG regasification terminal at Colón (first in Central America).
  • Distribution: EDEMET, EDECHI (western region), and Elektra Noreste (Naturgy) — all private concessions serving residential and commercial customers.
Panama's electricity sector is uniquely shaped by the Canal. The Panama Canal is a massive, non-negotiable electricity consumer — its locks, pumps, and locomotive systems demand reliable, continuous power. AES Panama's hydro plants supply much of the Canal's electricity; in severe droughts (as in 2023, when low Gatún Lake levels forced Canal transit restrictions), the energy system and the Canal's water system face linked stress simultaneously.

Strategic Crossroads — Colombia Interconnection & Hub Ambitions

Panama occupies the most strategically significant position in the SIEPAC system — it is the southern terminus, and the planned Panama–Colombia interconnection would extend regional integration into South America for the first time:

  • The planned interconnection would traverse the Darién Gap via submarine cable or overland HVDC — technically challenging but strategically transformative: it would link SIEPAC to the Andean electricity market (Colombia, Ecuador, Peru).
  • Colombia's large hydro system and Panama's gas + hydro portfolio would provide natural balancing opportunities; estimated investment exceeds $1 billion.
  • ETESA's PDP 2023–37 explicitly includes the Colombia interconnection as a strategic priority, alongside a second SIEPAC circuit with Costa Rica.
  • As SIEPAC's southern gateway, Panama already earns transit revenues and system fees; the Colombia link would significantly increase this role.

Drought Vulnerability — The Canal Link

Panama's 2023 drought underlined the structural coupling between the electricity system and the Canal:

  • Low rainfall reduced Gatún Lake levels, forcing the Panama Canal Authority (ACP) to restrict vessel drafts and transit numbers — costing Panama an estimated $200M+ in lost Canal revenue.
  • Simultaneously, lower reservoir levels reduced hydro generation, increasing gas dispatch and electricity costs.
  • Both the Canal and the hydro system draw from the same watershed; climate-driven changes in rainfall patterns threaten both simultaneously.
  • ETESA's expansion plan addresses this by diversifying toward solar (less water-dependent) and maintaining gas capacity as a climate-resilient backup.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Panama's Panama–Colombia interconnection is the most strategically consequential pending energy infrastructure decision in Latin American integration. Linking SIEPAC to the Andean electricity market would create the first transcontinental power grid in the Americas. The economics are compelling — Colombia's hydro surplus in wet years could balance SIEPAC's drought periods — but Darién's geography, bilateral political alignment, and financing complexity have delayed the project for two decades. The next financing breakthrough will define whether this decade sees completion.
2
The Canal–electricity coupling is Panama's most distinctive and underappreciated energy security risk. No other country has its primary export revenue source (Canal tolls) directly linked to the same watershed that powers 36% of its electricity. Climate-driven rainfall variability simultaneously threatens Canal revenues and hydro generation — a dual fiscal and energy shock. Decoupling Panama's electricity system from rainfall dependence through solar, storage, and gas is both an energy and macroeconomic resilience priority.
3
Panama has Central America's most commercially mature electricity market — and its most complex governance. With ETESA, ASEP, CND, AES, NATURGY, and multiple distribution concessions, Panama's sector involves more sophisticated institutional actors than any other SIEPAC country. This complexity enables sophisticated PPAs and project financing — but also creates coordination challenges and regulatory disputes that slow new investment.
4
AES Panama's dominant position in hydro is a concentration risk the sector needs to manage. AES operates Changuinola, Estí, Bayano, and several gas plants — giving a single foreign corporate entity disproportionate influence over Panama's generation stack. Diversifying the generator base through new IPP solar and wind entrants is strategically important for both competition and resilience.
5
Panama's LNG terminal at Colón is a regional asset whose value extends beyond Panama's own market. The Gas Natural Atlántico LNG terminal — Central America's first — gives Panama the ability to import gas at international spot prices and distribute it to thermal generators. As regional gas demand grows and domestic reserves decline in neighbouring countries, Panama's LNG infrastructure positions it as a potential gas hub for the isthmus — complementing its electricity transit role.
