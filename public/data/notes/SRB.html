Generation Mix (2024)

Serbia's electricity system is dominated by EPS (Elektroprivreda Srbije), the state-owned vertically integrated utility. In 2024, roughly 60% of generation came from lignite, 30% from hydro, and the remaining 10% from wind, solar, and biomass. The commissioning of the new 350 MW Kostolac B3 lignite unit in December 2024 — Serbia's first new coal plant in decades — underscored the country's continued reliance on domestic lignite despite EU accession obligations.

Market Structure & Regulation

The Serbian power sector is dominated by EPS, which owns all large generation facilities and serves the bulk of consumers. Nominal liberalisation exists but market competition is limited in practice. Serbia is an EU accession candidate and a founding member of the Energy Community, which obliges it to align with EU energy acquis — including unbundling, third-party access, and environmental standards.

  • Regulator: AERS (Agency for Energy of the Republic of Serbia) — sets tariffs, licences operators, monitors compliance.
  • TSO: EMS (Elektromreža Srbije) — nominally unbundled from EPS; ENTSO-E observer member; manages cross-border flows with 5 neighbouring TSOs.
  • DSO: EPS Distribution — EPS subsidiary serving ~3.5 million customers.
  • Market operator: SEEPEX (South East European Power Exchange) — day-ahead and intraday, participating in regional market coupling.
  • Kostolac B3 controversy: The 350 MW Kostolac B3 plant was built with a Chinese Exim Bank loan and constructed by CMEC — a project that collides with Serbia's EU accession trajectory and Energy Community obligations on coal finance.
EPS holds a near-total grip on generation, wholesale, and retail, making it one of the least-liberalised power sectors in the Western Balkans. Unbundling of EPS distribution from supply remains a core Energy Community compliance obligation that Serbia has repeatedly deferred.

Lignite — The Kolubara & Kostolac Basins

Serbia holds Europe's third-largest lignite reserves. The Kolubara basin, southwest of Belgrade, feeds the TENT (Nikola Tesla) plants — the largest power station in Serbia. The Kostolac basin to the east hosts the Kostolac A and B plants, and the newly commissioned B3 unit. Lignite mining and power production employ tens of thousands directly and anchor entire regional economies.

  • TENT A (1 650 MW) and TENT B (1 000 MW): Operational but ageing; several units require significant rehabilitation. TENT A units are among the oldest in the system.
  • Kostolac B3 (350 MW): Completed December 2024 with financing from China Exim Bank and construction by CMEC. This directly contravenes Energy Community obligations on coal subsidies and raises EU accession compatibility questions.
  • Phase-out timeline: Serbia's Integrated National Energy and Climate Plan (INECP) envisages coal exit by 2050 — far later than EU member states. No binding near-term phase-out schedule exists.
The Kostolac B3 commissioning in late 2024 sends a contradictory signal: Serbia simultaneously negotiates EU accession, participates in the Energy Community, and commissions a new Chinese-financed coal plant. This tension will define Serbia's energy diplomacy through the 2030s.

Renewables — Rapid Deployment

Despite coal's dominance, Serbia added significant renewable capacity in 2024. Total RE capacity reached approximately 3.9 GW — a 22% increase over 2023. Wind, solar, and biomass IPPs have grown rapidly, driven by feed-in premiums and improving project economics.

Cross-Border Interconnections

Serbia's central position in the Western Balkans makes it a critical transit hub. All interconnections are within the ENTSO-E Continental Europe synchronous area. Serbia interfaces with five neighbouring TSOs and is a key transit corridor for Bulgaria–Western Balkans trade flows.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
EPS's vertically integrated monopoly is Serbia's biggest structural energy problem. It dominates generation, distribution, and supply, suppressing competition and deferring the unbundling obligations Serbia committed to under the Energy Community Treaty over a decade ago.
2
Kostolac B3 — a new Chinese-financed coal plant commissioned in 2024 — directly conflicts with EU accession. Serbia cannot credibly progress toward EU membership while commissioning new coal under Chinese sovereign debt, and this contradiction will require a political resolution before any accession chapter on energy closes.
3
Lignite will not exit quickly. Serbia's coal reserves are vast, domestic employment is politically powerful, and no binding coal phase-out date exists. The INECP target of 2050 is among the latest in the region — a full two decades after the EU's trajectory.
4
Renewables are growing fast despite institutional inertia. Wind and solar IPPs added ~22% new capacity in 2024, driven by market economics and improved permitting. Serbia has genuine wind and solar potential that could partially offset coal decline if the regulatory environment improves.
5
Serbia is the Western Balkans' key electricity transit hub. Its central grid position, shared Danube hydro with Romania, and interconnections with five neighbours make EMS a strategically important TSO for regional power flows — and give Serbia leverage in any future regional market integration.
