The Permanent Crisis

Iraq's electricity sector is defined by one of the most severe supply-demand gaps in the world. With approximately 30 GW of installed capacity (of which only 20–25 GW is actually operational at any given time due to fuel shortages, maintenance failures, and infrastructure degradation), Iraq faces a demand of ~54 GW for 24-hour supply — a gap of 25–30 GW. Summer temperatures regularly exceed 50°C, making air conditioning a survival necessity, not a luxury. Power cuts of 8–16 hours daily are the norm in major cities. Despite being OPEC's second-largest oil producer, Iraq cannot reliably power its own homes and factories.

The Gas Flaring Paradox

Iraq's electricity crisis exists alongside one of the world's most wasteful energy practices: burning ~17 billion cubic meters of associated gas annually in oil field flares — enough to generate ~30 GW of electricity continuously. This gas is produced alongside crude oil from southern Iraq's supergiant fields (Rumaila, West Qurna, Zubair) but, lacking gathering and processing infrastructure, is simply burned. The solutions are known:

  • Gas gathering networks connecting wellheads to processing plants — requiring billions in infrastructure investment by international oil companies (BP, ExxonMobil, TotalEnergies) already operating the fields.
  • Power generation directly at wellheads using associated gas — "stranded gas" power plants that generate electricity where the gas is, avoiding transportation costs.
  • TotalEnergies' Iraq Energy Deal (signed 2021, implementation ongoing) includes a 1,000 MW solar plant, 1 GW of gas capture from oil fields, seawater injection for enhanced recovery, and storage — the most comprehensive integrated deal of its kind.
Iraq flares gas worth ~$10B/year while spending $1B+/year importing gas and electricity from Iran. This is not a resource constraint — it is a governance, investment, and institutional failure of extraordinary scale. The gas flaring paradox is Iraq's electricity crisis in miniature: the country has the resources to solve its own problems but lacks the institutional capacity, fiscal discipline, and geopolitical independence to do so. Every summer blackout in Baghdad is, in a technical sense, unnecessary.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Iran's December 2025 gas cutoff — losing 4,500 MW overnight — exposed Iraq's most dangerous single point of failure. The US maximum pressure campaign against Iran rescinded the waiver allowing Iraq to pay Iran for electricity. Iraq lost nearly a fifth of its generation capacity in days. Diversifying away from Iranian supply is an existential electricity security priority — the GCCIA link (600 MW) and Turkiye link (300 MW) are small but strategically essential first steps toward a less Iranian-dependent electricity system.
2
Stopping gas flaring is Iraq's single highest-return energy investment — and the IOCs operating Iraqi oil fields are the agents who must act. BP, ExxonMobil, and TotalEnergies collectively control the fields that flare the most gas. Their field development contracts give them discretion over associated gas handling. International pressure, the TotalEnergies Iraq Energy Deal, and carbon pricing expectations are creating incentives to invest in gas capture. If Iraq can capture 50% of its flared gas within 5 years, it could become gas-self-sufficient for electricity without any Iranian imports.
3
Solar energy is the fastest-deployable solution for Iraq's demand gap — and US and UAE companies are increasingly active in Iraq's solar market. Several 500–1,000 MW solar projects have been tendered or developed in western and southern Iraq; solar can be installed in 18–24 months versus 4–6 years for gas-fired plants. At $40–50/MWh in Iraq's irradiance environment, solar is already competitive with imported Iranian gas. The scale question is grid integration — Iraq's grid is too weak and fragmented to absorb variable solar without significant investment in balancing capacity.
4
Iraq's Mosul Dam is both a critical asset and an existential risk. Mosul Dam (1,000 MW) has been described by some engineers as the most dangerous dam in the world — built on soluble gypsum rock that dissolves in contact with reservoir water, requiring continuous grouting. A catastrophic failure would inundate Mosul and downstream cities within hours, killing potentially hundreds of thousands. The dam's structural integrity is a national security issue as much as an electricity infrastructure issue.
5
Iraq's electricity crisis is ultimately a governance crisis, not a resource crisis. Iraq earned $80B+ in oil revenues in 2022 and still cannot provide reliable electricity. The barriers are institutional: corruption in generation contracts, fuel supply manipulation, technical staff incentives that do not reward reliability, and political interference in procurement. Technical solutions — gas capture, solar, GCC interconnection — are necessary but insufficient without governance reform. International financial institutions have conditioned energy financing on institutional reforms that have been promised and not delivered for two decades.
