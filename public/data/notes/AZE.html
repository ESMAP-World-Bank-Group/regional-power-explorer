1. Energy context

Azerbaijan is a South Caucasus oil and gas state of 10 million people bordering Russia, Georgia, Armenia, Iran and the Caspian Sea. Hydrocarbon revenues dominate the economy — oil and gas account for roughly 90% of export earnings — and cheap domestic gas has historically suppressed incentives to develop alternative energy sources. The electricity sector reflects this: ~85% of generation comes from gas-fired plants, with the remainder from Soviet-era hydropower cascades on the Kura River.

The country hosted COP29 in Baku in November 2024, creating significant international pressure to accelerate the renewable energy transition. Azerbaijan has responded with a 7 GW RE target by 2030 and ambitions to export green electricity to Europe via the Black Sea Energy corridor — a potential long-term shift in the country's energy geopolitical role.

RE capacity gap: With fewer than 700 MW of installed renewables at end-2024, Azerbaijan must add over 6 300 MW in under six years to meet its 7 GW COP29 target — roughly 1 GW per year. This would be a ten-fold acceleration from the current commissioning pace.

2.3 Grid and interconnections

Azerbaijan operates an island grid — synchronised neither to the ENTSO-E continental European system nor to the Russian/CIS IPS. This reflects both geography and energy geopolitics. Cross-border flows occur via asynchronous links or lower-voltage AC ties.

2.4 The Nakhchivan exclave

Nakhchivan is an Azerbaijani autonomous republic geographically isolated from mainland Azerbaijan by Armenian territory. It currently relies on electricity imports from Iran and Turkey. A 400 kV interconnection line between Nakhchivan and Turkey is under development and will reduce its dependence on Iranian supply — a strategic energy security priority for Azerbaijan.

3. Sector organisation and actors

3.1 Market structure

Azerbaijan operates a single-buyer model with no competitive wholesale electricity market. Azerenergy JSC is the state vertically integrated utility controlling generation and transmission. Distribution is managed by Azərişıq. Tariffs are set by the Tariff Council (a government body) — there is no independent energy regulator. Residential prices (~3–5 US¢/kWh) are heavily subsidised, suppressing efficiency signals and weakening the investment case for RE alternatives.

4. Key issues and dynamics

4.1 COP29 and the 7 GW renewable target

  • Azerbaijan hosted COP29 in Baku (November 2024), announcing a 7 GW RE target by 2030 — implying 30% of electricity from renewables.
  • Installed RE at end-2024: ~700 MW. Gap: ~6 300 MW in six years (~1 GW/year required — a ten-fold acceleration).
  • The Garadagh Solar (230 MW) and Khizi-Absheron Wind (240 MW) IPPs validated the private investment model but are far below the needed pace.
  • Delivering the target requires: rapid IPP procurement rounds, cost-reflective tariff reform, grid reinforcement, and SOCAR gas pricing adjustment.

4.2 Green Energy Corridor to Europe

  • December 2022 MoU: Azerbaijan, Georgia, Hungary, Romania and the EU signed an agreement to develop the Black Sea Energy (BSE) submarine cable (~1 170 km, 1 GW, expandable to 4 GW).
  • Azerbaijan's ambition: supply 1–4 GW of new solar and wind (Absheron Peninsula) to the EU via this corridor, monetising RE in European carbon-priced markets.
  • This would extend Azerbaijan's energy geopolitical role beyond oil and gas into the post-fossil era.
  • Realistic commissioning date: 2030–2032 at the earliest. Key hurdles: Georgian wheeling capacity, cable financing (~€2.5–3 bn), EU offtake frameworks.

4.3 Azerenergy reform and IPP framework

  • The single-buyer, state-monopoly model is structurally incompatible with the investment volumes required for 7 GW of RE.
  • The nascent IPP framework (Garadagh, Khizi-Absheron) needs to be systematised: transparent tendering, bankable PPAs, independent grid codes, and dispute resolution.
  • SOCAR gas pricing reform is a prerequisite — subsidised fuel artificially suppresses RE competitiveness.

4.4 Karabakh electricity infrastructure

  • Following Azerbaijan's 2023 restoration of control over Karabakh, rebuilding electricity infrastructure is an immediate priority.
  • Sarsang HPP (170 MW) — previously under Armenian management — is being rehabilitated.
  • Total investment estimated at ~$1 bn for transmission lines, substations and distribution networks in formerly occupied territories.
  • Karabakh's solar and wind resources are among the best in the country — a medium-term RE development opportunity.
Azerbaijan's island grid — not synchronised to ENTSO-E or Russian IPS — is both a constraint and an opportunity. It complicates large-scale RE export, but gives Azerbaijan flexibility to design a modern clean grid architecture from scratch, without inheriting legacy synchronous system requirements.

5. Five things to take away

1
COP29 created a credibility test: Azerbaijan announced a 7 GW RE target while hosting the global climate conference. Delivering on this while maintaining cheap gas-based electricity for domestic consumers is the central tension in sector policy.
2
The Green Energy Corridor is Azerbaijan's most important long-term energy opportunity — exporting clean electricity to Europe would diversify revenues beyond oil and gas and extend the country's energy geopolitical relevance into the post-fossil era.
3
The Azerenergy monopoly model is the main barrier to RE scale-up. Moving to competitive IPP procurement with bankable PPAs, transparent grid codes and cost-reflective tariffs is a structural prerequisite.
4
Karabakh electricity restoration (~$1 bn) is the near-term priority — it must be completed before large-scale RE development can proceed in the region, which holds some of Azerbaijan's best solar and wind resources.
5
Azerbaijan's island grid is both constraint and opportunity — it requires significant investment to interconnect at scale, but offers flexibility to design a modern clean-energy system without inheriting legacy Soviet synchronous architecture.
