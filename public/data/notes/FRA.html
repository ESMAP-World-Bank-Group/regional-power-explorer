Generation Mix & Sector Overview

France operates the world's second-largest nuclear fleet and derives more of its electricity from nuclear than any other country — 67.1% in 2024, with 57 reactors across 18 sites producing 361 TWh. This followed a difficult 2022-23 period in which widespread corrosion issues (stress corrosion cracking) reduced output to a 30-year low (~279 TWh in 2022), forcing France to become a net electricity importer for the first time since the 1980s. By 2024, maintenance programmes restored output to near-historical norms. The milestone year for French nuclear was December 2024: Flamanville 3 — a 1,600 MW EPR and France's first new reactor since 1999 — achieved commercial operation after 17 years of construction. France is planning six new EPR2 reactors, with government backing EDF (100% state-owned since 2023) to lead the programme.

The 2022 Nuclear Crisis and Recovery

France's nuclear system suffered its most significant crisis in 2022 — a stress corrosion cracking (SCC) problem discovered in emergency core cooling system pipes of multiple reactors:

  • Scale: At peak, 32 of 56 reactors were offline simultaneously for inspection and repair. Nuclear generation fell to 279 TWh — the lowest since 1988 — and France became a net electricity importer during a European energy crisis caused by Russian gas supply cuts.
  • European impact: France's export shortfall (typically 50–100 TWh/year) coincided with the Russia-Ukraine gas crisis, creating a perfect storm. European wholesale electricity prices reached €500-800/MWh in August 2022 — 10× pre-crisis levels.
  • Recovery programme: EDF implemented an accelerated "Grand Carénage" — a systematic inspection and repair programme covering all reactors. By 2023, most reactors were back online; by 2024, generation recovered to 361 TWh and France was again a significant net exporter.
  • Lesson for nuclear operators globally: The French crisis demonstrates that SCC is a systemic risk in PWR fleets with specific pipe metallurgy — not an isolated incident. It is now a reference case for regulatory requirements in Belgium, the UK, and South Korea, all of which inspected their own fleets after France's disclosure.
France's nuclear fleet is simultaneously Europe's most important climate asset and its most critical infrastructure vulnerability. 62.9 GW of nuclear provides roughly 20% of all EU electricity — low-carbon, dispatchable, predictable. But the 2022 crisis showed that a systemic technical problem (SCC) can take 30+ reactors offline simultaneously, turning the continent's firmest clean power source into a net importer. The ageing fleet (average reactor age: 36 years) means this vulnerability will grow unless life-extension investments and the new EPR2 programme proceed on schedule.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
France's nuclear fleet is Europe's most important single low-carbon infrastructure asset — and its continued operation or failure directly shapes European electricity prices, CO₂ emissions, and energy security in ways no other national energy policy decision can match. When French nuclear runs well (2024: 361 TWh), it exports clean power to Belgium, Switzerland, Italy, Spain, and indirectly to Germany. When it runs poorly (2022: 279 TWh), European electricity prices surge and gas generation increases across the continent. No other country's power sector has this degree of systemic European impact — which makes French nuclear policy a matter of European energy governance, not just French industrial policy.
2
EDF's full nationalisation in 2023 was the logical endpoint of a 20-year contradiction: a state-owned company with public-service obligations operating in a deregulated market that cannot price nuclear's full social value. EDF's partial privatisation in 2005 forced it to compete in electricity markets that price power at marginal cost (typically gas) — meaning EDF's nuclear plants, with near-zero marginal cost, earned revenues far below their total cost of capital and long-term investment needs. The ARENH mechanism (forced sale of nuclear power to competitors at regulated price) further eroded EDF's balance sheet. Nationalisation was not ideological but financial necessity to enable the new build programme.
3
Flamanville 3's 17-year construction timeline and €13B+ cost (from €3.3B estimate) is the single most damaging data point for nuclear new build credibility globally — but the EPR2 programme is designed to draw entirely different lessons from it. Flamanville was a first-of-a-kind project built by an industry that had not built a reactor in France for a decade, with a heavily modified design, changing regulatory requirements, and a supply chain that had atrophied. EPR2 features a simplified, standardised design, a restored supply chain, and a series-build programme (6 reactors, not 1). Whether EPR2 will deliver on cost and schedule remains the central question for global nuclear's credibility through 2040.
4
France's offshore wind development is far behind its peers — despite having excellent Atlantic and North Sea resources — primarily due to a decade of legal challenges, local opposition, and regulatory delays that make the French offshore permitting process the slowest in Europe. France has less than 1 GW of operational offshore wind vs. the UK (16 GW) and Germany (8+ GW). The first French commercial offshore farms (Saint-Nazaire 480 MW, Fécamp 500 MW) were completed 2022-23 after 12+ years of development. Macron's 2022 offshore acceleration plan targets 40 GW by 2050 — which requires a fundamental reform of permitting and judicial challenge procedures that the French state is only beginning to implement.
5
France's energy model — near-100% low-carbon electricity from nuclear and hydro — is the global reference case for what low-carbon electricity looks like at scale, at low cost, and with high reliability, but it depends on institutional capabilities (EDF, RTE, ASN) that took 50 years to build and cannot be replicated quickly. Countries looking at France as a model for nuclear must recognise that France built its 58-reactor fleet in 15 years (1975–1990) only because it had exceptional state industrial capacity, a dedicated supply chain, and political stability that allowed long-term commitment. Replicating this in the 2020s requires not just technology transfer but institutional building that takes decades — the technology is the easy part.
