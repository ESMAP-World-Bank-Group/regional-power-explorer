Country & Sector Overview

Georgia sits at the crossroads of the South Caucasus, bordered by Russia to the north, Azerbaijan to the east, Armenia and Turkey to the south, and the Black Sea to the west. Its electricity system is among the most hydro-intensive in the world: rivers descending from the Greater and Lesser Caucasus mountains provide an abundance of cheap renewable energy in summer, but a structural winter shortfall forces the country to rely on gas-fired backup or cross-border imports every year.

Georgia signed an EU Association Agreement in 2014 and applied for EU candidate status in 2022, giving European energy-market integration a political rationale beyond economics. The sector was liberalised in 2006 when direct Russian gas supplies were cut; since then, the country has built a market framework anchored by the Georgian National Energy and Water Supply Regulatory Commission (GNERC), the Georgian State Electrosystem (GSE) as TSO, and the Electricity System Commercial Operator (ESCO) as market and balancing operator.

Georgia's Enguri HPP (1 300 MW) — the country's single largest power asset — straddles the administrative boundary with Abkhazia. The physical dam is in Georgian-controlled territory, but the reservoir and parts of the infrastructure extend into the breakaway region, making it uniquely vulnerable to geopolitical disruption.

Institutional framework

  • GNERC — independent sector regulator: licences, tariffs, network codes
  • GSE — state-owned TSO; operates and develops the high-voltage grid
  • ESCO — electricity market and balancing operator; single buyer for regulated PPAs
  • Energo-Pro Georgia — dominant distribution company (Czech-owned), serving most of the country outside Tbilisi
  • Telasi — Tbilisi distribution network; historically Russian-owned, now under restructuring

Generation Mix

Approximately 13 TWh was generated in 2024. Large hydro accounts for roughly 77% of output; small run-of-river hydro adds another 12%. Gas-fired thermal covers the remaining 11–12%, operating almost exclusively in winter months when reservoir levels fall. Wind and solar remain negligible despite significant untapped potential.

Seasonal Imbalance

Georgia's hydro system peaks in May–August when snowmelt and rainfall maximise river flows. During these months, the country produces a surplus and exports primarily to Turkey. From October through March, reservoir drawdown and low river flows reduce hydro output sharply; demand for heating simultaneously surges, creating a structural winter deficit covered by gas-fired generation and electricity imports from Azerbaijan and Armenia.

Gas for winter backup is imported exclusively from Azerbaijan via the South Caucasus Pipeline (SCP) corridor — SOCAR is Georgia's primary gas supplier. No Russian gas has been imported since the 2006 supply disruption. The diversification away from Russian energy was completed nearly two decades ago.

Cross-Border Interconnections

Georgia's grid connects to four neighbours, making it both a net exporter in summer and a net importer in winter. The Turkish interconnection is the commercially most important: Turkey purchases Georgian summer hydro under seasonal contracts brokered via ESCO and TEIAŞ.

Key Trade & Energy Relations

🇦🇿
Azerbaijan Winter imports Gas supply Transit route
Azerbaijan is Georgia's most critical energy partner. SOCAR supplies natural gas to Georgian power plants and consumers under long-term contracts via the South Caucasus Pipeline. In winter months, Georgia also imports electricity directly from the Azerbaijani grid when domestic hydro output falls short of demand.
Georgia's strategic importance to Azerbaijan is reciprocal: every molecule of Azerbaijani gas exported to Turkey and beyond via SCP/TANAP — and every barrel of Caspian oil via BTC — transits Georgian territory. This mutual dependency creates a strong bilateral incentive for grid stability and pipeline security.
🇹🇷
Turkey Summer exports Largest trade partner
Turkey is the largest destination for Georgian electricity exports, absorbing the majority of the summer hydro surplus under seasonal contracts coordinated between ESCO and Turkey's transmission operator TEIAŞ. Export volumes have grown as Georgia's small-HPP fleet expanded over the past decade.
Turkey's own growing peak demand and the seasonal complementarity between Georgia's summer peak and Turkey's summer air-conditioning load make this a stable trade relationship. The planned Black Sea Energy cable would eventually add a second high-capacity export corridor directly to the EU, reducing Georgia's reliance on Turkey as a single primary buyer.

Strategic Projects & Pipeline

Black Sea Energy (BSE) submarine cable

The most transformative project in Georgian energy planning is the proposed Black Sea Energy (BSE) submarine HVDC cable connecting Georgia to Romania across approximately 1 170 km of Black Sea seabed. The cable would carry up to 1 GW of renewable electricity (expandable to 1.2 GW), giving Georgia — and potentially Azerbaijan — direct access to EU power markets for the first time.

  • EU status: Designated a Project of Common Interest (PCI); included in EU's Indo-Pacific and Southern Corridor energy diversification strategy
  • Financing: EIB financing discussions ongoing; significant public funding expected given geopolitical rationale post-2022
  • Target commissioning: ~2029–2030
  • Strategic rationale: Would allow Georgia to monetise its large untapped hydro and wind potential at EU market prices, and diversify Azerbaijan's gas export revenues with clean electricity

Namakhvani HPP (~433 MW) — suspended

The Namakhvani cascade on the Rioni River was contracted with Norwegian company Aqua Power (later ENKA) and represented one of the largest greenfield hydro investments in Georgia's post-Soviet history. In 2021, sustained mass protests by local communities, environmental groups, and a cross-political coalition forced the government to suspend the project. The contract was ultimately terminated.

Namakhvani revealed a structural shift: large hydro projects in Georgia now face organised and effective civil society opposition. Future large-scale hydro development will require substantially improved community consultation, environmental assessment, and benefit-sharing mechanisms.

Nenskra HPP (~280 MW) — slow development

The Nenskra HPP on the Nenskra River (Upper Svaneti) is backed by IFC and EBRD financing and has been in development since the mid-2010s. Progress has been slow due to technical complexity, resettlement challenges, and environmental concerns. As of 2025 it remains in a pre-construction phase.

Wind & solar potential

Georgia's wind resources — particularly in the Kartli uplands and along the Lesser Caucasus — are substantial, but development remains nascent. The 20 MW Kartli wind farm (operational 2016) is the only commercial wind installation; a pipeline of larger projects exists but few have reached financial close. Solar development is similarly early-stage despite Georgia's favourable irradiance in eastern lowlands. The BSE cable project creates a new commercial incentive to develop both resources at scale.

Five Takeaways

1
Georgia's hydro-dominated system is clean but seasonally fragile — summer surplus masks a structural winter deficit that requires gas backup or imports every year. Until significant storage or dispatchable renewables are added, this asymmetry will persist.
2
The Black Sea Energy cable to Romania is the decade's most transformative project: if built, it would give Georgia direct access to EU electricity markets and monetise its renewable potential at scale, fundamentally changing the country's energy economics.
3
Enguri HPP's partial location in Abkhazia is a permanent geopolitical risk — any escalation in the breakaway region threatens Georgia's single largest power asset, which accounts for roughly one-quarter of total national generation capacity.
4
The Namakhvani controversy revealed a new political reality: large hydro projects in Georgia now face organised civil society opposition that can halt major investments. Future developers must factor in robust community engagement and benefit-sharing from the outset.
5
Georgia's position as an energy transit corridor — for Azerbaijani gas and Caspian oil via BTC/SCP — is a strategic asset, but one that depends on continued political stability across the South Caucasus and the continued viability of Azerbaijan as a major producer and exporter.
