Generation Mix & Sector Overview

Solomon Islands' electricity sector is on the cusp of its most significant transformation since independence: the Tina River Hydroelectric Project (57 MW, Guadalcanal, ADB + IFC + GEF financed, under construction since 2022) is expected to commission in 2026-2027. When operational, this single project will supply approximately twice Honiara's current peak demand (~30 MW) — delivering 100% renewable electricity to the capital, saving approximately US$20-25M/year in diesel fuel costs, and retiring most of the SIEA (Solomon Islands Electricity Authority)'s HFO generation. The current system (2024) is dominated by HFO/diesel generation at SIEA's Lunga Power Station in Honiara, supplemented by the 10 MW Gold Ridge Solar Farm (Guadalcanal highlands, ADB-financed, commissioned 2020) which provides ~20% of current generation. Small river hydro stations (Kongulai, Gold Ridge area, ~5 MW combined) add another ~15%. The Tina River project is the largest public infrastructure investment in Solomon Islands' history (~US$200M) and its successful delivery will define the country's energy trajectory for a generation. Beyond Honiara's Guadalcanal grid, approximately 90 inhabited islands are served by diesel mini-grids or no electricity at all — the national access rate is approximately 25%, with access outside Honiara and a few provincial towns near-zero.

Tina River Hydro: the transformational projectTina River Hydro (57 MW, Tina River / Mount Popomanaseu watershed, Central Guadalcanal): ADB loan (US$120M) + IFC loan (US$30M) + GEF grant (US$8M) + Australian DFAT (US$30M) + Government of SI (US$20M). Construction awarded to China International Water and Electric Corporation (CWE, a Chinese SOE subsidiary) — SIEA's first internationally competitive EPC contract. Construction progress: dam and tunnel works substantially advanced by mid-2024; commissioning expected 2026-2027. Environmental and social management: extensive community consultation with Tina and Lunga watershed communities (Guadalcanal plains and mountain communities); a community benefit sharing agreement provides royalties to watershed communities. When commissioned: SIEA will decommission most Lunga diesel generation; expected annual diesel saving of ~SBD 150M (US$18M+). This saving approximately equals SIEA's current annual fuel bill — transforming SIEA from chronically loss-making to potentially financially viable.
SIEA: financial crisis and turnaround potentialSolomon Islands Electricity Authority has been chronically financially distressed: revenues insufficient to cover fuel costs + debt service at historical HFO prices; government subsidy required for operations; billing collection approximately 70-80% of invoiced amounts; technical losses ~18-22%. The combination of high diesel cost and low billing efficiency creates an operating deficit that limits SIEA's ability to invest in grid extension or RE. Tina River's commissioning fundamentally changes this arithmetic: replacing US$18M/year of diesel fuel with near-zero marginal cost hydro, and eliminating the primary operating cost driver, gives SIEA the financial headroom to invest in grid extension, billing system improvement, and provincial town solar hybridisation. SIEA's institutional reform programme (World Bank technical assistance 2022-2025) is focused on billing and collection improvement ahead of Tina River commissioning — to capture the financial benefit when hydro comes online.
Gold Ridge Solar Farm: proof of conceptGold Ridge Solar Farm (10 MW, Guadalcanal highlands, near the former Gold Ridge Gold Mine site, ADB-financed, commissioned November 2020): Solomon Islands' first utility-scale solar project. The farm supplies approximately 20% of Honiara's electricity — displacing roughly 3 million litres of diesel/year. The Gold Ridge project demonstrated three important things: (1) large-scale solar is technically viable in SI's humid tropical climate; (2) SIEA can operate utility solar with ADB-provided training; (3) the economic saving (US$3-4M/year fuel displacement) is material relative to SIEA's budget. Gold Ridge is sometimes cited as one of the Pacific's highest-impact per-MW solar investments because the avoided diesel cost in Honiara (~US$0.80/litre effective cost including transport) is far higher than the per-kWh cost of solar generation.
China-SI security agreement: geopolitical context for energySolomon Islands switched diplomatic recognition from Taiwan to China in September 2019. In April 2022, SI signed a security cooperation agreement with China — alarming Australia, New Zealand, and the US, who viewed it as potentially enabling Chinese military access to the Pacific. The geopolitical shift has energy dimensions: Chinese contractors (CWE) are building Tina River; Chinese companies have expressed interest in SI's tuna fishing and logging sectors that generate electricity demand. Australia has also responded with intensified development engagement — the Australian Infrastructure Finance Facility for the Pacific (AIFFP) has increased Pacific energy financing including in SI to counter Chinese infrastructure diplomacy. SI's energy sector is a geopolitical arena as much as a development priority.
Provincial access: outer islands entirely off-gridSolomon Islands has approximately 900 inhabited islands; outside Honiara (Guadalcanal) and a handful of provincial towns (Gizo, Auki, Kirakira, Tulagi), electricity access is near-zero. Typical provincial town electricity: 4-8 hours/day from a diesel generator, US$0.80-1.20/kWh equivalent. Rural communities: essentially no electricity; kerosene and candles for lighting. The World Bank "Solomon Islands Rural Development Programme" includes solar lantern distribution and mini-grid feasibility. ADB "Sustainable Energy Programme": provincial town solar hybridisation (Gizo 500 kW solar, Auki 300 kW solar, Noro solar hybrid). The 100% RE target by 2030 refers to the Honiara grid (achievable with Tina River); provincial and outer island access by 2030 is near-impossible at current programme pace.

Post-Tina River: What Changes in 2027?

When Tina River Hydro commissions (2026-2027), Solomon Islands' energy sector undergoes four simultaneous transformations:

  • Grid mix: Honiara goes from ~35% RE to approximately 85-90% RE overnight. The remaining ~15% will be Gold Ridge solar + small hydro, with retired diesel capacity maintained as emergency backup. SIEA will need grid frequency management protocols for a system now dominated by a single large hydro station.
  • SIEA finances: The ~US$18-20M/year fuel cost saving transforms SIEA from subsidy-dependent to potentially self-financing. This enables SIEA to invest in provincial solar hybridisation programmes without government subsidy — a feedback loop from major RE to broader access investment.
  • Electricity tariff: Tina River's debt service (~US$15M/year for 25 years) must be recovered in tariffs. SIEA's tariff-setting authority has been working with ADB on a cost-reflective tariff structure that reflects Tina River's financing cost while reducing fuel surcharge. Net result: tariffs may not fall immediately despite fuel cost savings — the transition period requires careful communication with consumers and regulators.
  • Development credibility: Successful delivery of Solomon Islands' largest infrastructure project would substantially enhance the government's credibility for future project procurement — including provincial town energy access programmes. Conversely, delays or cost overruns would set back development confidence.
Tina River Hydro is arguably the Pacific's most transformational single energy project since Fiji's Monasavu (1983) — a single plant that will convert a capital city from diesel-dependent to nearly 100% renewable, dramatically improve SIEA's financial position, and unlock provincial electrification investment. Its 2026-2027 commissioning is the central event in Solomon Islands' energy history. The question after Tina River is not "can the Honiara grid go renewable?" — it will — but "can the SIEA institutional capacity and fiscal improvement be translated into meaningful provincial and outer island access improvement by 2030?"

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Tina River Hydro's 57 MW capacity — nearly double Honiara's ~30 MW peak demand — will provide the Solomon Islands' electricity sector with structural surplus capacity for the first time ever, and SIEA should plan now for how to productively use this surplus (provincial town supply via extension cables, electric water heating to replace kerosene, EV charging infrastructure, and new industrial connections in Honiara) rather than discovering after commissioning that the surplus is stranded for lack of demand. Surplus hydro capacity in a Pacific SIDS context — where all fuel is imported and all electricity access improvement requires capital that SIEA historically lacked — is an extraordinary opportunity. A 10 MW extension cable from Honiara to Auki (Malaita), Solomon Islands' second city (30,000 people), would use Tina River surplus to provide Malaita with reliable electricity for the first time. The cable distance (~200 km by sea, but potentially 100 km overland/submarine) makes this non-trivial but potentially the highest-impact post-Tina River investment.
2
The Gold Ridge Solar Farm's track record — operating reliably at 90%+ availability since 2020 in SI's high-humidity tropical climate, providing ~20% of Honiara's electricity at a fraction of diesel cost — demolishes the historically common Pacific utility objection that solar is unreliable in cloudy tropical conditions, and SIEA should use Gold Ridge as the reference case for provincial solar procurement rather than requiring new feasibility studies for every new site. The Pacific "solar in cloud" argument — that persistent cloud cover makes solar unreliable in Melanesia — has been empirically refuted by Gold Ridge, which generates approximately 1,750 kWh/kWp/year despite SI's wet climate. This is 10-15% below the Caribbean's best sites but comparable to the UK and northern Europe — and dramatically better than the argument implies. Provincial solar procurement using Gold Ridge as the design reference (same panel and inverter specifications, same battery sizing rationale, same O&M training model) would dramatically shorten procurement timelines.
3
The China-Australia competition for infrastructure influence in Solomon Islands — with CWE building Tina River and Australia increasing AIFFP financing for Pacific energy projects — is creating a supply of concessional financing for SI's energy sector that SI must manage strategically rather than simply accepting the first available offer, because financing terms, debt covenants, local employment conditions, and technology standards vary significantly between Chinese and Australian/MDB financing packages. Solomon Islands is simultaneously a beneficiary and a bargaining chip in Pacific geopolitical competition. SI's government has demonstrated some sophistication in using competing financing interest to negotiate better terms — the Tina River financing package (ADB + IFC + Australian + GEF co-financing) is more favourable than a comparable Chinese EPC-only deal would have been. This negotiating leverage is real but temporary: once Tina River is complete, SI will have fewer major infrastructure projects to offer as competitive financing opportunities, reducing its bargaining position.
4
Solomon Islands' 2021 political riots (ethnically motivated Malaita-Guadalcanal tensions, with Honiara's Chinatown targeted) created security risk for the Gold Ridge Solar Farm and its Chinese-linked supply chain — a reminder that political instability in small Pacific states can directly threaten energy infrastructure, and that energy project design in fragile Pacific SIDS must incorporate political risk management alongside technical and financial risk. The Gold Ridge Solar Farm is located in the Gold Ridge area of Guadalcanal — a location with historical ethnic tension (Gold Ridge mine was a major site of Malaitan-Guadalcanal conflict during the 1999-2003 "Tensions"). The 2021 riots did not directly damage the solar farm, but the security environment required armed guard deployment and delayed an O&M visit. Tina River's location in a watershed area with complex community relationships around land and resource rights requires sustained community relations investment throughout operation, not just during construction.
5
Solomon Islands' outer islands — 875+ inhabited islands outside Guadalcanal with near-zero electricity access, served at best by provincial diesel mini-grids costing US$0.80-1.20/kWh equivalent — represent the Pacific's largest concentration of energy poverty in absolute terms after PNG, and the post-Tina River financial improvement of SIEA is the only realistic domestic funding source for provincial solar hybridisation at meaningful scale without indefinite dependence on donor grants. SIEA's post-Tina River financial improvement (US$18-20M/year fuel saving) could fund approximately 5-8 MW of provincial solar annually if 50% of the saving is directed to capital investment. At this pace, all provincial towns (Gizo, Auki, Kirakira, Tulagi, Munda) would have solar hybrid electricity within 5-7 years — a meaningful improvement over the current 4-8 hours/day diesel that defines provincial electricity quality. The institutional prerequisite: SIEA must actually retain the financial benefit from Tina River rather than having it absorbed by tariff reductions that leave SIEA no better off financially than before.
