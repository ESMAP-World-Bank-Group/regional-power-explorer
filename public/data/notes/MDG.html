JIRAMA financial collapse. JIRAMA (Jiro sy Rano Malagasy — electricity and water utility) is one of the most financially distressed state utilities in Africa. It operates at a structural deficit: HFO fuel costs dominate expenditure; electricity tariffs are well below cost recovery; government arrears to JIRAMA are large; and technical losses exceed 30%. JIRAMA cannot pay its fuel suppliers on time, leading to recurring HFO stock-outs and planned/unplanned blackouts of 8–16 hours per day even in Antananarivo. Despite this, the Madagascar government has not implemented the tariff reform or institutional restructuring needed for JIRAMA's viability — making JIRAMA one of SAPP's clearest examples of politically-constrained utility failure.

1. Energy context

Madagascar is an island nation of 28 million people, the world's fourth largest island, located in the Indian Ocean 400 km east of Mozambique. It is a SAPP member but is not physically connected to the SAPP continental grid — its electricity system is entirely isolated, operating as a set of interconnected regional grids (principally Antananarivo and the highlands) and many isolated provincial grids (Toamasina, Mahajanga, Fianarantsoa, Toliara, etc.). Madagascar's geography — long, mountainous, cyclone-exposed — makes grid development extremely capital-intensive relative to the population it can serve.

With ~22% electrification and JIRAMA in a perpetual financial crisis, Madagascar's energy sector is characterised by chronic undersupply to connected consumers and near-total absence of electricity in rural areas (where 70% of the population lives). The country's significant hydro potential (estimated 7,800 MW technical potential, only ~200 MW developed) and biomass resources (extensive forest cover, though threatened by deforestation) are largely untapped. Solar energy is expanding rapidly through the off-grid market — Madagascar is one of Africa's largest off-grid solar SHS markets — but the formal utility sector remains stagnant.

  • Hydro (~39% of generation, ~200 MW): Andekaleka HPP (165 MW, on the Mangoro River, northeast of Antananarivo) is the largest and most important hydro asset, supplying the Antananarivo interconnected grid. Mandraka HPP (24 MW), Volobe (in rehabilitation), and many smaller run-of-river stations across the highlands provide the foundation of the formal grid's generation. Madagascar's total exploitable hydro potential (~7,800 MW) remains largely undeveloped due to capital constraints and JIRAMA's financial inability to co-fund projects.
  • Oil/HFO (~35% of generation, ~200 MW HFO plant): JIRAMA operates HFO thermal plants in Antananarivo (Ampefiloha, Ambohimanambola), Toamasina, Mahajanga, Toliara, and other provincial centres. HFO is expensive and imported — accounting for the majority of JIRAMA's operational expenditure. HFO price volatility and JIRAMA's inability to pay suppliers on time leads to fuel stock-outs and blackouts. The HFO plants are the most expensive component of JIRAMA's generation portfolio and the primary driver of its financial crisis.
  • Coal (~20%, ~100 MW): The Ambatovy nickel mine (Sherritt International/Sumitomo) and the Toamasina port industrial area operate coal-fired generation, making coal a significant share of Madagascar's electricity mix. This is industrial self-generation rather than grid-supplied power.
  • Solar (emerging, ~3%): Several solar IPPs have been commissioned under ORE (Office de Régulation de l'Electricité) licences. Ambatolampy solar (20 MW), Morondava, and others. The off-grid solar market (SHS lanterns and small systems) is growing rapidly without JIRAMA involvement — private companies (Starsight, Bboxx, ENGIE Mobisol) have distributed millions of solar lanterns and small SHS across rural Madagascar.

3. Sector organisation

JIRAMA — Jiro sy Rano Malagasy Financially Non-viable

JIRAMA (electricity and water) is one of sub-Saharan Africa's most financially distressed utilities. Operating deficit: JIRAMA spends MGA 700–800 billion/year more than it earns in revenue. Government transfers cover part of the gap but are irregular; fuel suppliers are paid late; and technical losses of 30–35% mean a large fraction of generated electricity produces no revenue. Multiple World Bank-supported reform programmes (2013, 2019) have attempted tariff reform and operational improvement with limited sustained results. JIRAMA's political economy problem is that tariff reform in one of Africa's poorest countries threatens social stability — the government's reluctance to raise tariffs is understandable, but it condemns JIRAMA (and its customers) to perpetual crisis.

4. Key issues and challenges

  • JIRAMA's financial trap: JIRAMA's HFO fuel costs are the immediate trigger of most crises (stock-outs → blackouts), but the root cause is structural: below-cost tariffs plus high losses mean JIRAMA cannot generate adequate cash flow. Increasing tariffs is the economically necessary step, but in a country where GDP per capita is ~USD 500, it is politically and socially fraught. The consequence is a permanent crisis: JIRAMA borrows to pay fuel suppliers, accumulates debt, delays payments, loses supply quality, loses credibility, and the cycle repeats.
  • Cyclone vulnerability: Madagascar is one of the world's most cyclone-exposed countries, hit by multiple Category 3–5 cyclones every year (2022: Batsirai and Emnati both caused widespread grid damage; 2023: Cyclone Freddy also struck). Transmission lines, substations, and distribution networks are repeatedly damaged and require reconstruction — consuming capital that could otherwise fund capacity expansion. Climate resilient grid hardening is essential but requires financing that JIRAMA cannot provide.
  • Off-grid solar vs. formal grid: Madagascar's off-grid solar market has boomed: private companies have distributed 3–4 million solar lanterns and SHS, reaching rural households that JIRAMA's grid will never serve economically. This is genuinely positive — but creates a bifurcated energy system: well-connected households (Antananarivo, Toamasina) on an unreliable JIRAMA grid; rural households on private solar (lanterns, SHS); and a vast middle (peri-urban, smaller towns) with no adequate service. The government's Rural Electrification Programme (PREA) has made limited progress in coordinating these markets.
  • Hydro potential vs. JIRAMA's inability to develop it: Madagascar's 7,800 MW technical hydro potential is one of Africa's most underutilised resources. Several projects have been in development for years (Sahofika: 192 MW; Volobe rehabilitation: 90 MW), but JIRAMA's lack of creditworthiness means they cannot sign PPAs or take on project debt. Multilateral financing (World Bank Scaling Solar, AfDB MGA hydro) is the only viable route to developing these projects — but requires political willingness for institutional reform that has historically been absent.

5. Five key takeaways

1
JIRAMA is a textbook case of a utility trapped in financial non-viability by a political economy that cannot afford cost-reflective tariffs: the cycle of below-cost tariffs → cash deficit → fuel non-payment → stock-outs → blackouts → economic damage → political pressure → tariff freeze has repeated for 20+ years with no structural break.
2
Madagascar's 7,800 MW technical hydro potential — with only ~200 MW developed — is one of the largest undeveloped renewable resources relative to market size in SAPP; the barrier is not engineering but financing, creditworthiness, and JIRAMA's inability to be a viable offtaker without institutional reform.
3
The off-grid solar boom (3–4 million SHS/lanterns distributed by private companies) is Madagascar's electricity success story — achieved entirely outside the JIRAMA system, by private companies using pay-as-you-go mobile money models that bypass both the utility and the traditional grid finance paradigm.
4
Madagascar's island status (no SAPP grid interconnection) means it cannot benefit from regional trade — unlike Lesotho or Eswatini which can import from South Africa, Madagascar must self-supply or develop domestic alternatives. This geographic isolation makes JIRAMA reform and domestic generation development a non-optional national priority.
5
Cyclone-proofing Madagascar's electricity grid is a precondition for any sustainable development trajectory: annual cyclone damage reconstructs the same vulnerable infrastructure year after year, diverting resources from expansion and creating a chronic reconstruction cycle that keeps the grid permanently below its design capacity.
