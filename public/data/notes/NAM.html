1. Energy context

Namibia is one of Africa's most sparsely populated countries (2.6 million people across 824,000 km²) and one of the most import-dependent electricity systems in SAPP: approximately 70% of Namibia's electricity is imported, primarily from South Africa (Eskom) and Zambia. NamPower, the state utility, operates the Ruacana hydropower plant (332 MW) on the Kunene/Cunene River (Angola border) as its primary domestic source — but Ruacana's output depends on water releases from Angola's Calueque Dam upstream, creating a geopolitical dependency within its domestic resource.

What makes Namibia exceptionally interesting in the energy transition is its extraordinary renewable resource endowment: among Africa's highest solar irradiance (southern Namib, >6 kWh/m²/day), strong Benguela Atlantic wind (southern Namibia, Lüderitz region), and vast uninhabited land. These resources are the foundation for the Hyphen Hydrogen Energy project ("HyNam") — a proposed 3 GW wind+solar complex for green hydrogen and ammonia export, targeting European markets. If realised, it would be transformative for Namibia's economy and a reference project for the global green hydrogen industry.

  • Ruacana HPP (332 MW — the generation backbone): Ruacana is Namibia's largest and most important power asset, located on the Kunene/Cunene River on the Angola border. It produces run-of-river (flow-dependent) power. The critical constraint: Angola's Calueque Dam (upstream) controls water releases into Namibia. Angola's decisions on irrigation, hydropower, and releases directly affect Ruacana's output — a bilateral energy relationship managed through a Namibia-Angola Permanent Water Commission. In 2023, Ruacana produced ~1,320 GWh (~70% of domestic generation).
  • Solar (~510 GWh, ~27% of domestic generation): Namibia has commissioned several utility-scale solar plants: Hardap Solar (45 MWp, Mariental, commissioned 2019, IPP by Solar Reserve/juwi, under NamPower PPA), Omburu (13 MW), plus a growing community of smaller commercial and rooftop installations. Solar is Namibia's fastest-growing domestic generation source and the key to reducing import dependence. NamPower's Renewable Energy Feed-in Tariff (REFIT) programme has supported multiple private solar projects.
  • Van Eck (120 MW coal, Windhoek): Van Eck is Namibia's only coal-fired plant, an old and expensive station in Windhoek used for system balancing and backup. It runs on imported coal (from South Africa) and has one of the highest generation costs in the NamPower portfolio. It is scheduled for decommissioning as solar and gas alternatives are developed.

3. Sector organisation

NamPower — Namibia Power Corporation Transmission + Generation SOE

NamPower is responsible for bulk electricity generation and transmission; distribution is handled by regional electricity distributors (REDs) and some municipalities. NamPower is one of SAPP's more financially stable state utilities — it benefits from Namibia's relatively good governance and macroeconomic management. However, high import costs (purchasing from Eskom at above-average tariffs during ZAF load shedding) have been a financial pressure. NamPower manages the SAPP import arrangements, operates Ruacana, and is the contracting party for all IPP PPAs in Namibia.

Hyphen Hydrogen Energy (HyNam) Green H2 Developer (in development)

Hyphen Hydrogen Energy Ltd (previously HDF Energy / private consortium including Nicholas Holdings) has proposed a 3 GW wind+solar complex in the Tsau //Khaeb National Park (southern Namibia, near Lüderitz) for green hydrogen and ammonia production for export to Europe. Environmental Impact Assessment approved in 2023. The project is divided into phases: Phase 1 (250 MW, FID target 2025–2026) would produce ~200,000 tonnes/year of green hydrogen. Total project cost estimated at USD 10+ billion for full build-out. This is the most significant proposed investment in Namibia's history, dwarfing current GDP in eventual capital deployed.

4. Key issues and challenges

  • Import dependency and Eskom exposure: Namibia's 70% import dependence on Eskom makes it highly vulnerable to South Africa's load shedding — when Eskom reduces exports during Stage 6+, NamPower must source emergency replacement power from Zambia and Mozambique (at higher cost and limited availability) or implement domestic load shedding. The structural fix is developing domestic solar and Ruacana reliability — but this takes years, and the Eskom exposure is immediate.
  • Ruacana water governance: Ruacana's output depends on Angola releasing adequate water through Calueque Dam. Angola's upstream water use decisions — for its own agriculture, Soyo irrigation, and Ruacana HPP (Angola also has turbines at the Ruacana complex) — affect Namibia's electricity supply. The Namibia-Angola Permanent Water Commission provides a diplomatic framework, but in drought years the competing interests of the two countries for the same water resource create bilateral tension.
  • Hyphen — scale, timeline, and bankability: The HyNam project faces three major challenges: (1) scale — 3 GW of renewable capacity in a remote, infrastructure-scarce desert requires enormous upfront infrastructure investment (water supply for electrolysis in a desert, port expansion at Lüderitz, worker accommodation); (2) offtake — European green hydrogen demand has developed more slowly than projected, making long-term PPAs for green ammonia uncertain; and (3) financing — at USD 10B+ total, it is beyond what Namibia's domestic financial market or any single DFI can provide. Phase 1 FID (250 MW) is the critical near-term test.
  • Rural electrification gap: Despite 56% overall electrification, rural Namibia (the vast communal areas of the north, east, and south) has electrification rates below 20%. Grid extension is uneconomical across Namibia's vast distances. Solar home systems and mini-grids are the appropriate technology, but the national rollout programme (REEEI) has progressed slowly. The resources from a potential HyNam royalty stream could fund transformative rural access — if project economics and government revenue distribution policies align.

5. Five key takeaways

1
Namibia imports 70% of its electricity while sitting on some of the world's best solar and wind resources — a paradox driven by the geographic concentration of renewable resources in the remote southern Namib and the absence of the transmission infrastructure to connect them to demand centres in the north.
2
Ruacana (332 MW) is Namibia's most strategically important power asset, but its output is controlled in part by a foreign country (Angola's Calueque releases) — a sovereignty vulnerability that no domestic investment can resolve, only diplomacy and potentially the development of domestic solar to reduce Ruacana's share of total supply.
3
The Hyphen/HyNam green hydrogen project, if realised at even 50% of its planned scale, would be the largest private investment in Namibia's history — transforming its economy from a mining-services base to a clean energy export hub and demonstrating that Africa's Saharan/Kalahari belt can be a global renewable energy production zone.
4
South Africa's load shedding is Namibia's electricity crisis by proxy: every Stage 6+ event in South Africa triggers a supply emergency in Namibia that NamPower must manage with reduced import volumes, demonstrating that SAPP members' energy security is not merely correlated but coupled.
5
Namibia's governance track record (rule of law, contractual reliability, SACU membership) is among the strongest in SAPP — this makes it, alongside Botswana, the most creditworthy environment for renewable energy IPP investment in the region, and the gap between Namibia's governance quality and its current renewable deployment is a market opportunity that targeted financing structures can unlock.
