War's Impact on Yemen's Grid

Yemen's electricity system has been catastrophically damaged by over a decade of civil war. Pre-war installed capacity of ~1.5 GW has been reduced to approximately 1 GW of available supply — and even this serves only Aden and nearby southern governorates. The national grid, which once reached all major cities, is effectively non-functional. Yemen is divided between Houthi control of the north (including Sana'a) and the Saudi-backed Presidential Leadership Council / STC control of the south; each zone has its own fragmented electricity supply arrangements. For a population of 30 million, this means the vast majority live without reliable grid electricity — many without any at all.

Solar as War-Zone Energy Solution

Yemen has become an unlikely case study in solar energy adoption under conflict conditions. As the national grid collapsed, two parallel solar energy developments emerged:

  • UAE donor-funded utility-scale solar: The Abu Dhabi Fund for Development has financed 120 MW (Aden) and 53 MW (Ataq) of utility-scale solar in STC-controlled areas — using solar to provide basic electricity to constituencies that support Saudi-led coalition governance. These projects serve real humanitarian needs while reinforcing political alignments.
  • Bottom-up private solar: Across Yemen — including Houthi-controlled territories — individual consumers, businesses, and local mosques have purchased solar panels, batteries, and inverters. The solar panel import market operates through informal channels. Yemen's south24.net reported a thriving solar market economy as one of the few growing economic sectors during the war.
  • Humanitarian off-grid: UNDP's programme powering 152 public service facilities with solar demonstrates that critical infrastructure (hospitals, water, schools) can be solar-powered even in active conflict zones, without any grid connection.
Yemen is developing the world's most extreme case of solar energy as a conflict-zone survival tool. In the absence of any functioning state electricity system across most of the country, individual Yemenis have become some of the most innovative off-grid solar adopters in the world — not out of environmental commitment but because solar panels are the only reliable source of electricity available. This bottom-up solar economy, however chaotic, is building the consumer literacy, supply chains, and technical expertise that any future Yemeni electricity system will need.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Yemen's electricity crisis will not be solved until the political conflict is resolved — but solar deployment can reduce humanitarian costs in the interim. No reconstruction programme, however well-funded, can rebuild a national grid in an active conflict zone where infrastructure is a strategic target. The focus should be on hardened, decentralised solar + battery systems that cannot be easily disrupted by a single strike — not on rebuilding vulnerable centralised grid infrastructure that has been repeatedly destroyed.
2
The UAE's strategic use of solar diplomacy in Yemen — Aden 120 MW, Ataq 53 MW — demonstrates that energy infrastructure can be deployed as geopolitical currency in conflict zones. Abu Dhabi is investing in electricity for STC-controlled areas where its political allies govern. The projects deliver real electricity to real people; they also reinforce political loyalty and demonstrate UAE influence at a fraction of the cost of direct military operations. This model of "energy diplomacy" is being replicated across the Horn of Africa and the Sahel.
3
Yemen's post-conflict electricity reconstruction will require the largest per-capita solar deployment of any country in history. With the national grid destroyed, the most cost-effective path to universal electricity access is not rebuilding a centralised system but deploying distributed solar + storage at every level — village micro-grids, household systems, municipal solar plants. This is faster, cheaper, and more resilient than central grid reconstruction. Yemen's geography (dispersed population, difficult terrain) and irradiance (5.5+ kWh/m²/day) make distributed solar optimal.
4
Yemen's pre-war gas reserves (Marib) could anchor post-conflict gas-fired generation if peace is achieved — but are currently controlled by tribal and conflict actors. The Marib gas field was Yemen's pre-war electricity backbone; the Marib power complex (400 MW) used associated gas for generation. Control of Marib has been contested throughout the war; Houthi capture attempts and tribal dynamics have kept it unstable. Post-conflict, Marib gas + new solar could serve as the twin pillars of Yemen's electricity reconstruction.
5
Yemen's informal solar economy is building human capital that formal reconstruction programmes will need. The technicians installing, repairing, and trading solar equipment across Yemen — in both Houthi and STC territories — are developing skills that no pre-war training programme could have delivered. A post-conflict skills recognition and certification programme for Yemen's solar workforce would convert this informal capacity into a formal electricity sector asset. Similar spontaneous technical development occurred in post-conflict Sierra Leone and Liberia.
