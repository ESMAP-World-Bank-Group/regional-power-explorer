1. Energy context

Singapore operates the most liberalised electricity market in Southeast Asia from a resource base that is almost entirely imported — the city-state has no indigenous fossil fuels, negligible hydropower, and severe land constraints on renewables. The entire system runs on natural gas piped from Indonesia and Malaysia, supplemented by LNG imports via the Singapore LNG Terminal (operational 2013). This structural dependency on gas imports is the defining characteristic of Singapore's electricity sector and the central driver of its long-term energy strategy.

The Energy Market Authority (EMA) manages a competitive wholesale market — the National Electricity Market of Singapore (NEMS), established 2003 — in which generators bid into half-hourly auctions and large industrial consumers can choose their retailer. Singapore's residential tariff (~S$0.27–0.30/kWh) is among Southeast Asia's highest, reflecting the full cost of gas imports with no subsidies. As of Q2 2026, the regulated tariff (inclusive of GST) is S$0.297/kWh.

The government's long-term challenge is the solar land ceiling: even with aggressive deployment, Singapore revised its 2030 solar target upward to 3 GWp (from 2 GWp) and set a forecast ceiling of 5.3 GWp by 2035. The strategic response is the Low-Carbon Energy Imports (LCEI) framework — importing up to 6 GW of low-carbon power from regional sources by 2035 (target raised from 4 GW), including hydro from the Mekong region and potentially solar from Australia. Net Zero by 2050 is enshrined in the Singapore Green Plan 2030.

  • Natural gas (83% capacity / 94% generation): All dispatchable generation from combined-cycle gas turbines (CCGTs) and open-cycle gas turbines (OCGTs) — 10.4 GW total. Gas supplied via the West Natuna Transportation System (WNTS) from Indonesia's Natuna Sea and the Malaysia-Singapore pipeline, supplemented by LNG regasification at the Singapore LNG Terminal (~6 mtpa capacity, Jurong Island). Major generators: Senoko Energy (~3.3 GW, Marubeni/Keppel consortium), YTL PowerSeraya (~3 GW), Tuas Power (~2.6 GW, Vopak/Keppel), Sembcorp Cogen (~1.5 GW).
  • Solar (10% capacity / ~2% generation): ~1.4 GWp installed by mid-2025 (up from 1.2 GWp end-2024). Singapore added 504 MWp in 2025 alone, a new record. Revised 2030 target: 3 GWp. Deployed on rooftops (~60%), Tengeh Reservoir floating solar (60 MWp, one of the world's largest floating arrays), HDB (public housing) estates, and industrial rooftops. The 118 MWp Sembcorp Jurong Island ground-mounted farm (2025) is Singapore's largest single project.
  • Waste-to-Energy (3% capacity): Senoko WtE plant (~345 MW), contributing baseload from municipal solid waste incineration. A replacement integrated facility is in long-term planning.
  • Energy storage (2%): ~200 MW grid-scale battery storage deployed by end-2024, supporting frequency regulation and renewable intermittency management. Growing rapidly as solar penetration increases.
Singapore's system is structurally dependent on gas imports with no viable domestic alternative at scale. NEMS is the most competitive electricity market in ASEAN, with full retail contestability for all consumers since 2018 (Open Electricity Market). The 2022 LNG price spike drove tariffs to a record ~S$0.39/kWh, exposing acute vulnerability to global gas market volatility.

Demand growth (~2%/yr) reflects steady GDP expansion, industrialisation on Jurong Island, and a fast-growing data centre sector. The commercial sector dominates (offices, data centres, retail malls), followed by industry (chemicals, refining, wafer fabrication) and residential. Data centre power consumption reached an estimated ~3 TWh/yr in 2023 and continues to grow ~15%/yr. Singapore hosts ~80 hyperscale data centres; the government imposed a moratorium on new DC permits (2019–2022) due to grid and sustainability concerns.

Tariffs (Q2 2026): Residential: S$0.2727/kWh excl. GST; S$0.2972/kWh incl. 9% GST (~USD 0.22/kWh). The energy component (~76% of tariff) directly passes through LNG import costs, revised quarterly. Large contestable customers participate directly in NEMS spot prices. No subsidies at any consumption level.

3. Sector organisation and actors

Energy Market Authority (EMA) Regulator & Market Operator

The EMA, under the Ministry of Trade and Industry (MTI), is Singapore's electricity, gas, and district cooling regulator. It issues generation licences, sets transmission and distribution charges, operates the capacity market mechanism (USEP), and conducts market surveillance. The EMA works with the Energy Market Company (EMC), which runs the NEMS half-hourly auction platform. EMA coordinates the LCEI framework and LNG procurement strategy on behalf of national energy security.

SP Group Transmission / Distribution / Retail

SP Group (Singapore Power), wholly owned by Temasek Holdings, operates the regulated transmission system (SP PowerAssets, all 230 kV lines and below) and the distribution network. SP Group was separated from generation in 2001. SP Services acts as the Market Support Services Licensee (MSSL) and default retailer for non-contestable customers. SP Group is also the Distribution System Operator (DSO), coordinating solar integration, storage, and demand response.

Gencos & Licensed Retailers Generation / Competitive Retail

Generation is entirely privately owned: Senoko Energy (~3.3 GW, Marubeni/Keppel), YTL PowerSeraya (~3 GW, Malaysian-owned), Tuas Power (~2.6 GW, Vopak/Keppel), Sembcorp Cogen (~1.5 GW), and Keppel Electric (~1.3 GW). Solar generators (rooftop and utility-scale) participate as intermittent generators. Since the Open Electricity Market (2018), households can choose from 12+ licensed retailers competing on price and green tariff options.

4. Key issues and challenges

  • Gas supply security and LNG price exposure: The Indonesia Natuna piped gas contracts are progressively declining and will expire by ~2030. Singapore is increasingly dependent on LNG spot and term contracts. The 2022 LNG price spike (global gas crisis) drove tariffs to record highs (~S$0.39/kWh), exposing acute structural vulnerability. EMA is diversifying LNG supply sources and exploring hydrogen co-firing (20% blend feasibility by 2030) and blue hydrogen with CCS.
  • Solar deployment and the 3 GWp target: Singapore's 730 km² land area severely constrains solar deployment. The revised 2030 target of 3 GWp (up from 2 GWp) is achievable via aggressive rooftop, reservoir floating solar, and ground-mounted deployment on industrial land. However, a forecast ceiling of 5.3 GWp by 2035 remains well below peak demand (~6 GW). Variable solar also requires growing battery storage and grid flexibility investments.
  • Low-carbon energy imports (6 GW by 2035): LCEI is the only credible decarbonisation lever at scale given the solar ceiling. EMA has issued RFPs for large-scale imports from Cambodia, Vietnam, and Indonesia. The LTMS-PIP (200 MW current capacity) is a small fraction of the 6 GW target; scaling requires multi-year development of long subsea HVDC cables, political agreements, and grid code harmonisation across multiple ASEAN governments.
  • Data centre load management: Singapore hosts ~80 hyperscale data centres, with power demand ~3 TWh/yr in 2023 and growing ~15%/yr. The 2019 moratorium was lifted in 2022 with stringent PUE (≤1.3) and sustainability requirements. Future DC growth is being partially redirected to Johor (Malaysia) under the Johor–Singapore Special Economic Zone (SEZ) framework.
  • Net Zero 2050 pathway: Singapore's updated NDC (2024) maintains net zero by 2050. The electricity pathway relies on LCEI, hydrogen co-firing, and potential CCUS. The carbon tax rises to S$50–80/tCO₂ by 2030 (from S$25 in 2024), creating market signals but at levels still below abatement cost for most options. A credible pathway to net-zero power requires significant breakthroughs in low-carbon hydrogen economics and/or very large LCEI agreements.

5. Five key takeaways

1
Singapore runs on imported gas with no viable domestic alternative at scale — energy security is existential, not incidental, and drives all major policy decisions from LNG diversification to the LCEI import framework.
2
NEMS is ASEAN's most liberalised electricity market: competitive generation, full retail choice (since 2018), no generation subsidies — a governance model the region references but has not replicated.
3
The solar land ceiling is real (~5.3 GWp maximum domestically by 2035); the LCEI framework targeting 6 GW of regional low-carbon power by 2035 is the only credible decarbonisation lever at scale.
4
LNG spot price exposure is Singapore's biggest near-term tariff risk; the 2022 crisis (tariffs to S$0.39/kWh) accelerated action on supply diversification, long-term contracts, battery storage, and demand-side flexibility.
5
Singapore's regional grid interconnections are strategically undersized — LTMS-PIP Phase 2 at 200 MW is a fraction of the 6 GW LCEI target; scaling requires long-horizon political and financial commitments across multiple ASEAN governments.
