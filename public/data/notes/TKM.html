1. Energy context

Turkmenistan is the energy paradox of Central Asia. It sits atop the world's fourth-largest natural gas reserves — Galkynysh alone is one of the largest gas fields on earth — and yet remains one of the region's most economically constrained states, its extraordinary resource wealth captured by a small political elite and channelled into a patronage system that includes, as one of its pillars, subsidised or historically free electricity for the population. The sector is entirely gas-fired, entirely state-owned, and entirely closed to the outside world in terms of data and governance.

What is distinctive about Turkmenistan's electricity balance is that it generates more than it consumes domestically: approximately 46% of generation is exported to Afghanistan, Iran, and Uzbekistan. This export model reflects both the low domestic demand (a population of ~6 million in an arid country) and the geopolitical utility of electricity as a diplomatic instrument — particularly toward Afghanistan, whose power deficit makes it dependent on Central Asian and Iranian suppliers. The 2022 succession from Gurbanguly Berdimuhamedov to his son Serdar introduced no substantive policy change; the elder retains power as "National Leader" and the sector governance model is unchanged.

Turkmenistan's gas is so cheap domestically that building renewable energy to displace it would offer no near-term cost saving. The incentive for renewables exists only if: (a) gas exports earn hard currency and renewables free up gas for export, or (b) a carbon price or international pressure creates a relative cost advantage. Neither dynamic is operational today, though the export logic is increasingly discussed in policy documents.

With a population of ~6 million and a relatively modest industrial base (gas processing, chemical industry, some mining), domestic electricity consumption is estimated at ~14–15 TWh/year — well within the capacity of the existing fleet. The surplus (~13–15 TWh) is exported. Electricity has historically been provided to households at zero or near-zero cost as part of the state's energy subsidy package; this system has been gradually reformed since ~2021, with tariffs rising toward a target of approximately $0.05/kWh residential. The transition is gradual and politically managed: energy subsidies are a pillar of the government's social contract with the population.

Retail tariffs (2025, estimated): Residential tariffs have been raised from effectively zero to a target of approximately $0.05/kWh — a significant reform in political terms, though still among the lowest in the world. 2.3 Grid isolation and CAPS withdrawal

Turkmenistan withdrew from the Central Asian Power System (CAPS) in 2003, choosing instead to operate its grid in parallel with Iran's interconnected system. This makes Turkmenistan the only Central Asian country operating entirely outside both CAPS and Russia's unified energy system — a deliberate grid isolation reflecting Ashgabat's longstanding policy of formal "permanent neutrality" in international affairs (adopted 1995).

The practical consequence: Turkmenistan has no multilateral power exchange obligations, no participation in the regional balancing mechanisms that are becoming more important as CAPS evolves toward a market structure, and no direct electrical connection to Kazakhstan or Uzbekistan (with whom it shares land borders but no power lines). Electricity trade with Uzbekistan goes via Turkmenistan's own bilateral arrangement, not the CAPS coordination framework.

2.4 Cross-border trade

🇦🇫
Afghanistan — the geopolitical export Ongoing

Afghanistan is Turkmenistan's largest electricity export destination. Ashgabat has supplied northern Afghanistan via 220 kV lines for decades, and continued doing so after the Taliban's return to power in 2021 — making electricity one of the few normal bilateral relations between Turkmenistan and the Taliban administration. Afghanistan's domestic generation capacity is roughly 250 MW against a demand of over 1,000 MW; the deficit is split among Central Asian suppliers (Turkmenistan, Tajikistan, Uzbekistan) and Iran. Payment by the Taliban has been inconsistent; the physical flows continue. Turkmenistan has periodically threatened to cut supply over arrears but has not done so at scale.

🇮🇷
Iran — the grid partner Structural

Since leaving CAPS in 2003, Turkmenistan's grid has operated in parallel with Iran's interconnected system — meaning their alternating current frequencies are synchronised and power can flow bidirectionally without conversion. This is a structural grid relationship, not merely a trade arrangement. The volume and direction of flows depend on seasonal demand and generation profiles on both sides. The relationship gives Turkmenistan grid stability without multilateral obligations — consistent with its permanent neutrality doctrine.

3. Sector organisation and actors

3.1 Market structure

Turkmenistan's electricity sector is a fully vertically integrated state monopoly. Turkmenenergo controls generation, transmission, distribution and retail supply under a single government entity reporting directly to the presidency. There is no independent regulator, no market mechanism, no private participation, and no published financial accounts. Tariff setting is a government decision with no arms-length institutional process. Energy pricing has historically been a political instrument — free electricity was provided to households as part of the social contract until the gradual reform that began around 2021.

This model has persisted unchanged across the transition from Niyazov (1991–2006) to Gurbanguly Berdimuhamedov (2006–2022) to Serdar Berdimuhamedov (2022–present). The 2022 succession was designed to preserve continuity; the elder Berdimuhamedov retains institutional power as chairman of the upper house of parliament and "National Leader." No structural reform of the electricity sector has been signalled.

4. Key issues and dynamics

4.1 The export model — and its vulnerabilities

  • Turkmenistan's electricity export model — selling roughly half its generation to neighbours — is a deliberate political and economic strategy, not simply a surplus disposal problem. Exports to Afghanistan are an instrument of regional influence; exports to Iran reflect the grid synchrony relationship; exports to Uzbekistan reflect an economic arbitrage. The government has periodically signalled plans to cease or reduce electricity exports to redirect capacity toward domestic industrial development, though the timeline and seriousness of these signals are uncertain.
  • The Afghanistan relationship carries the most complexity: the Taliban government needs the electricity but does not reliably pay for it. Accumulated payment arrears create a political lever that Ashgabat has used — threatening cuts — without typically following through. The electricity supply is too important to Afghan political stability for Turkmenistan to cut entirely; but the arrangement is financially unsatisfying.
  • The ADB's $500 million transmission infrastructure upgrade (1,400 km of lines, 11 substations, underway since 2018) is the most significant external engagement with the Turkmen electricity sector in the post-Soviet era. It improves export infrastructure reliability and partially opens the sector to outside technical engagement — but does not change the market structure or governance model.

4.2 The end of free electricity

  • For most of the post-Soviet period, Turkmenistan provided free or near-free electricity, water, gas and petrol to its citizens — a social contract funded by the hydrocarbon wealth of the state. This system was introduced under Niyazov and maintained under Gurbanguly Berdimuhamedov as a core pillar of political legitimacy.
  • The dismantling began around 2021–2022, as fiscal pressures from commodity price volatility and the costs of the dynastic succession forced a gradual rationalisation. Residential electricity tariffs are being raised in stages toward approximately $0.05/kWh — still extremely low internationally, but a complete structural reversal from the free-provision era. The transition is managed carefully: too fast risks political instability; too slow perpetuates a fiscal drain on the state.
  • The reform signals some awareness that the subsidy model is unsustainable — but does not represent a move toward market pricing or sector governance modernisation. Turkmenenergo remains a monopoly with no accountability structures, and the tariff reform is entirely a top-down presidential decision.

4.3 The renewable opportunity not taken

  • Turkmenistan has 655 GW of identified solar potential — one of the highest absolute figures in Central Asia — and significant wind resources across the Caspian coast and eastern desert regions. The theoretical case for solar development is strong: displace domestic gas consumption to free up gas for export, earn hard currency from both the gas exports and, eventually, renewable electricity exports.
  • In practice, the sector remains at near-zero renewable capacity. The absence of competitive pressure, the extreme cheapness of domestic gas, the opacity of the investment environment, and the absence of an IPP framework have collectively made Turkmenistan the region's most inert renewable market. IFC and EBRD have had exploratory contacts; no project has reached commercial development.
  • Policy documents from 2022–2025 reference renewable energy targets ("80%+ renewable electricity", "100% renewable energy by 2030") but these appear aspirational rather than operational — there is no financing, no regulatory framework, and no private investor engagement that would make them credible near-term targets.

4.4 Grid isolation and regional integration limits

  • Turkmenistan's 2003 withdrawal from CAPS and grid synchronisation with Iran was a deliberate choice of bilateral dependency over multilateral integration — consistent with the country's "permanent neutrality" doctrine. It means Turkmenistan has no formal obligation to the CAPS balancing framework and no exposure to the regional electricity market integration that the World Bank's REMIT programme is now building.
  • The isolation also means Turkmenistan cannot benefit from the CASA-1000 export corridor (which routes Central Asian hydro to Pakistan), cannot access the Caspian Green Corridor (if it is built), and has no grid redundancy from the CAPS system in case of domestic supply disruption. The bilateral Iran link provides some stability; otherwise the system operates entirely on its own generation capacity.

5. Five things to take away

1
Turkmenistan generates more electricity than it consumes — roughly 46% is exported to Afghanistan, Iran and Uzbekistan. This export model funds the state and is a tool of regional influence, particularly toward Afghanistan. But payment reliability from Afghanistan is poor, and the long-term durability of the export business depends on neighbours finding or developing alternatives.
2
100% gas-fired, with the world's 4th largest natural gas reserves: there is no near-term supply constraint and no near-term structural pressure to change. The case for renewables — freeing up gas for hard-currency export — is rational but has not been acted upon. Zero renewable capacity in a country with 655 GW of solar potential is a policy choice, not a resource constraint.
3
The free electricity social contract is being dismantled, but slowly. Residential tariffs are rising toward ~$0.05/kWh from effectively zero. The reform is a fiscal necessity, not a market signal — Turkmenenergo remains a monopoly with no accountability structures and no published accounts.
4
Turkmenistan left CAPS in 2003 and operates its grid in parallel with Iran. It is the only Central Asian country entirely outside both CAPS and Russia's unified energy system — a deliberate choice reflecting the permanent neutrality doctrine. This isolation constrains any future regional electricity integration, including the Caspian Green Corridor and the World Bank REMIT regional market programme.
