1. Energy context

Senegal has historically been one of West Africa's most oil-dependent power systems — over 80% of electricity generation relies on heavy fuel oil (HFO) and diesel, making it acutely exposed to global oil price volatility and import bills that consume a disproportionate share of GDP. That picture is changing rapidly: first oil from the Sangomar field (June 2024, Woodside-operated) and first LNG from the Grand Tortue Ahmeyim (GTA) FLNG facility (shared with Mauritania, Phase 1 online early 2025) are transforming Senegal into a new oil and gas producer — with major implications for power sector fuel supply and fiscal space for energy investment.

Concurrently, Senegal has deployed one of West Africa's largest renewable energy programmes, with the Taiba N'Diaye wind farm (158.7 MW, Africa's largest onshore wind project when commissioned in 2023) and a growing solar pipeline. The Plan Sénégal Emergent (PSE) and the electricity access programme (PNER — Programme National d'Electrification Rurale) target universal electrification by 2025, a goal that has slipped but reflects genuine political ambition.

  • Oil / HFO (~81% of generation): The vast majority of Senegal's electricity is produced by SENELEC's own HFO plants (Bel Air, Boubakar Barry, Cap des Biches) and by the Karpowership floating power station (36 MW LNG/HFO, Dakar Bay). Diesel and HFO imports are the dominant fuel cost item. The Tobene Power Plant (125 MW, coal, Engie/Contour Global) was commissioned in 2022 using coal — controversial given climate commitments.
  • Solar (~7% of generation): Several utility-scale solar plants are in service: Malicounda Biomass/Solar (60 MW, SENELEC/CEB), Ten Merina Dakhar (30 MW), and several smaller IDA/AfDB-supported projects. Rooftop solar is growing in Dakar. Target of 1 GW of solar by 2030 is under active development.
  • Wind (~5% of generation): Taiba N'Diaye wind farm (158.7 MW, CWP Global/Lekela Power, 46 km north of Thiès) was Africa's largest onshore wind project on commissioning (2023). A second phase and additional wind sites are planned.
  • Hydro (~4% of generation): Senegal's OMVS share of the Manantali hydropower plant (200 MW total, Senegal's share ~33% = ~66 MW) provides the main domestic hydro contribution. Seasonal river flow variation is significant.

3. Sector organisation

SENELEC — Société Nationale d'Electricité Vertically Integrated SOE

SENELEC is a state-owned enterprise that generates (~50% of output from its own plants), transmits, and distributes electricity across Senegal. Unlike CIV's affermage model, SENELEC retains direct operational control of its assets. IPPs sell electricity to SENELEC under PPAs, and SENELEC distributes to customers. SENELEC's financials are stressed by HFO import costs (~40% of operational expenditure) and tariffs that have historically been set below cost recovery — government subsidy transfers were estimated at XOF 200B+ in 2022. SENELEC also manages the SEN side of OMVS power trade.

CRSE — Commission de Régulation du Secteur de l'Electricité Regulator

The CRSE is Senegal's electricity sector regulator, responsible for tariff setting, licensing, and quality-of-service standards. Tariff adjustments require CRSE approval and have periodically been delayed by cost-of-living considerations. The introduction of a "vérité des prix" (cost-reflective pricing) reform has been a recurring policy commitment but implementation has been partial.

Key IPPs — CWP/Lekela, Tobene Power, Karpowership Independent Producers

CWP/Lekela Power (Taiba N'Diaye wind, 158.7 MW) and several solar IPPs sell to SENELEC under 20-year PPAs at competitive tariffs. Tobene Power (Engie/Contour Global, 125 MW coal, Thiès) has been controversial for its coal fuel source. Karpowership (36 MW floating HFO/LNG unit, Dakar Bay) provides peaking capacity at relatively high cost. Under the new Electricity Code (2021), the IPP framework has been updated to facilitate renewable energy procurement through standardised contracts.

4. Key issues and challenges

  • Oil-to-gas transition with GTA LNG (2025): The Grand Tortue Ahmeyim FLNG facility (BP/Kosmos/PETROSEN/SMHPM, Phase 1 capacity ~2.5 MTPA) delivered first LNG in early 2025. While the bulk of production is earmarked for export, Senegal has provisions to purchase domestic gas at preferential rates for power generation. Building out the midstream infrastructure (pipelines, regasification) to shift SENELEC's fleet from HFO to gas is now the key infrastructure challenge. Gas-to-power conversion could reduce electricity generation costs by 30–40%.
  • Subsidy burden and tariff reform: SENELEC's HFO dependence means electricity generation costs are highly volatile. Government subsidies to keep residential tariffs affordable consumed a growing share of the budget in 2021–2023 (oil price shock). With GTA gas coming online, there is a fiscal window to restructure tariffs toward cost-reflective levels while passing fuel-cost savings to consumers.
  • Rural access gap: Senegal's overall electrification of 79% masks a stark urban/rural gap: urban areas (Dakar, Saint-Louis, Thiès) are close to 100% electrified, while rural areas are at ~56%. The PNER programme uses SENELEC grid extension and off-grid solar (mini-grids, solar lanterns) to close this gap, but the economics of last-mile rural electrification remain challenging without sustained subsidy.
  • OMVG interconnection and regional integration: Senegal is a member of both OMVS (with Mali and Mauritania, the Senegal River authority) and OMVG (with Gambia, Guinea, and Guinea-Bissau, the Gambia River authority). Both institutions manage regional hydropower and transmission infrastructure. The OMVG Kaléta–Gambia–Bissau link becoming operational in 2023 is a significant step toward a coherent Atlantic West Africa sub-regional grid, with Senegal as the central node.

5. Five key takeaways

1
Senegal's electricity sector is undergoing a structural transformation: the Sangomar oil field (June 2024) and GTA LNG (2025) have made Senegal an oil and gas producer for the first time, with the potential to convert the grid from expensive imported HFO to cheaper domestic gas — a once-in-a-generation cost reduction opportunity.
2
SENELEC remains heavily oil-dependent (81% of generation from HFO/diesel in 2023), creating cost volatility that is managed through government subsidies rather than market reform — the GTA gas transition is an opportunity to reset this subsidy dynamic while maintaining affordability.
3
The Taiba N'Diaye 158.7 MW wind farm established Senegal as a benchmark for large-scale renewable energy in francophone West Africa — demonstrating that competitively-tendered RE projects can reach financial close and deliver against schedule in the WAPP zone.
4
Senegal plays a critical bridging role between OMVS (Mali, Mauritania) and OMVG (Gambia, Guinea-Bissau, Guinea) — the two overlapping river basin organisations — making it a potential transit hub for West Africa's hydropower-rich interior to reach the Atlantic coast grid.
5
The rural electrification gap (56% rural vs. near-100% urban) is Senegal's most politically and economically consequential energy challenge — requiring a combination of grid extension, off-grid solar, and rural energy subsidy reform that is more complex and expensive than the urban supply improvements of the past decade.
