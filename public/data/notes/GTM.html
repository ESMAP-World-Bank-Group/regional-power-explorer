Generation Mix & Capacity

Guatemala has the most advanced and diversified electricity market in Central America, with approximately 3.2 GW of installed capacity and a renewable share of 83.5% as reported by the AMM (wholesale market operator) at end-2024. The mix is distinctive: hydro leads at 48% of installed capacity, but biomass (largely sugarcane bagasse cogeneration) contributes a remarkable 28% — a structural feature unique in the region. Solar PV surged in 2023, reaching 17% of generation (up from under 2% in 2022), signalling the beginning of a solar acceleration phase.

Market Structure

Guatemala operates the most liberalised electricity market in Central America — a three-tier model separating generation (competitive), transmission (INDE monopoly), and distribution (private concessions):

  • CNEE (Comisión Nacional de Energía Eléctrica): Independent regulator; sets tariffs and standards; technically sound institution.
  • AMM (Administrador del Mercado Mayorista): Private non-profit wholesale market operator; manages dispatch, cross-border trade via SIEPAC, and market settlements; one of the most effective market operators in the region.
  • INDE (Instituto Nacional de Electrificación): State company; owns and operates the national transmission grid; also generates through Chixoy HPP and several smaller plants; net revenues fund rural electrification.
  • EEGSA (Empresa Eléctrica de Guatemala): Enel-controlled distributor serving Guatemala City and surroundings (~1.5 million clients).
  • Energuate: Private distributor covering most of the rest of the country; owned by I Squared Capital.
Guatemala is Central America's largest electricity market and its most dynamic in terms of renewable energy investment. The AMM's competitive wholesale market has attracted dozens of private IPPs across hydro, solar, wind, biomass, and geothermal — a track record that contrasts sharply with the state-monopoly models of Honduras and Nicaragua. Guatemala is consistently a net exporter in the SIEPAC MER, sending surplus power to El Salvador and Honduras.

SIEPAC & Regional Role

Guatemala is the northern anchor of the SIEPAC transmission line (Sistema de Interconexión Eléctrica de los Países de América Central) — the 1,800 km regional grid linking all six SIEPAC nations. Key regional dynamics:

  • Guatemala is structurally a net exporter in the Regional Electricity Market (MER); biomass and hydro surplus flows south to El Salvador and Honduras during dry seasons.
  • The planned SIEPAC expansion includes a new interconnection with Mexico (SENER/CFE) at the northern border — which would give Guatemala access to Mexico's larger market and make it a transit node for bi-directional flows.
  • Guatemala's connection to Belize (under study) could extend regional integration further.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Guatemala's biomass/bagasse sector is a structurally unique and underappreciated competitive advantage. ~895 MW of seasonal cogeneration from sugarcane provides firm, dispatchable renewable capacity during the dry season — precisely when hydro weakens. This load-balancing role is not replicable in most markets and gives Guatemala lower backup thermal costs than its neighbours.
2
Solar PV grew from 2% to 17% of generation in a single year (2022→2023), with further growth expected. The 1.4 GW PEG-5 auction and ongoing private development will make solar Guatemala's largest generation technology by installed capacity within a few years. Managing the integration of variable solar into a predominantly hydro + biomass system is the key technical challenge ahead.
3
Guatemala's AMM-based market model is the regional benchmark. The separation of transmission (INDE) from generation and distribution, the private non-profit AMM as market operator, and CNEE as an independent regulator have produced a competitive, investment-attracting market that other SIEPAC countries have not replicated. This institutional advantage is durable and should be protected.
4
The planned Guatemala–Mexico interconnection is the region's most strategically significant cross-border project. Linking Guatemala to Mexico's national grid would dramatically expand the market for Guatemalan exports, provide a large balancing partner for variable renewables, and position Guatemala as the gateway between Mesoamerican and North American electricity markets.
5
Rural electrification remains incomplete despite market success. Urbanised commercial and industrial customers benefit from competitive generation markets; rural and indigenous communities in highland and jungle areas still face limited access. INDE's rural electrification mandate — funded partly by Chixoy revenues — has closed much of the gap, but the last mile remains a social infrastructure challenge distinct from the commercial market.
