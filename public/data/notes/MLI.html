1. Energy context

Mali is a large, landlocked, Sahel country that has historically relied on a combination of hydro power from the Niger and Bafing rivers (operated via the OMVS and national dams), imports from Côte d'Ivoire, and HFO/diesel thermal plants. The sector is managed by EDM-SA (Énergie Du Mali S.A.), the state-owned vertically-integrated utility that is chronically under-resourced, operationally stressed, and dependent on government subsidies to remain functional.

The security crisis has created a growing electricity challenge: jihadist attacks on transmission lines in the north and centre of the country have isolated the Mopti, Ségou, and Gao regions from the main grid. Emergency diesel generators have been deployed in isolated towns, at high fuel cost. Gold mining operations (Mali is Africa's third-largest gold producer) have largely shifted to self-generation. The electricity sector's fragility mirrors and compounds the broader governance crisis.

2. The electricity system

2.1 Generation mix

The 2023 generation figure (6.1 TWh) represents a sharp increase from 2022 (4.4 TWh), mainly due to expanded HFO thermal dispatch to compensate for drought-reduced hydro output from the Sélingué reservoir. HFO-intensive operation sharply increased fuel import costs and put major financial pressure on EDM-SA.

  • Oil / HFO (~79% of generation): Thermal plants at Darsalam (Bamako), Sirakoro, and Balingué run primarily on heavy fuel oil. EDM-SA also operates smaller diesel units in regional towns. HFO dependence makes the system acutely vulnerable to global oil prices and foreign exchange availability — EDM-SA's fuel bill consumed much of its revenue in 2022–2023 (oil shock). Emergency contracts with private fuel suppliers and generator rentals have been common.
  • Hydropower (~16% of generation): The Sélingué dam (44 MW, on the Sankarani river, a Niger tributary) is Mali's primary domestic hydro. The OMVS Manantali dam (200 MW total, Mali's allocation ~52% ≈ 104 MW) supplies Bamako via the 225 kV OMVS line. Rainfall variability and climate change are reducing reliable hydro output: the 2021–2023 Sahel droughts severely reduced Sélingué output. Félou (59.5 MW) is a run-of-river plant on the Senegal River managed under OMVS. Sotuba (5 MW, Bamako) is a run-of-river micro plant.
  • Solar (~2.5% of generation): Several IPP solar projects have reached completion: Kita (50 MW, Scatec), Ségou (33 MW), and distributed AMADER off-grid systems. Solar capacity is growing but is limited by grid absorption capacity and financing constraints. A government target of 200 MW solar by 2030 is aspirational under current conditions.

3. Sector organisation

EDM-SA — Énergie Du Mali Financially Stressed SOE

EDM-SA is the vertically-integrated state utility responsible for generation, transmission, and distribution in urban Mali. It is majority owned by the state (~66%) with a minority holding by IFC (formerly Saur International as concessionaire 2000–2005; concessionaire contract cancelled after financial difficulties). EDM-SA is chronically loss-making: tariffs are below cost recovery, HFO fuel costs have surged, and government subsidy transfers are irregular and insufficient. Technical and commercial losses exceed 30%. The military authorities government has periodically announced EDM-SA reform, but institutional coherence is limited under the current political environment.

AMADER — Rural Electrification Agency Off-grid / Rural

The Agence Malienne pour le Développement de l'Énergie Domestique et de l'Électrification Rurale (AMADER) manages rural electrification outside EDM-SA's service area through a concession-based off-grid model: private operators install and manage mini-grids (diesel + solar hybrid), with AMADER providing licensing and partial capital grants. AMADER has connected several hundred villages, but the security crisis in northern and central Mali has prevented expansion in conflict-affected areas and has rendered several existing mini-grids non-functional or abandoned.

4. Key issues and challenges

  • Security crisis and grid targeting: Jihadist groups (JNIM, affiliated with AQIM/Al-Qaeda) have repeatedly attacked electricity infrastructure in northern and central Mali — transmission pylons destroyed, substations targeted, and power plants in isolated towns threatened. The isolation of Kidal, Gao, and Mopti from the main grid has been a recurring crisis. Emergency diesel supply to isolated towns is expensive, logistically difficult under military escort, and politically fraught.
  • Hydro drought and HFO dependency trap: The 2021–2023 Sahel drought reduced Manantali and Sélingué output by 30–50% below average, forcing emergency HFO thermal generation at very high cost. EDM-SA's fuel bill consumed most of its revenue, creating payment arrears to CIE (CIV electricity supplier), HFO importers, and the government. The drought-thermal cycle is a structural trap: when hydro fails, expensive HFO must fill the gap, but EDM-SA cannot afford it without subsidies.
  • CIV supply dependency and geopolitical risk: Mali relies on CIV for ~30–40% of its electricity (via the 225 kV Ferkéssédougou–Bamako line). This dependency creates vulnerability: any CIV-side supply disruption (drought, security, generation failures) directly translates into load-shedding in Bamako. The geopolitical deterioration of Mali-France relations and Mali's AES alliance (with Burkina Faso and Niger, all three under military-led transitional governments) has not directly affected the CIV supply line — CIV is a politically different context — but the precedent of regional fragmentation is a risk.
  • Gold mining self-generation and lost revenue: Mali's gold mining sector (Barrick/Randgold, Hummingbird, Resolute, and others) has largely shifted to dedicated self-generation (HFO captive plants) following EDM-SA reliability failures. This represents a major lost revenue stream for EDM-SA (mining is the largest electricity consumer) and creates a paradox: the sector's most creditworthy potential customers have disconnected from the grid.

5. Five key takeaways

1
Mali's electricity crisis is inseparable from its security crisis: jihadist attacks on the transmission grid have isolated major population centres, made rural electrification expansion impossible in affected areas, and driven self-generation by the mining sector — the utility's most valuable customer base.
2
The 2021–2023 Sahel drought exposed Mali's hydro-dependent system as highly climate-vulnerable: Sélingué and Manantali output falls of 30–50% forced expensive HFO emergency dispatch, creating a financial crisis at EDM-SA that no amount of operational efficiency could resolve without major subsidy transfers.
3
Mali's 30–40% dependence on Côte d'Ivoire electricity imports via a single 225 kV corridor is a strategic vulnerability: CIV is a politically stable and reliable supplier today, but the arrangement is hostage to both CIV's own supply balance and the security of the southern Mali transmission corridor.
4
Solar offers Mali the most politically and logistically feasible path to diversification: solar resources are excellent (>5 kWh/m²/day), modular projects can be deployed quickly, and solar does not depend on river hydrology or imported fuel. Several projects (Kita 50 MW, Ségou 33 MW) demonstrate commercial viability even in the current environment.
5
EDM-SA's structural insolvency — tariffs below cost recovery, massive HFO fuel debt, government subsidy dependence — is the chronic illness underlying every acute crisis. Without tariff reform, commercial loss reduction, and a financial restructuring plan, no technical intervention (solar, hydro, regional imports) will create a sustainable sector.
