Generation Mix & Capacity

Nicaragua has approximately 1.9 GW of installed capacity, with a distinctive split between a large legacy diesel/HFO fleet (which dominates installed capacity at ~54%) and a growing renewable generation base that contributes approximately 75% of actual electricity output. The gap between installed capacity share and generation share reflects the low capacity factors of diesel plants, which run primarily as backup. Nicaragua has been one of Central America's most ambitious renewable energy developers, building geothermal, wind, hydro, and biomass capacity over the 2010s to break a historic diesel dependency.

Market Structure & Governance

Nicaragua's electricity sector is more state-controlled than Guatemala's liberalised market but has successfully attracted private renewable IPPs through concession arrangements:

  • MEM (Ministerio de Energía y Minas): Energy policy, concessions, and planning; created in 2007; close alignment with government economic priorities.
  • ENATREL (Empresa Nacional de Transmisión Eléctrica): State-owned transmission company; manages national grid and SIEPAC border points with Costa Rica and Honduras.
  • DISNORTE / DISSUR (Union Fenosa / Gas Natural): Two private distribution companies serving north and south of the country respectively; Spanish-owned; ongoing tariff tensions with regulator.
  • INE (Instituto Nicaragüense de Energía): Electricity regulator; sets tariffs; technically challenged institution.
  • Albanisa: Joint venture between state oil company PETRONIC and Venezuelan PDVSA (historically); involved in power generation projects and fuel supply; opaque governance.
Nicaragua's renewable energy transformation is one of the most dramatic in the Western Hemisphere. In 2007, Nicaragua imported expensive oil for ~75% of its electricity; by 2020, renewables were producing ~75% of electricity. This reversal — driven by geothermal, wind, biomass, and hydro IPPs — was achieved despite limited institutional capacity and challenging governance, demonstrating the power of resource endowment when combined with even modest regulatory openness.

Geothermal — Nicaragua's Strategic Advantage

Nicaragua's position on the Central American volcanic arc gives it geothermal resources unmatched outside Costa Rica in the region:

  • Momotombo (77.5 MW): One of the oldest geothermal plants in the Americas (first unit 1983); sits at the base of the Momotombo volcano on Lake Managua; national landmark and flagship renewable asset.
  • San Jacinto-Tizate (~120 MW): In Chinandega department; developed by Polaris Energy (now RAM Power); expanded in stages; largest geothermal plant in Central America.
  • Untapped potential: 1,500+ MW across multiple volcanic fields — if developed, could make Nicaragua a major geothermal electricity exporter to the MER.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Nicaragua's renewable transition is a success story overshadowed by governance challenges. The move from 75% fossil to 75% renewable generation in ~15 years is an extraordinary achievement. But the same political environment that enabled rapid concession-based development also creates investor risk: opaque Albanisa linkages, regulatory uncertainty, and the post-2018 political crisis that has reduced foreign investment across all sectors including energy.
2
Nicaragua's geothermal potential (1,500+ MW) is its most underdeveloped strategic resource. With only ~200 MW developed against 1,500+ MW of estimated potential, geothermal expansion is Nicaragua's highest-value energy opportunity. Geothermal provides firm, 24/7, carbon-free, low-variable-cost baseload — a combination increasingly valuable as the region adds more variable wind and solar. Attracting the specialised capital and expertise needed for geothermal exploration requires improved investor confidence.
3
The oversized diesel fleet represents a structural inefficiency that inflates system costs. ~1,020 MW of diesel/HFO capacity sitting at low utilisation factors (~10–15%) incurs fixed O&M costs without proportionate energy value. A systematic decommissioning plan — replacing diesel backup with battery storage and grid-scale wind/solar firming — would reduce system costs and improve the economic case for further renewable investment.
4
Nicaragua is chronically a SIEPAC net importer — primarily from Costa Rica — despite strong renewable resources. The combination of growing demand, seasonal hydro weakness, and inadequate peaking capacity means Nicaragua regularly relies on cross-border purchases. Developing 200–300 MW of additional geothermal would likely shift Nicaragua to a net exporter position and reduce its exposure to volatile MER spot prices.
5
The political risk premium on Nicaragua is now the binding constraint on energy investment. Post-2018 political developments, the reduced US diplomatic presence, and asset nationalisation risks in other sectors have all raised the perceived investment risk in Nicaragua. Renewable energy — especially geothermal — requires long-horizon, capital-intensive investment that is extremely sensitive to political risk. Until the investment climate improves, Nicaragua's geothermal potential will remain largely latent.
