1. Energy context

Liberia's electricity sector bears the deep imprint of two civil wars (1989–2003) that destroyed most of the country's infrastructure, including the 64 MW Mount Coffee hydropower plant (commissioned 1966, demolished 1990). The story of the Liberian electricity sector over the past two decades has been a slow, costly, and incomplete reconstruction — with the rehabilitation of Mount Coffee (88 MW, completed 2018 by Sinohydro with USAID/World Bank funding) as the most significant milestone. Even so, approximately 72% of the population lacks grid electricity access in 2024.

Liberia is part of the CLSG interconnection framework — the 225 kV ring connecting Côte d'Ivoire, Liberia, Sierra Leone, and Guinea. The Liberia section has been completed, allowing some CIV power to flow toward the Guinea border. When the Sierra Leone section of CLSG is completed, Liberia would be connected to a regional mesh grid for the first time, potentially accessing cheaper CIV hydropower at scale. Liberia's electricity tariff — around USD 0.30/kWh — is among the highest in the WAPP zone, reflecting the high cost of diesel thermal generation.

  • Hydro (~56% of domestic generation): Mount Coffee (88 MW, St. Paul River, 40 km east of Monrovia) was rehabilitated by Sinohydro under a World Bank/USAID/AfDB/EIB funded project and re-commissioned in 2018. It is by far Liberia's largest generator. Mount Coffee is a run-of-river plant sensitive to rainfall seasonality: output can drop sharply in the dry season, requiring diesel backup. Potential for additional hydro on the St. Paul and Cavalla rivers exists but is unexplored.
  • Diesel/HFO (~42% of domestic generation): LEC operates diesel/HFO thermal plants in Monrovia (Bushrod Island station, ~80 MW HFO) and several smaller diesel sets for backup. These are expensive to run and have contributed to Liberia's among-the-highest electricity tariffs in the region. Emergency rental units (Aggreko) have been used during peak demand periods. Provincial towns served by RREA are even more diesel-dependent.
  • Solar (~2%): Several small solar systems for off-grid rural communities (RREA programmes, NGO-funded), rooftop at LEC facilities, and a small utility-scale project. Solar expansion is in LEC's planning pipeline but financing remains the constraint.

3. Sector organisation

LEC — Liberia Electricity Corporation State Utility

LEC is the state-owned vertically-integrated electricity utility, managing generation, transmission, and urban distribution (Monrovia). LEC was reorganised after the civil war with USAID and World Bank support and has benefited from significant donor technical assistance. LEC's financial position is stressed: tariffs at ~USD 0.30/kWh are high but do not fully cover costs (diesel fuel is the dominant expense), and collection efficiency from government entities and informal settlements is poor. LEC serves approximately 70,000 registered customers, a small fraction of Monrovia's estimated 1.5 million population. A management contract with Manitoba Hydro International (2010–2016) was partially successful in improving operational practices.

RREA — Rural and Renewable Energy Agency Rural Off-grid

RREA manages rural electrification through a combination of diesel mini-grids in provincial towns and off-grid solar programmes in rural communities. RREA's approach to renewable energy, including several donor-funded solar mini-grid projects, has expanded access in areas LEC cannot economically serve. Provincial towns like Buchanan, Gbarnga, Tubmanburg, and Zwedru have small RREA-managed diesel mini-grids.

ArcelorMittal Liberia Industrial Self-generator

ArcelorMittal Liberia's iron ore mining operations at Nimba Mountain (production paused 2016, resuming ~2024) operated a dedicated HFO/diesel power plant for their mine and rail infrastructure. The mine's power plant is entirely self-sufficient and not connected to LEC. If AML iron ore mining resumes at full scale (~5 mtpa), it would represent the largest power demand in Liberia, entirely self-generated — representing a missed opportunity for LEC grid supply if grid extension to Nimba was ever feasible.

4. Key issues and challenges

  • Highest tariff in WAPP — affordability vs. cost recovery: Liberia's electricity tariff (~USD 0.30/kWh) is among the highest in West Africa, reflecting the high cost of diesel generation. This makes electricity inaccessible to most households and SMEs and limits productive electricity use. Despite high tariffs, LEC still requires subsidies due to collection inefficiencies and high operational costs. A transition to cheaper supply sources (Mount Coffee, CLSG imports) would allow tariff reduction — the most impactful pro-poor energy intervention available.
  • Mount Coffee seasonality — the hydrology trap: Like Bumbuna in Sierra Leone, Mount Coffee is a run-of-river plant that is highly seasonal. In the dry season, Mount Coffee output drops significantly, forcing LEC to increase diesel dispatch sharply. The combination of lower hydro generation and higher diesel costs creates a seasonal affordability spike — further pushing customers off the grid or into self-generation. A reservoir regulation component for Mount Coffee, or complementary solar to offset seasonal hydro gaps, would be transformative.
  • CLSG ring completion dependency: Liberia has completed its section of the CLSG ring, but the ring remains open at the Sierra Leone section. Until SLE completes its segment, Liberia cannot access CIV hydro power through Monrovia (the ring connects CIV→LBR→SLE→GIN; power from CIV to Monrovia requires the full loop via SLE to close). This is a structural dependency on another country's infrastructure completion schedule — an unusual exposure for a country that has done its own part of the investment.
  • Post-war reconstruction pace: Liberia's reconstruction has been one of the slower in West Africa, constrained by limited government fiscal capacity, a small tax base (mineral-dependent), and persistent governance challenges. Two Ebola epidemics (2014–2016, minor 2021) disrupted development trajectories just as the country was recovering from civil war. The electricity sector reflects this — 28% access after 20 years of post-war reconstruction is a sobering benchmark, even relative to other post-conflict peers.

5. Five key takeaways

1
Liberia's Mount Coffee rehabilitation (88 MW, completed 2018) is the most significant post-conflict electricity reconstruction achievement in the WAPP zone — bringing clean, cheap hydro power back to Monrovia 28 years after the civil war destroyed the original dam — yet 72% of the population still lacks access.
2
Liberia has the highest electricity tariff in the WAPP core zone (~USD 0.30/kWh) despite having only 28% electrification — a combination that reflects the high cost of diesel backup and the fragility of a system where cheap hydro supply is seasonal and alternative sources are absent.
3
Liberia has completed its section of the CLSG ring — the most commercially crucial WAPP investment it could make — but cannot benefit from cheap CIV hydro power until Sierra Leone's section is complete. This is perhaps the clearest example in WAPP of how regional infrastructure's benefits are hostage to the slowest member's completion pace.
4
ArcelorMittal Liberia's iron ore mine at Nimba operates (when active) entirely on self-generated power, disconnected from LEC — the country's single largest potential industrial customer chooses self-generation over grid supply, a stark reflection of LEC's reliability limitations and the opportunity cost of unreliable electricity for national resource mobilisation.
5
Completing CLSG's SLE section and reducing Liberia's exposure to Mount Coffee's seasonal output variability through complementary solar are the two most impactful near-term interventions for the Liberian electricity sector — both require donor and development bank continuity at a time when WAPP as a whole is competing for limited infrastructure capital.
