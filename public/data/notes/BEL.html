Generation Mix & Sector Overview

Belgium's electricity system is anchored by nuclear power at roughly 50% of generation — from seven reactors at Doel (Antwerp) and Tihange (Liège) operated by Engie/Electrabel. After years of political debate over a nuclear phase-out, Belgium has definitively reversed course: parliament repealed the 2003 nuclear ban law in May 2025, and the government signed a letter of intent to nationalise the Belgian nuclear sector in April 2026, targeting an expansion from 4 GW to 8 GW of nuclear capacity. The offshore wind fleet (2.26 GW, 399 turbines) provides ~10% of electricity. Belgium is a strongly interconnected market — Elia's grid connects to France, Netherlands, Germany, Luxembourg, and the UK (Nemo Link), making Belgium a critical European electricity transit hub.

The Nuclear U-Turn: From Phase-Out to Nationalisation

Belgium's nuclear policy reversal is one of Europe's most dramatic energy policy stories of the decade:

  • 2003: Belgium passes law requiring nuclear phase-out by 2025 — all reactors to close, no new construction permitted.
  • 2022: Russia invades Ukraine; European energy crisis. Belgium announces Doel 4 and Tihange 3 will be extended 10 years to 2035 — first breach of the phase-out law.
  • May 2025: Belgian parliament votes by large majority to repeal the 2003 nuclear ban law entirely. New nuclear construction is again legal.
  • April 2026: Government signs letter of intent with Engie/Electrabel to acquire all nuclear assets — effectively nationalising the Belgian nuclear sector. Rationale: the state needs to control long-term nuclear investment decisions; private operators cannot take 60-year investment horizons commercially.
  • Target: Double nuclear capacity from 4 GW to 8 GW through new units (design under evaluation) + SMR potential. Timeline: 2035–2040 for first new units optimistically.
Belgium's nationalisation of its nuclear fleet is the most significant European energy policy development of 2026 — and signals a broader truth: the clean energy transition cannot be delivered entirely by private capital with commercial time horizons. Nuclear's 60–80 year asset life, 15–20 year construction timeline, and exposure to political risk make it fundamentally incompatible with private financing on commercial terms. Belgium, like France (EDF), Finland (TVO as cooperative), and the UK (RAB model for Hinkley), has concluded that the state must own or guarantee nuclear to make it bankable. This is a structural lesson for any country planning new nuclear.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Belgium's nuclear reversal — from legislated phase-out in 2003 to nationalisation and capacity doubling in 2026 — is the most complete example of how energy security crises override long-standing political commitments to nuclear exit. The 2022 Ukraine shock compressed two decades of gradual policy evolution into two years of emergency decisions. What was politically impossible in 2021 (new nuclear) became the official state strategy by 2026. The lesson: nuclear policy reversals happen fast under crisis conditions, but delivery timelines (15+ years for new capacity) remain immune to political urgency.
2
Belgium's strategic position as Europe's electricity transit hub — interconnected to France, Netherlands, Germany, Luxembourg, and the UK — makes its supply adequacy a European problem, not just a Belgian one. During periods of French nuclear outage (which were severe in 2022), Belgium provided import cover. During Belgian nuclear maintenance seasons, France and Germany supply Belgium. Elia's cross-border flows run continuously in both directions across 5 borders. Any Belgian capacity shortfall (nuclear outage, delayed new build) propagates into neighbouring markets — making Belgium's nuclear investment decisions of European systemic importance.
3
The Princess Elisabeth offshore zone (3.15–3.5 GW) represents Belgium's largest single RE development and its first step toward an offshore wind capacity that could eventually match nuclear in contribution. Belgium's North Sea zone is small compared to the UK or Denmark, but the Elisabeth zone is being designed with next-generation specifications: direct DC coupling, potential hybrid interconnection to Norway, and integration with hydrogen production. If fully realised, it pushes Belgian offshore to ~5.5 GW — covering ~15–20% of national consumption from offshore alone.
4
Belgium's capacity market (CRM) has become a model that other European countries are adopting — and demonstrates that energy-only markets cannot guarantee supply adequacy in systems with high variable RE shares. As dispatchable generators (nuclear, gas) cannot earn adequate revenues from volatile spot prices alone in high-RE systems, capacity payments become necessary to maintain the firm capacity that balances the system. Belgium introduced this mechanism in 2021; France, Germany, Poland, and others have followed or are following. The design details (auction structure, capacity testing, cross-border participation) are the critical policy challenge.
5
Belgium's plan to expand nuclear from 4 GW to 8 GW faces the same fundamental challenge as all new nuclear in Europe: who bears the construction risk and cost overrun exposure? The nationalisation decision is an implicit answer — the Belgian state will bear it. But the capital required (~€15–20B for new units) competes with offshore wind, grid investment, and social spending. Belgium's debt-to-GDP ratio (above 100%) limits fiscal headroom. The practical question is not whether Belgium will nationalise nuclear but whether it can finance 8 GW of nuclear without crowding out other transition investments or straining sovereign creditworthiness.
