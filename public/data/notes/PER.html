Generation Mix & Capacity

Peru's power system rests on two pillars: Andean hydropower and the Camisea gas fields in the Amazon basin, whose output reaches the coast by pipeline and fuels the combined-cycle cluster at Chilca south of Lima. Between them they supply almost all generation, with hydro around 55% and gas most of the remainder.

The system's distinguishing feature is persistent overcapacity. Capacity was contracted on demand projections, largely mining-driven, that did not materialise, leaving installed capacity far above peak demand and depressing wholesale prices. That is comfortable for consumers and difficult for generators, and it has all but stopped new investment outside distributed and small renewables.

Non-conventional renewables remain a small share despite exceptional solar resource in the southern highlands and strong coastal wind. The RER auction scheme that brought the first projects has run infrequently, and with spot prices low there is little merchant case. Meanwhile roughly 4% of Peruvians, concentrated in rural Andean and Amazonian communities, still lack access.

Market Structure & Institutions

  • MINEM: The Ministry of Energy and Mines sets policy and awards concessions; electrification policy for rural areas sits here as well.
  • Osinergmin: The regulator, setting regulated tariffs and supervising quality and safety across electricity and hydrocarbons.
  • COES-SINAC: Dispatches the national interconnected system on audited variable cost and administers the short-term market. Like Chile and Brazil, Peru is cost-based rather than bid-based.
  • Regulated and free clients: Large consumers, principally mines, contract directly; the mining sector's load is large enough that its investment cycle drives national demand growth.
  • Private generation, mixed distribution: Generation is largely private after the 1990s reforms; distribution mixes state companies such as Distriluz with private utilities in Lima.
  • Interconnections: A modest 220 kV link to Ecuador exists but operates in isolated mode most of the time because of frequency and regulatory mismatch. Peru is, in practice, close to an electrical island despite sitting between Ecuador, Bolivia and Chile.
Peru has the opposite problem to most of the region: too much capacity, too cheap, and therefore no investment signal. Low prices mask the fact that the fleet is ageing, that renewables are barely present despite world-class resource, and that the rural access gap will not close on commercial terms. Fixing the investment signal without raising consumer prices is the central regulatory puzzle.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Overcapacity has frozen the generation investment cycle. With spot prices below the cost of new entry, nothing gets built without a regulated contract. That is stable in the short run and risky over a decade, because it defers the replacement of an ageing thermal fleet.
2
Camisea concentration is a single-point dependency. One field complex and one pipeline supply most thermal generation. The 2010s pipeline incidents demonstrated the exposure, and the long-proposed southern gas pipeline that would have diversified it has not been built.
3
Renewable potential is large and almost entirely unused. Solar irradiance in Moquegua and Tacna rivals the Atacama, and coastal wind is strong. Without regular auctions or a merchant case, that resource stays undeveloped while the region around Peru decarbonises.
4
The access gap is now a service quality and off-grid problem. The remaining 4% are in terrain where grid extension costs exceed any plausible tariff recovery. Progress depends on subsidised off-grid solar and on sustaining operation and maintenance, which is where similar programmes usually fail.
5
Peru is the missing link in an Andean corridor. A functioning Peru–Ecuador interconnection and an eventual Peru–Chile link would join the Andean market to the Southern Cone. The obstacles are regulatory harmonisation and political will rather than distance or cost.
