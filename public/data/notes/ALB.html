Generation Mix

Albania's electricity system is almost entirely renewable. Large hydropower dominates — approximately 95% of generation in normal rainfall years — with the Drin River cascade forming the backbone of the system. Solar is the fastest-growing new source following the commissioning of the 140 MW Karavasta plant in October 2023, the largest solar facility in the Western Balkans at the time. Installed capacity is now approximately 2.8 GW, with private producers accounting for over 50% of capacity for the first time in 2024.

Market Structure & Regulation

Albania's power sector is organised around three main state entities with a partially liberalised retail market. Reforms under the Energy Community and EU accession process are ongoing.

  • Regulator: ERE (Energy Regulatory Authority) — sets tariffs, licences operators, oversees Energy Community compliance.
  • KESH: Albanian Power Corporation — state-owned generator; operates the Drin cascade and holds the dominant position in wholesale supply.
  • OST: Transmission System Operator — manages 220/400 kV grid and interconnections; nominally unbundled from KESH.
  • OSHEE: Distribution and supply operator (state-owned) — serves retail customers; high distribution losses historically (>25%) though improving under reform pressure.
  • Market liberalisation: Eligible consumers (above a threshold) can access competitive supply; Energy Community obligations require further retail liberalisation and wholesale market development.
Albania's near-100% hydro grid is an environmental advantage but an operational liability. In low-rainfall years, the Drin cascade output can fall by 30–50%, triggering large import requirements and price spikes. The 2017 drought caused emergency imports at significant fiscal cost. Climate change makes this vulnerability structural, not episodic — solar and wind diversification is not optional.

Drought Vulnerability — The Systemic Risk

Albania's near-total dependence on hydropower makes it uniquely exposed to rainfall variability and climate change. The Drin River basin, which feeds all three KESH cascade reservoirs, is sourced from high Albanian and Macedonian watersheds. Drought years can cut hydro output by 30–50%, forcing large, expensive electricity imports from Greece and Montenegro — and straining the state budget when regulated retail tariffs prevent cost pass-through.

  • 2017 drought: OSHEE faced an €80+ million shortfall from emergency imports at spot prices while selling to consumers at regulated rates. The episode triggered substantial fiscal pressure and accelerated distribution loss reduction efforts.
  • Climate trajectory: Albania's NECP (October 2024) acknowledges declining average rainfall in key hydropower catchments. The Drin cascade's multi-year storage provides buffer capacity, but declining inflows over decadal timescales represent a structural challenge.
  • Diversification imperative: The Karavasta solar plant and Masdar JV pipeline are explicitly motivated by the need to reduce hydro dependency, not just add capacity. Even 500–700 MW of firm solar + wind would materially reduce drought-year import exposure.
Albania's green credentials are real — nearly 100% renewable electricity is a genuine distinction — but the lack of generation diversity makes the system brittle. A portfolio of solar and wind would not just add clean capacity; it would be the most important grid resilience investment Albania can make in the 2020s.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Albania is among the greenest electricity systems in Europe — but that is also its greatest vulnerability. Near-100% renewables is an asset in normal years; in drought years it creates severe supply deficits and import costs that can destabilise state finances. Diversification into solar and wind is not optional — it is the most urgent grid resilience priority.
2
The Drin cascade is the irreplaceable backbone of Albania's power system. Fierza, Komani, and Vau i Dejës together account for ~48% of installed capacity and a much larger share of actual generation. The multi-year storage capacity in Fierza reservoir provides seasonal flexibility that no other asset can replicate — protecting it and expanding it is a strategic priority.
3
The Masdar–KESH joint venture (2024) is potentially transformative. If the announced gigawatt-scale renewable development materialises, it could shift Albania from a structurally import-vulnerable system to a net green exporter — significantly improving energy security and generating export revenue. Execution risk is real; monitoring project progression is critical.
4
Distribution losses and OSHEE reform remain unfinished business. Historically above 25% (among Europe's highest), losses are improving but remain a significant economic drag. OSHEE's financial health and reform trajectory will determine whether investment in the distribution grid can keep pace with demand growth and EV uptake.
5
Albania's untapped hydro potential (~65% undeveloped) must be weighed against environmental and social costs. New large hydro faces serious constraints — EIA requirements, community opposition, river ecology concerns — that did not apply when the Drin cascade was built. Small hydro proliferation has caused real ecosystem damage. Future hydro development, if any, needs rigorous standards that the current permitting regime has not consistently applied.
