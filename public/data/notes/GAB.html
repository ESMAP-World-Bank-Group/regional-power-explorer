Generation Mix & Capacity

Gabon's electricity system is modest in size relative to the country's hydrocarbon wealth: SEEG, the state monopoly, operates approximately 450 MW of installed capacity producing ~2.8 TWh annually. Gas-fired plants — burning associated and non-associated gas from Gabon's offshore fields — account for about 60% of generation, with the Owendo and Port Gentil plants as the main assets. Hydro provides the remaining 40%, concentrated on the Ogooué River basin. Despite sitting on an estimated 5–6 GW of hydro potential, Gabon has developed less than 10% of it.

Market Structure & Regulation

Gabon's electricity sector is organised as a vertically integrated state monopoly. SEEG — Société d'Energie et d'Eau du Gabon — handles generation, transmission, distribution, and water supply under a concession from the state. The sector has faced persistent under-investment relative to Gabon's high per-capita income from oil revenues.

  • SEEG (100% state-owned): Operates all electricity and water services; monopoly concession; chronic under-investment in generation and grid despite Gabon's oil wealth. The company's dual electricity-and-water mandate has diluted focus on each.
  • ANARE-G (Agence Nationale de Régulation du Secteur de l'Eau et de l'Électricité): Regulatory agency; sets tariffs and supervises SEEG's concession; technically independent but limited in practice.
  • Ministry of Energy: Sets policy; key role in approving IPP entry and PPP structures for new projects.
  • IPP entry: The Ayémé Plaine solar project (Solen Renewables) represents the first significant private generation entry into Gabon's historically monopolistic sector. More IPP frameworks are under development.
Gabon is one of Central Africa's wealthiest countries per capita — yet SEEG's 450 MW capacity and chronic supply shortages reflect a longstanding failure to invest oil revenues into the power sector. Libreville and Port Gentil suffer regular load-shedding despite the country's hydro and gas riches. The political economy of oil-funded state spending has consistently crowded out the patient infrastructure investment that the power sector requires.

The Hydro Gap — 5–6 GW Potential, <10% Developed

Gabon's hydropower potential — estimated at 5,000–6,000 MW — is among the highest per-capita in Africa, yet less than 400 MW has been developed. The Ogooué River and its tributaries represent an extraordinary resource that a country of 2.3 million people has barely tapped. Constraints include:

  • Financing: Large-scale hydro requires patient concessional or multilateral finance; Gabon's middle-income status limits access to IDA-terms World Bank lending; commercial finance requires off-take guarantees that SEEG cannot easily provide given its financial position.
  • Grid capacity: Even if new hydro is built, the transmission grid cannot absorb it without reinforcement — and SEEG has not invested adequately in transmission.
  • Governance: SEEG's monopoly and weak regulatory framework have not created the conditions for large PPP hydro investment. The Ayémé Plaine solar IPP is an early test of whether the country can attract private generation at scale.
  • Kinguélé Aval (AfDB): A ~35 MW addition to the existing Kinguélé site, financed by the African Development Bank; represents the incremental, de-risked approach that has been more achievable than new large hydro.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Gabon's electricity sector vastly underperforms its resource endowment. With 5–6 GW of hydro potential, offshore gas, and high per-capita income, Gabon should have one of Central Africa's most reliable grids. Instead, it has 450 MW, regular load-shedding in its capital, and a utility whose finances are strained. This is a governance and investment prioritisation failure, not a resource constraint.
2
The 2023 coup and transition government create uncertainty about energy reform trajectory. The military takeover of August 2023 removed the Bongo family's 56-year rule. The transitional government has signalled commitment to economic reform, but the energy sector's institutional direction — particularly SEEG's future ownership structure and IPP policy — remains unclear.
3
Ayémé Plaine solar (2024) is a sector-opening moment. The first utility-scale IPP solar project breaking SEEG's effective generation monopoly is significant beyond its 30 MW capacity. If it operates successfully and the regulatory framework holds, it opens the door to a pipeline of RE projects that Gabon's grid urgently needs.
4
Gas monetisation and power are inseparable in Gabon's energy future. Associated gas currently flared from offshore production could fuel additional gas plants; better utilisation of this resource for domestic power generation is both economically rational and environmentally preferable to flaring. The Port Gentil CCGT model could be replicated with minimal fuel cost if gas gathering infrastructure is extended.
5
Gabon's hydro could power Central Africa if transmission ever materialises. As a CAPP member, Gabon sits at the centre of the Congo Basin hydro corridor. If cross-border transmission infrastructure — connecting Cameroon, Gabon, Congo-Brazzaville and DRC — were ever built, Gabon's undeveloped hydro would be among the first assets to be commercially viable. This remains a long-horizon aspiration, but it is worth tracking as regional integration discussions evolve.
