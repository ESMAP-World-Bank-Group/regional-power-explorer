Electricity Sector Overview

The Central African Republic (CAR) is one of the world's least electrified countries. With a national electrification rate of approximately 17% — and rural access of just 2% — over 80% of the population has no grid connection. The sector has been severely damaged by decades of political instability and armed conflict; much of the country outside Bangui is effectively ungoverned, with no realistic prospect of grid extension in the near term. Installed capacity reached ~131 MW by 2023, having risen sharply from ~41 MW as World Bank-financed solar projects came online — notably the 25 MWp Danzi Solar Plant commissioned in November 2023.

ENERCA & Market Structure

ENERCA (Energie Centrafricaine) is the state-owned national utility responsible for generation, transmission, distribution, and commercialisation of electricity. It operates exclusively in Bangui and a small number of provincial capitals where grid infrastructure exists. ENERCA is financially distressed: tariff revenues do not cover generation costs, and the government cannot provide adequate transfers due to chronic fiscal constraints.

  • ENERCA: Vertically integrated monopoly; ~900 staff; operations concentrated in Bangui; effectively unable to extend services to conflict-affected provinces.
  • ASER (Agence de Stimulation Economique des Régions): Rural electrification agency; limited capacity; dependent on donor programmes.
  • World Bank (PURACELL programme): The most consequential active actor in CAR's electricity sector — financing the Danzi solar plant, the distribution network rehabilitation in Bangui, and the 15 MWp Danzi expansion. Without World Bank support, the sector would have barely evolved in the last decade.
  • Other donors: EU, AFD (French), AfDB — active in mini-grid and solar home system programmes for rural communities.
CAR's energy sector cannot be understood without the conflict context. Armed groups control significant portions of the national territory; the national army (FACA) and Russian Wagner Group (now Africa Corps) forces are the de facto security providers in much of the country. Infrastructure investment outside Bangui faces existential security risks. The World Bank's PURACELL programme — focused on the capital — represents the pragmatic ceiling of what can be achieved in the current environment.

Danzi Solar — A Turning Point

The commissioning of the 25 MWp Danzi Solar Plant in November 2023 was the most significant electricity sector development in CAR in decades. Located near Bangui, the plant more than doubled the capital's reliable generation capacity and reduced dependence on expensive diesel generation. Key facts:

  • Financed by the World Bank under the Emergency Project for Access to Electricity (PURACELL)
  • Connected directly to the ENERCA grid; supplies Bangui's residential and commercial consumers
  • A further 15 MWp expansion is being financed by the World Bank and IFC; when complete, Danzi will total 40 MWp — by far the largest generation asset in CAR
  • Demonstrates that utility-scale solar is viable in CAR despite governance challenges, creating a template for future donor-financed RE projects

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Peace is the prerequisite for energy development in CAR. Every energy access metric — electrification rate, grid coverage, investment volume — is constrained by armed conflict. The 2% rural electrification rate is not primarily a financing or technology problem; it is a security problem. No energy programme can scale while conflict prevents infrastructure deployment and maintenance.
2
Danzi Solar (2023) is the most consequential energy investment CAR has seen in a generation. The 25 MWp plant — with 15 MWp more in the pipeline — is transforming Bangui's power supply from diesel-dependent and unreliable to increasingly solar-anchored. This is a real improvement for the capital's ~1 million residents and a proof of concept for future donor-financed RE in CAR.
3
Off-grid solar is the only realistic electrification pathway for rural CAR. With 2% rural access and vast areas under armed group control, grid extension is impossible at scale. Solar home systems and mini-grids — delivered through NGOs and donors in areas with some security — are the only viable rural electrification technology. The 2030 target of 50% access is achievable only through massive off-grid deployment.
4
ENERCA is financially unviable without sustained donor support. Cost-reflective tariffs are politically impossible in one of the world's poorest countries; ENERCA's operating deficit is structural. The sector will remain donor-dependent for the foreseeable future. The question is not how to make ENERCA financially independent but how to structure donor support most effectively.
5
CAR has real hydro potential that cannot be developed under current conditions. The Ubangi and Sangha rivers have significant untapped hydro capacity. Under a stable governance environment, CAR could develop hydro resources that provide reliable baseload for a future national grid. That scenario is a decade or more away — but it should inform how donor programmes today (particularly mini-grid standards and grid architecture) are designed to enable future integration.
