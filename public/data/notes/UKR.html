Generation Mix & Capacity

Ukraine entered 2022 with one of Europe's largest power systems: roughly 55 GW of installed capacity, a nuclear fleet of fifteen reactors supplying over half of generation, and large coal and hydro fleets inherited from the Soviet system. It was a net exporter and had begun coupling its market to European rules under the Energy Community.

The full-scale invasion changed every parameter. The Zaporizhzhia plant, Europe's largest at six reactors and 5,700 MW, has been under occupation since March 2022 and produces nothing while requiring continuous off-site power for cooling. Thermal and hydro plants, substations and transformers have been targeted in repeated waves, and a large share of pre-war generating and transmission capacity has been damaged or lost.

Against that, Ukraine and Moldova synchronised with the Continental European grid in March 2022, weeks after an isolated-operation trial that happened to be under way. Commercial trade with the EU followed, and imports from Poland, Slovakia, Hungary, Romania and Moldova have become a structural part of keeping the system supplied through each winter.

Market Structure & Institutions

  • Ministry of Energy: Sets policy and coordinates the repair and resilience programmes with international partners.
  • NEURC: The National Energy and Utilities Regulatory Commission sets tariffs and market rules; its independence is a standing condition of EU and IFI support.
  • Ukrenergo: The transmission system operator and market operator for balancing, and Ukraine's counterpart in ENTSO-E. It carries the physical burden of reconnecting a grid under attack.
  • Energoatom: Operates the nuclear fleet, which supplies the majority of generation and is the reason the system has functioned at all.
  • Market model: A liberalised model on the EU template was introduced in 2019 with day-ahead, intraday, balancing and bilateral segments. Wartime price caps and emergency measures have since overridden much of it, and restoring cost-reflective prices is a central reconstruction issue.
  • Interconnections: Links to Poland, Slovakia, Hungary, Romania and Moldova, with trading capacity progressively increased by ENTSO-E since 2022. The Burshtyn island, long synchronised with Europe, was the precedent for full synchronisation.
Ukraine is the only European system operating under sustained attack, and it has stayed up. The lesson other systems are drawing is about distribution rather than generation: dispersed, repairable, partly self-supplied capacity has proved more resilient than a small number of large plants, and interconnection with neighbours has been worth more than any single asset.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Nuclear availability is the system's central variable. With the remaining reactors supplying the majority of generation, their operation and the integrity of the substations that evacuate their output determine whether the system holds through winter.
2
Occupied Zaporizhzhia is a nuclear safety issue independent of its output. The plant produces nothing but still needs reliable off-site power for cooling. Repeated loss of that supply has made it a continuing safety concern rather than an energy one.
3
Synchronisation with Europe was the decisive strategic move. It removed dependence on the Russian and Belarusian systems and made EU imports physically possible. Everything since, from emergency imports to market coupling, rests on that three-week operation.
4
Decentralisation is the reconstruction doctrine. Small dispersed units, cogeneration and rooftop solar with storage are being built precisely because they present no worthwhile target. This inverts the conventional economies-of-scale logic of power system planning.
5
Restoring cost-reflective tariffs is the hardest reconstruction question. Wartime price caps protect households and starve the sector of the revenue needed to repair and rebuild. Sequencing that adjustment, with donor support, will shape the sector long after the physical repairs.
