Generation Mix & Capacity

Bhutan's electricity sector is defined by a single technology and a single export market: 100% hydropower, ~70% exported to India. The country has ~3,490 MW of installed hydro capacity, developed almost entirely through bilateral inter-governmental agreements with India under which Indian agencies (NHPC, SJVN, THDC) financed and built the plants, with Bhutan taking an equity stake and the two countries sharing output. Hydropower constitutes approximately 63% of Bhutan's total export earnings — making it the backbone of the national economy, not merely the electricity sector. Export revenue reached INR 24.3 billion in FY2025.

DGPC & the India Relationship

Bhutan's electricity sector is institutionally simple: the Druk Green Power Corporation (DGPC) — a subsidiary of Druk Holding and Investments, Bhutan's sovereign wealth fund — manages all generation and export operations. There are no IPPs, no competitive market, no regulator in the conventional sense. The entire sector operates through bilateral agreements with India:

  • India finances construction (typically 60% grant + 40% loan) and receives ~70% of output at a negotiated rate; Bhutan retains the rest for domestic use and gets an equity share in each project.
  • DGPC sells to India's NTPC and state utilities via long-term PPAs. Seasonal dynamics: Bhutan exports heavily May–November (monsoon) and imports from India December–April (lean season).
  • The Adani Group–DGPC MoU (2024) for 5,000 MW of new hydro and pumped storage marks the first private sector engagement in Bhutan's generation sector at scale.
  • The World Bank Dorjilung financing (May 2026) introduces multilateral development bank financing alongside Indian bilateral funding — diversifying Bhutan's project financing base for the first time.
Bhutan is perhaps the purest example in the world of a country whose entire economic development strategy is built around a single clean energy export: hydropower to India. With 63% of export earnings from hydro, Bhutan's fiscal health, development spending, and even its Gross National Happiness philosophy of sustainable development rest on the India-financed HPP pipeline continuing to deliver. The pipeline expanding to 15 GW by 2040 — if realised — would make Bhutan the largest clean energy exporter per capita of any country in Asia.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Bhutan's hydropower model is uniquely successful but uniquely concentrated. 100% hydro, 70% exported, 63% of national export earnings from a single sector to a single customer — this concentration delivers extraordinary development outcomes in normal times but creates systemic vulnerability to any disruption in the India relationship, the Himalayan hydrological cycle, or Indian demand/payment patterns.
2
The completion of Punatsangchhu-I and II in 2025 roughly doubles Bhutan's installed capacity and export revenue potential. Adding 2,220 MW in a single year (to a country that had ~1,270 MW before) is an economic transformation event. Managing the revenue windfall from ~INR 24B to potentially INR 50B+ requires careful fiscal management to avoid the resource curse dynamics that have afflicted other hydropower-dependent economies.
3
The Adani–DGPC MoU for 5,000 MW signals a strategic shift toward private sector involvement in Bhutan's generation sector. India's state-financed bilateral model has delivered Tala, Mangdechhu, and Punatsangchhu. But with the pipeline target of 15 GW by 2040, the scale of required investment exceeds what bilateral GoI grants and loans can absorb alone. Private capital — through the Adani MoU and potentially other PPP structures — is necessary. Managing private investor returns alongside Bhutan's sovereignty over its rivers is the key regulatory design challenge.
4
Glacier retreat is Bhutan's existential long-term hydro risk. The Hindu Kush Himalaya holds one of the largest concentrations of glacial ice outside the poles. Bhutan's rivers — the Wangchu, Mangde Chhu, Punatshang, Kurichhu — are fed substantially by glacial meltwater. As glaciers retreat under climate warming, river flows will first increase (more melt), then eventually decline (glaciers exhausted). The 2050–2100 horizon represents a potential slow-motion threat to the hydro revenues that fund Bhutan's entire development model.
5
The World Bank's first Bhutan hydro financing (Dorjilung, 2026) is strategically significant beyond its 125 MW. Introducing multilateral development finance alongside bilateral Indian funding diversifies Bhutan's financing base, brings World Bank governance standards (environmental and social safeguards, procurement transparency), and signals Bhutan's opening to a broader set of international energy finance relationships. This precedent, if followed by ADB and others, could accelerate the pipeline beyond what India–Bhutan bilateral capital can support.
