1. Energy context

Somaliland — the self-declared Republic of Somaliland, capital Hargeisa, population approximately 4 million — has operated as a de facto independent state since its 1991 declaration of independence from Somalia. Unlike South Somalia, Somaliland has maintained relative stability, held multiparty elections, and built functioning government institutions, including a basic electricity infrastructure centred on Hargeisa.

The dominant electricity entity is SomPower (formerly Hargeisa Electricity Agency/HEA), the state-owned utility serving the capital. SomPower operates approximately 38 MW of diesel generation and 20 MW of solar PV — the solar installed in phases from 2019 onwards and representing a significant share of a small system. Other cities (Berbera, Borama, Burao, Las Anod) are served by separate isolated diesel grids, some under SomPower jurisdiction and some under local municipalities or private operators.

Electricity tariffs in Somaliland (~USD 0.57–0.59/kWh) are among the highest in Africa and significantly above both the Somali average and most EAPP members, reflecting the near-total reliance on expensive diesel fuel. The solar programme has begun to reduce costs modestly; further solar deployment is the stated government priority. The big geopolitical question for Somaliland's energy future involves its relationship with Ethiopia — the January 2024 MoU included proposals for Ethiopian energy investment in Somaliland in exchange for Red Sea port access, but this deal was significantly complicated by the Ankara Agreement of December 2024.

2. The electricity system

2.1 Generation

  • SomPower — Hargeisa: ~38 MW diesel HFO + ~20 MW solar PV (phased installations 2019–2023). Solar was partly financed by the African Development Bank and private developers. The hybrid system reduces diesel consumption by ~15–20% when solar is generating, improving economics and reducing import costs. Hargeisa grid is a 33/11 kV distribution system. Supply hours: reported 12–18 hours per day, variable by season and maintenance status.
  • Berbera: SomPower sub-operation; ~5–8 MW diesel. Berbera is Somaliland's main port, being developed by DP World (UAE). Port expansion and potential free zone development will increase electricity demand substantially; additional generation investment is planned.
  • Other cities: Borama, Burao, Erigavo, Las Anod — isolated municipal or private diesel generators, typically 1–5 MW. Supply is intermittent. Las Anod is politically contested (2023 clashes between Somaliland forces and SSC-Khatumo/Puntland-aligned groups); electricity supply there is severely disrupted.
  • Off-grid solar: As in Somalia proper, PAYGo solar home systems are rapidly growing in peri-urban and rural Somaliland due to high diesel grid tariffs. Off-grid solar is price-competitive against SomPower's tariff at USD 0.57–0.59/kWh.
Las Anod conflict (2023–present): Clashes between Somaliland forces and SSC-Khatumo independence movement (backed by Puntland) in Las Anod (Somaliland's eastern capital) in early 2023 resulted in significant displacement and infrastructure damage. Las Anod's electricity supply has been severely disrupted. This reduces effective Somaliland-controlled territory and complicates eastern expansion of grid infrastructure.

2.2 The Ethiopia MoU and its aftermath

In January 2024, Somaliland President Muse Bihi Abdi and Ethiopian Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed signed a Memorandum of Understanding granting Ethiopia commercial and military access to Berbera port on the Red Sea — potentially including a naval base — in exchange for Ethiopia recognising Somaliland's independence and providing economic support, including possible energy investment. Ethiopia (landlocked since Eritrea's independence in 1993) has sought Red Sea access as a strategic priority.

The deal provoked immediate and forceful opposition from the Federal Government of Somalia (which considers Somaliland its sovereign territory) and Egypt (which feared Ethiopian strategic access to the Red Sea could change the balance of power in the Nile basin dispute). Turkiye subsequently mediated the Ankara Agreement (December 2024) between Ethiopia and Somalia, which included Ethiopia committing to respect Somalia's sovereignty — effectively superseding the MoU with Somaliland. President Abiy Ahmed did not formally withdraw from the January 2024 MoU, creating legal and diplomatic ambiguity.

For Somaliland's electricity sector, the MoU had represented a potential pathway to Ethiopian grid interconnection — which would dramatically reduce dependence on diesel — but Ankara has pushed this prospect into the uncertain future.

4. Key issues and dynamics

4.1 Recognition constraints — the binding limit on investment

  • Somaliland's non-recognition status is the primary structural constraint on electricity development. Multilateral development banks (World Bank, AfDB, IFC) cannot lend directly to unrecognised entities. Investment treaties, sovereign guarantees, and normal diplomatic channels for project finance are unavailable. What investment has occurred has been routed through Somalia umbrella mechanisms (politically complicated), bilateral donors working informally, or diaspora capital.
  • The Berbera port (DP World concession, UAE investment) demonstrates that significant private investment is possible via commercial deal-making that does not require UN recognition. But electricity infrastructure — requiring long-term financing, regulatory stability, and government guarantees — is harder to finance commercially than port logistics.

4.2 Solar transition potential

  • At USD 0.57–0.59/kWh diesel tariff, Somaliland has among the strongest economics for solar in Africa. Solar irradiance (~2,100 kWh/m²/year in Hargeisa) is high; the population is urban-concentrated enough that mini-grid solar is viable; and diaspora investment capital (Somaliland has a very large diaspora in the UK and Nordic countries) provides an unusual off-budget financing channel.
  • SomPower's 20 MW solar installation is a proof of concept. Scaling to 50–100 MW of solar in Hargeisa would substantially displace diesel and cut tariffs. AfDB engagement via Somalia umbrella is the most viable multilateral route; bilateral engagement from UAE-aligned investors (given Berbera port) is another channel.

5. Five things to take away

1
Somaliland has functioning de facto governance and a basic electricity utility (SomPower) serving Hargeisa and Berbera — a meaningful contrast with South Somalia. However, the lack of international recognition prevents normal development finance, creating a structural financing gap that diaspora capital, UAE investment, and informal AfDB/WB umbrella mechanisms only partially fill.
2
Electricity tariffs of USD 0.57–0.59/kWh are among Africa's highest, reflecting near-total diesel dependence. SomPower's 20 MW solar hybrid installation (Hargeisa) demonstrates the transition economics: high solar irradiance and high diesel cost make solar commercially compelling. Scaling solar to 80–100 MW in Hargeisa would cut tariffs and improve reliability, but requires financing that the recognition constraint makes difficult.
3
The January 2024 Ethiopia-Somaliland MoU — which proposed Ethiopian recognition and energy investment in exchange for Red Sea port access — was the most significant geopolitical development for Somaliland's energy future in years. The December 2024 Ankara Agreement (Turkiye-mediated Ethiopia-Somalia normalisation) has cast this deal into uncertainty. Whether Ethiopia ultimately pursues the Berbera arrangement or formally abandons it will have major implications for Somaliland's infrastructure investment prospects.
4
The Las Anod conflict (2023–present) between Somaliland forces and the SSC-Khatumo movement has reduced Somaliland's effective territorial control and disrupted eastern infrastructure. It is a reminder that Somaliland's relative stability — often cited as distinguishing it from South Somalia — is not guaranteed and depends on managing sub-national political tensions that could escalate.
5
For development partners, engaging Somaliland's electricity sector requires navigating the recognition constraint creatively: Somalia umbrella instruments, commercial investment facilitated through third-country entities, and diaspora capital mobilisation are the available channels. Direct sovereign lending is not possible. The institutional capacity of SomPower and the Somaliland Ministry of Energy is modest but functional — more so than most comparable fragile state entities.
