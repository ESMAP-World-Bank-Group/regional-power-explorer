Generation Mix & Sector Overview

Mauritius — a volcanic island of 2,040 km² in the Indian Ocean with a population of approximately 1.3 million and a GDP per capita of approximately US$12,000 (upper middle income) — is the most economically developed Indian Ocean SIDS and one of Africa's most prosperous economies. Its electricity sector reflects this relative development: a grid that functions at near-European reliability standards (less than 30 minutes average annual outage), a mix of state utility (CEB) and independent power producers (IPPs), and a generation portfolio that uniquely includes significant bagasse-fired cogeneration from the sugar cane industry. However, Mauritius also has one of the least RE-diversified electricity mixes of any upper-middle-income island state: approximately 45% of electricity comes from coal (entirely imported), and the transition from coal to RE is the defining challenge of Mauritius's energy sector in the 2025-2035 period.

The Central Electricity Board (CEB) is the state transmission and distribution utility; generation is split between CEB's own plants and IPPs. Coal: CEB's St. Louis Power Station (~110 MW) and IPP coal plants (Nicolay Power Station, operated by Terragen Holdings) supply approximately 45% of generation from coal imported from South Africa and Mozambique. Bagasse (sugar cane fibre residue): three sugar group cogeneration plants — Omnicane (Les Mares, Savannah), Terra (CIPREL), and ALTEO (Beau Plan) — supply approximately 13% of national generation during the crushing season (July-November), providing firm seasonal RE. Solar PV: a feed-in tariff (FIT) programme launched 2015 has driven approximately 120 MW of rooftop and ground-mounted solar installation; the first utility-scale solar tenders (50 MW + 50 MW BESS, 2023 tender) will commission 2025-2026. Wind: modest (~20 MW at Plaine des Roches and other sites). CEB is also constructing a 600 km submarine cable connecting Mauritius main island to Rodrigues Island (~40,000 people, currently diesel-dependent) — an ADB-financed project targeting commissioning in 2026.

Bagasse: Mauritius's unique seasonal firm RE from the sugar cane industryMauritius's sugar industry — which once dominated the economy and still accounts for approximately 60,000 hectares of cane cultivation — produces bagasse (the fibrous residue after juice extraction) as a by-product. Three sugar group energy subsidiaries (Omnicane Les Mares ~80 MW, Omnicane Savannah ~60 MW, Terra CIPREL ~70 MW, ALTEO Beau Plan ~40 MW) operate bagasse-fired cogeneration plants that supply electricity to the grid during the July-November crushing season. Bagasse cogeneration is genuinely firm and dispatchable during the season — unlike wind and solar, it can match demand in real time. Outside the crushing season, these plants operate on coal or imported biomass (woodchips). Total bagasse contribution: approximately 13% of annual generation, concentrated in five months. Mauritius's bagasse model is the clearest example in the Indian Ocean region of an agricultural by-product providing firm RE at utility scale — a model of interest to Fiji (FSC bagasse) and other sugar-growing SIDS.
Coal dependency and the just transition challengeMauritius committed to "no new coal" in its 2022 NDC and 2023 Long-Term Low Emissions Development Strategy. CEB's St. Louis coal plant is operated by CEB; the IPP coal plants are operated under long-term power purchase agreements (PPAs) that expire 2028-2032. The just transition challenge: approximately 1,500-2,000 workers are employed directly or indirectly in coal-fired generation (CEB, Terragen, port handling, fuel procurement). These are well-paid, skilled jobs relative to Mauritian alternatives — the transition to solar and BESS creates fewer direct jobs per MW than coal plant operations. Mauritius's robust labour market (near full employment, significant Indian and Bangladeshi migrant workers in manufacturing) means coal worker transition is more manageable than in larger coal economies, but it still requires explicit transition support (retraining, early retirement schemes) to prevent opposition to the coal phase-out from this workforce constituency.
Financial services, tourism, and electricity reliability requirementsMauritius's economy rests on two pillars: financial services (IFC, offshore banking, fund administration — approximately 12% of GDP) and tourism (~1.5 million visitors/year, ultra-premium market, approximately 8% of GDP). Both sectors are electricity-quality-sensitive in ways that commodity-based economies are not. Financial services firms require sub-second power quality (UPS, generator backup) — a grid outage that causes an uninterruptible power supply to activate is a business disruption event. Premium resorts (Four Seasons, One&Only, Constance) with grid interruptions receive compensation claims and TripAdvisor complaints. CEB's high reliability (SAIDI <30 minutes/year) is therefore a competitive asset for the economy, not just a utility metric — and the RE transition must maintain or improve this reliability standard as coal plants are retired. The first 50 MW solar + 50 MW BESS tender (2023) explicitly specified grid-firming capability as a procurement requirement.
Rodrigues submarine cable: ending a 40,000-person island's diesel dependencyRodrigues Island (~40,000 people, 600 km east of Mauritius) has been electrically isolated from the main island, relying on a diesel plant (~10 MW) operated by CEB's Rodrigues branch. Diesel for Rodrigues is shipped by tanker — one of the Pacific-equivalent logistics premium situations in the Indian Ocean. CEB and ADB have financed a submarine cable connecting Rodrigues to the Mauritius main grid (600 km, 40 MW capacity — one of the longest island interconnection cables in the Indian Ocean; ADB loan ~US$200M, commissioning projected 2026). When commissioned, Rodrigues will receive electricity from Mauritius's main island grid — including the growing RE share — eliminating Rodrigues's diesel dependency entirely. The Rodrigues cable is the largest single investment in Mauritius's electricity infrastructure in decades and will transform both Rodrigues's energy cost (from diesel tariff to grid tariff, approximately 40% reduction) and its emissions profile.

Accelerating the 27%→60% RE Transition

Mauritius's 60% RE target by 2030 — from 27% today, in six years — requires approximately 300-400 MW of additional RE plus 200 MW of grid-scale BESS: the most ambitious electricity transition among Indian Ocean SIDS by absolute MW. The pipeline: 100 MW solar + 100 MW BESS under 2023/2024 tenders; 100 MW additional solar (2025 tender, pre-development); offshore wind pre-feasibility assessment (2024, 200 MW conceptual) ongoing; expanded FIT rooftop solar; extended bagasse capacity (coal co-firing shifted to higher bagasse fraction). The binding constraints are not financial (Mauritius's investment grade sovereign rating enables EIB, ADB, and DFI financing) but land (2,040 km² island, significant land competition from agriculture, tourism, and housing) and grid management (rapid RE integration on a mid-size island grid without frequency collapse).

Mauritius is the most economically advanced and electricity-sophisticated SIDS in this briefing series — a country with near-European grid reliability, IPP market development, a functioning regulatory framework, and an ambitious but achievable 60% RE 2030 target. Its transition challenge — phasing out imported coal while maintaining the premium power quality that its financial services and tourism economy requires — is a just transition and grid management problem more than a financing problem. Getting it right matters beyond Mauritius: it demonstrates to Indian Ocean peers and the broader middle-income SIDS group that coal phase-out and premium reliability are compatible.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Mauritius's bagasse cogeneration model — where sugar industry by-product provides approximately 13% of national electricity as firm, dispatchable, seasonal RE — is the Indian Ocean's most successful agro-industrial energy integration case study and should be formally documented and packaged by IRENA and ESMAP as a replication reference for other sugar-growing island economies (Fiji, St. Kitts, Guyana) that are not yet using bagasse at full potential for grid electricity. Mauritius's bagasse model works because of three conditions: (1) long-term PPAs between sugar groups and CEB that provide revenue certainty for the cogeneration investment; (2) a sugar industry large enough to produce consistent bagasse volumes at scale (~3 million tonnes cane/year); (3) regulatory willingness to value bagasse's dispatchability at a premium over solar (which is firm during daylight but not on demand). The bagasse PPA structure, interconnection standards, and dispatch protocol are replicable in Fiji (where FSC bagasse is currently underutilised for grid power) and in Caribbean sugar states with residual cane industries.
2
The Rodrigues submarine cable (600 km, 40 MW, commissioning 2026) will be the Indian Ocean's most transformational island interconnection project — eliminating a 40,000-person community's 100% diesel dependency with no local RE installation required — and CEB and ADB should use the Rodrigues connection commissioning as an opportunity to redesign Rodrigues's electricity tariff to be cost-reflective from day one, avoiding the subsidy dependency that typically crystallises when island diesel tariffs are "bridged" to lower mainland-grid rates without a clear transition to cost-reflective pricing. Rodrigues currently receives electricity at a subsidy (CEB cross-subsidises Rodrigues from main island revenues). When the cable connects, Rodrigues's cost of supply drops dramatically (from ~US$0.35/kWh diesel equivalent to ~US$0.15/kWh grid equivalent). The opportunity: set the Rodrigues tariff at the true grid cost from commissioning, with a targeted low-income subsidy for households below a consumption threshold. This avoids locking in a new subsidy structure that is expensive to reform later and demonstrates to international partners that Mauritius's transition is fiscally coherent, not just technically ambitious.
3
Mauritius's coal PPA expiry schedule (2028-2032 for IPP coal plants) is the most important leverage point in its RE transition — because PPAs that expire without renewal automatically retire coal capacity without requiring active phase-out policy or compensation — and CEB and the government should resist any pressure to extend coal PPAs beyond their natural expiry, instead accelerating the solar + BESS pipeline to ensure adequate replacement capacity is commissioned before coal exits, rather than after. The temptation to extend coal PPAs to maintain supply security is the single greatest risk to Mauritius's 60% RE 2030 target. A 5-year PPA extension (say, 2028-2033) locks in 10 years of coal (the extension period plus the time to then commission replacement capacity) and makes the 60% RE target mathematically impossible by 2030. CEB's capital programming team and the Utility Regulator must jointly model the PPA expiry timeline against the solar + BESS commissioning pipeline and ensure the replacement capacity is committed (signed PPAs) at least 3-4 years before each coal plant's PPA expires.
4
Mauritius's financial services sector — managing ~US$1.4 trillion in administered funds (IFC-registered funds, offshore structures) — faces increasing ESG pressure from European and US institutional investors who require their portfolio managers to disclose and reduce Scope 2 electricity emissions, which means that Mauritius-based fund managers will increasingly seek green electricity certificates (GOs) or on-site solar to meet client reporting requirements, creating a commercial demand for RE certificates and corporate solar PPAs that does not currently exist in Mauritius's electricity market framework and that CEB and the Utility Regulator should enable through a GO issuance framework and direct corporate PPA licensing. Corporate RE PPAs and green certificates are standard in European and US electricity markets; Mauritius's financial sector clients expect the same. A Mauritius Renewable Energy Guarantee of Origin (MAREGO) framework — modelled on EU RECS (Renewable Energy Certificate System) — would allow CEB and solar IPPs to issue certificates for RE generation that financial services firms can use for Scope 2 reporting. This creates a revenue stream for RE generators (certificate premium above base tariff) and a compliance mechanism for financial services firms — a no-additional-cost RE incentive that benefits both sectors.
5
The Agalega Islands (two islands in the Indian Ocean, ~300 km north of Mauritius, ~300 Mauritius residents) are hosting a major Indian military infrastructure project (airstrip, jetty, communications) that has significantly changed the electricity demand profile of these previously minimal-infrastructure islands — and Mauritius should explicitly plan for grid-quality electricity supply to Agalega (via submarine cable or large solar + BESS) as a sovereign infrastructure obligation now that the islands have permanent Indian military presence, rather than treating Agalega electricity as a secondary priority behind the Rodrigues cable. India's construction of a 3,000 m runway and deep-water jetty on Agalega (under a 2015 agreement, construction 2021-2023) has been controversial in Mauritius (sovereignty concerns, environmental impact) and internationally (India expanding Indian Ocean military footprint). The infrastructure has brought approximately 200-400 Indian military and civilian personnel to Agalega whose electricity needs (communications, logistics, accommodation) far exceed the existing small diesel generator. Mauritius, as the sovereign authority, is responsible for civilian electricity supply on Agalega — and should plan accordingly, ensuring the civilian Agalegan population has reliable electricity independent of the military's supply arrangements.
