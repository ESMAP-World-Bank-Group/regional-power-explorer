Generation Mix (2024)

Bosnia and Herzegovina has the highest coal share of any country in the Western Balkans. In 2024, lignite accounted for approximately 62% of generation, hydro ~33%, and solar/wind the remainder. Coal generation has remained stable at ~8.5 TWh/yr despite growing EU pressure to phase out fossil fuels. The sector is split between two political entities — the Federation of BiH (FBiH) and Republika Srpska (RS) — each with its own utility, creating a uniquely fragmented governance structure.

Market Structure — The Three-Utility System

Bosnia and Herzegovina's electricity sector is legally defined by the country's post-Dayton constitutional structure. Three separate state-owned utilities operate largely independently, aligned with the two entities and the Croat political constituency within FBiH.

  • EP BiH (Elektroprivreda BiH): Largest utility; ~1,780 MW capacity (37% of national total); serves the Bosniak-majority Federation; owns Tuzla and Kakanj TPPs and the Neretva hydro cascade.
  • ERS (Elektroprivreda RS): RS-entity utility; ~1,300 MW (30%); owns Ugljevik and Gacko TPPs; strong political backing from RS government. Generated 4.7 TWh in 2024 (−16% YoY).
  • EPHZHB (Elektroprivreda HZ HB): Croat-majority FBiH areas; owns Čapljina and Mostar HPPs (~640 MW); smaller but significant in hydro.
  • NOS BiH: Independent System Operator — the nominally independent TSO. Operates transmission jointly across both entities; perpetually underfunded and politically contested.
  • DERK: State Electricity Regulatory Commission — national regulator; entity regulators (FERK and KERS) also exist, creating overlapping jurisdictions.
The three-utility structure mirrors Bosnia's political divisions. Coordination failures between EPBiH, ERS, and EPHZHB have blocked a functioning wholesale market, stalled Energy Community compliance, and made large infrastructure investments (new interconnectors, grid upgrades) politically contentious. This fragmentation is the defining governance challenge of the sector.

Coal — Energy Community's Compliance Flashpoint

Bosnia and Herzegovina is home to four large lignite power plants, all operating well beyond their originally planned lifetimes. The sector is the single largest source of emissions in the Western Balkans and has been the subject of repeated enforcement proceedings under the Energy Community Treaty for violating Large Combustion Plant (LCP) emission limits.

  • TPP Tuzla: Units 4, 5, and 7 are operational; Unit 7 (450 MW) was the subject of a controversial loan attempt from the China Development Bank. EU objected; financing collapsed. Tuzla 7 new-build was cancelled by 2024.
  • TPP Ugljevik (RS): 300 MW; among the most polluting plants per MWh in Europe by historical SOx/NOx measures; subject to Energy Community enforcement.
  • TPP Gacko (RS): ~300 MW; older plant; scheduled decommissioning repeatedly deferred.
  • TPP Kakanj: ~330 MW; partially rehabilitated; some units decommissioned.
BiH has been in persistent non-compliance with Energy Community environmental acquis on large combustion plants for over a decade. The EU has pursued infringement-style proceedings, but political deadlock between the entities has blocked coordinated responses. Coal plant operators benefit from low domestic lignite prices and lack of a functioning carbon pricing mechanism in BiH.

Renewables & Export Potential

Despite its coal-heavy grid, Bosnia and Herzegovina has significant hydropower resources and growing wind and solar deployment. The country is a net electricity exporter in most years, selling surpluses primarily to Serbia and Croatia. RE capacity reached 827 MW of wind and solar combined by end-2024.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
The three-utility structure is the root of BiH's energy governance failure. EPBiH, ERS, and EPHZHB operate as political instruments of their respective entities, blocking market reform, Energy Community compliance, and coordinated investment. Any energy sector reform requires political solutions that transcend the Dayton architecture.
2
BiH has the highest coal dependency in the Western Balkans and the worst emissions compliance record. Decade-long Energy Community enforcement proceedings have produced minimal change. Without a binding coal phase-out timeline aligned with EU standards — which would require entity-level consensus — this will remain a chronic accession obstacle.
3
The Tuzla 7 cancellation and Ugljevik's decline represent the start — not the end — of coal restructuring. No comprehensive just transition plan exists. The Tuzla region and Gacko valley are mono-industrial economies where coal closure will require sustained investment in alternative livelihoods.
4
Untapped hydro and wind potential could make BiH a significant green electricity exporter. With less than half its hydro potential developed and strong wind resources in the Dinaric highlands, BiH has a credible renewable energy future — but realising it requires governance reform and streamlined permitting that currently do not exist.
5
BiH is a structural electricity exporter today, but ageing coal plants create a supply-cliff risk in the 2030s. If coal plants close faster than RE projects are built — as Energy Community pressure intensifies — BiH could flip from exporter to importer within a decade. Managing this transition without blackouts or political crisis requires planning that has not yet started.
