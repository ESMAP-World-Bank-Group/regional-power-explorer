1. Energy context

Kazakhstan is Central Asia's largest economy and its only electricity system with significant connections to both Russia and the broader Central Asian grid. Its power sector is a direct inheritance of Soviet industrial strategy: massive coal deposits in the north (Ekibastuz, Karaganda) were developed to fuel a heavy industrial complex, and that structure persists today. Coal accounts for 75% of electricity generation — making Kazakhstan one of the most coal-intensive large electricity systems in the world. The fleet is ageing, nearly one-third of generation plants are over 30 years old, and chronic underinvestment has left the country running a structural electricity deficit covered by Russian imports.

The picture is changing. A national nuclear referendum in October 2024 returned 71.12% in favour of building the country's first nuclear plant since the 1999 decommissioning of BN-350. Rosatom was selected in June 2025. A 6.7 GW renewable energy auction pipeline was launched. And a landmark retail market restructuring took effect January 2025. Kazakhstan is simultaneously running coal flat-out to meet growing demand and betting on nuclear, wind and solar to transform the system by the mid-2030s.

  • Coal (72% cap / 75% gen): ~88 TWh in 2024. Dominated by Ekibastuz GRES-1 (4,000 MW, world's largest coal plant by single complex), Ekibastuz GRES-2 (1,000 MW), Zhambyl GRES and a network of older CHP plants. Nearly all coal is domestically mined (Ekibastuz and Karaganda basins). The fleet is large, cheap to run, but deteriorating: major unplanned outages in winter 2022–23 and 2023–24 triggered nationwide supply crises.
  • Gas and CCGT (10% cap / 10% gen): ~12 TWh in 2024. Concentrated in western Kazakhstan (Aktobe, Atyrau — gas-producing regions). Several CCGT modernisation projects underway (700 MW combined, completion 2028).
  • Hydro (10% cap / 9.5% gen): ~11 TWh in 2024. Key facilities: Bukhtarma (675 MW), Shulbinsk (702 MW), Kapchagay (434 MW) on the Irtysh and Ili rivers. Mostly run-of-river or reservoir regulation; limited further expansion potential.
  • Wind (4% cap / 3.8% gen): ~4.5 TWh in 2024. 59 wind plants with 958 MW installed — Kazakhstan has some of the best steppe wind resources globally. TotalEnergies' 1,000 MW Mirny farm (FID April 2026) will more than double current capacity when complete.
  • Solar (4% cap / 1.6% gen): ~1.9 TWh in 2024. 46 plants totalling ~1,148 MW; predominantly southern Kazakhstan (Zhambyl, Almaty regions). The capacity factor (~19%) reflects high irradiation but seasonal variability in a continental climate.
The 2022–23 winter crisis — triggered by ageing plant failures during an unusually cold snap — exposed how thin Kazakhstan's operating reserve margin has become. The government pledged $1.8 billion for repairs and modernisation over two years; the capacity gap nonetheless widened to a record 2.4 TWh import deficit in 2024.

Demand has grown at ~3–4% per year since 2020, driven by an expanding mining and smelting sector, rising residential consumption, and the arrival of large-scale cryptocurrency mining operations (which consumed an estimated 1.5–2 TWh in 2023–24 before regulatory clampdowns). Government forecasts require 26 GW of new generating capacity by 2035 — nearly equivalent to building the entire current system again. The Ministry of Energy targets eliminating the deficit by 2027 and becoming a net electricity exporter thereafter.

Retail tariffs (2025): Residential ~$0.063/kWh (integrated bill: generation + transmission + distribution + taxes). Industrial ~$0.076/kWh. Average wholesale price ~$62/MWh in 2024, down from $69/MWh in 2023 as coal plant dispatch intensified. Tariffs have been rising: maximum generation tariff ceiling was raised from KZT 8.87 to KZT 11.4/kWh in August 2024 and a 2026–2032 maximum tariff schedule was approved in December 2025.

2.3 Grid and interconnections

KEGOC operates the National Power Grid (NPG) across two primary voltage levels (500 kV and 220 kV). A defining structural feature is the north–south generation/demand split: the north (Ekibastuz, Pavlodar, Karaganda) produces 72.7% of national electricity but hosts the smaller share of demand; the south (Almaty, Shymkent) is heavily deficit. The 1998 north–south 500 kV backbone line was the Soviet-era solution; capacity bottlenecks on this corridor remain a persistent constraint on renewable integration from the resource-rich south.

2.4 Cross-border trade arrangements

Russia — the uncomfortable lifeline Structural

Kazakhstan imported 2.4 TWh from Russia in 2024 — covering the entire domestic generation deficit. This is Kazakhstan's most strategically uncomfortable energy dependency: a country with the world's largest uranium reserves and globally outstanding wind/solar resources relying on Russian coal-fired electricity to keep the lights on in winter. The government explicitly targets eliminating this import dependency by 2027 through new generation capacity (coal plant upgrades, CCGTs, and renewable auctions). Whether the timeline holds depends on delivery of several large projects simultaneously.

Central Asia trilateral — seasonal balancing Active

Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and Uzbekistan signed a 2024 trilateral electricity cooperation agreement formalising seasonal exchange: Kazakhstan provides thermal capacity to Kyrgyzstan and Uzbekistan in winter; Kyrgyz and Tajik hydro surplus flows back in spring-summer. This is the closest Central Asia has come to a functioning power pool arrangement since the Soviet era — still bilateral and protocol-based, but a foundation for the World Bank-backed REMIT regional market programme.

3. Sector organisation and actors

3.1 Market structure

Kazakhstan's electricity market is unbundled and more developed institutionally than most regional peers, reflecting 25 years of post-Soviet reform. Generation, transmission, distribution and retail supply are legally separate. The transmission natural monopoly (KEGOC) is operated under KREM's regulatory oversight. A capacity market has operated since January 2019 — generators receive payments for available capacity in addition to energy prices, a mechanism designed to incentivise investment in new dispatchable capacity.

The 2024–25 retail reform eliminated non-regulated electricity supply organisations and consolidated retail supply through 21 licensed transmission-affiliated organisations — a move that reduced market fragmentation while increasing regulatory oversight. The regulator (KREM) sets maximum generation tariffs per plant in multi-year schedules (2026–2032 schedule approved December 2025), providing investment certainty while preserving regulated return structures.

The dominant structural tension: Samruk-Kazyna, the sovereign wealth fund, owns both KEGOC (transmission monopoly) and Samruk-Energy (29.8% of generating capacity). This dual role — controlling the grid and competing in the generation market — is a fundamental conflict of interest that the ongoing reform framework has not yet resolved.

4. Key issues and dynamics

4.1 The nuclear return — from referendum to reactor

  • October 6, 2024: 71.12% of Kazakhstanis voted in favour of building a nuclear power plant — a legitimacy mandate the government needed given historical concerns (Soviet nuclear testing at Semipalatinsk). The vote was the largest popular endorsement of nuclear energy held anywhere in the world.
  • June 2025: An interagency commission selected Rosatom (VVER-1200) over French (EDF EPR-1200), Chinese (CNNC HPR-1000) and Korean (KHNP APR-1400) competitors. The selection carried explicit geopolitical weight: Kazakhstan chose its most immediate large neighbour's technology, deepening the Russian energy relationship in the same decade it is seeking to reduce Russian import dependency in power generation.
  • Project: two 1,200 MW VVER-1200 units near Ulken village on the shore of Lake Balkhash. Target commissioning: mid-2030s. At full output (~20 TWh/year), the plant would eliminate Kazakhstan's Russian electricity import dependency and potentially convert the country into a net exporter. Kazakhstan is the world's largest uranium producer — the nuclear plant would be the first large-scale domestic use of its own strategic resource.
Kazakhstan's long-term nuclear strategy envisions 3–4 plants by 2050. If all are built, nuclear could supply 20–25% of electricity demand by 2040 — transforming a coal-heavy system into one of Central Asia's lowest-carbon grids.

4.2 Renewable scale-up — 10 GW by 2030

  • Target: 15% renewable share by 2030 (from 6.4% in 2024) via 6.7 GW of renewable auctions in 2024–2027. Wind is the highest-potential resource: the northern steppe has Class 7 wind resources across hundreds of thousands of square kilometres.
  • TotalEnergies' Mirny project (Zhambyl region, FID April 2026): 1,000 MW wind + 600 MWh battery storage — the largest single renewable project in Kazakhstan's history and one of the largest in the post-Soviet space. Demonstrates that international capital can invest at scale once regulatory certainty is established.
  • The June 2024 renewable energy law introduced mandatory storage for projects above 499 MW — a requirement that will shape the economics of the next generation of large wind and solar projects but also ensure grid stability as variable RE penetration rises.

4.3 The deficit paradox — coal country running short

  • Kazakhstan is simultaneously the largest coal producer in Central Asia and the only country in the region running a chronic electricity deficit. The 2.4 TWh import gap (covered by Russia in 2024) reflects not a lack of installed capacity but a lack of available capacity: unplanned outages, fuel supply disruptions, and ageing equipment have taken ~20–25% of the fleet offline at any given time during winter peaks.
  • The path to deficit elimination by 2027 requires all of the following simultaneously: ~700 MW of new CCGT capacity (Zhambyl, Aktobe, Atyrau), completion of Ekibastuz GRES-3 unit 1, Balkhash IPP reaching financial close, and the renewable auction pipeline delivering on schedule. Any delay in any component extends the Russian import dependency.
  • Cryptocurrency mining, which grew rapidly in 2021–23 as miners fled Chinese crackdowns, added an estimated 1.5–2 TWh of unscheduled demand. The government imposed licence requirements and consumption limits on mining operations in 2023–24, reducing but not eliminating the sector's load impact.

4.4 Market reform and the Samruk-Kazyna tension

  • Kazakhstan has operated a capacity market since January 2019 — one of only two post-Soviet electricity systems (with Russia) to have implemented this mechanism. Generators receive annual capacity payments for certified available MW, creating investment incentives independent of spot energy prices.
  • The unresolved structural issue: Samruk-Kazyna controls both KEGOC (the monopoly grid operator) and Samruk-Energy (generating 33.8% of national output). A sovereign fund that simultaneously sets grid access terms and participates in the generation market creates vertical integration conflicts even in a formally unbundled structure. Separation of these ownership stakes has been discussed but not acted upon.

5. Five things to take away

1
Coal generates 75% of Kazakhstan's electricity and its dominance will not end quickly — the fleet is large, cheap to operate, and politically entangled with mining employment. But the trajectory is set: no new coal plants are under construction, and nuclear and renewables are the two declared pillars of the next generation of supply.
2
The October 2024 nuclear referendum (71% YES) and June 2025 Rosatom selection represent Kazakhstan's most consequential energy policy decision in a generation. Two 1,200 MW VVER-1200 units near Ulken will produce ~20 TWh/year when operational in the mid-2030s — enough to eliminate the Russian import dependency and convert Kazakhstan into a net electricity exporter.
3
The 2.4 TWh Russian import dependency is Kazakhstan's most strategically uncomfortable position — the world's largest uranium producer importing electricity from Russia. The deficit-elimination target (2027) is achievable only if multiple large projects deliver on schedule simultaneously.
4
The renewable pipeline is real: TotalEnergies' 1 GW Mirny wind farm (FID April 2026) + 6.7 GW in auctions 2024–2027. Kazakhstan's northern steppe has globally outstanding wind resources. The grid absorption constraint — a system built for dispatchable coal — is the binding limit on how fast the transition can go.
5
Samruk-Kazyna's dual ownership of both the grid operator (KEGOC) and the dominant generator (Samruk-Energy) is a structural conflict of interest that the reform framework has not yet resolved. Until these ownership stakes are separated, the market's competitive signals will remain distorted by the sovereign fund's vertically integrated interests.
