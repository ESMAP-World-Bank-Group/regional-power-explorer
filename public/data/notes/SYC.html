Generation Mix & Sector Overview

The Seychelles — 115 islands in the western Indian Ocean, with a population of approximately 100,000 and a GDP per capita of approximately US$18,000 (the highest in Sub-Saharan Africa and Indian Ocean SIDS) — is a high-income small island state whose electricity sector lags significantly behind its economic development level in RE penetration. Despite excellent solar irradiation (~2,000 kWh/kWp/year), steady south-east trade winds, and the financial capacity to invest in grid modernisation, Seychelles generates approximately 83% of its electricity from imported diesel/HFO — one of the higher diesel shares among middle- and high-income SIDS globally. The transition gap between economic capacity and actual RE deployment is the defining paradox of Seychelles's energy sector.

The Public Utilities Corporation (PUC), a state utility, operates Mahé Island's grid (~85 MW) — where approximately 90% of the population lives — plus separate smaller grids on Praslin (~7 MW) and La Digue (~2 MW), and minimal off-grid systems on outer islands (most outer islands are uninhabited nature reserves or very small fishing communities). Mahé's grid: diesel/HFO plant at Roche Caiman (~70 MW effective), approximately 14 MW of solar PV (PUC utility solar at Perseverance and Cap Ternay; growing rooftop programme), a 1.5 MW wind installation, and a 5 MW BESS (ADB/GEF 2023). Praslin: diesel + 1.5 MW solar. La Digue: the standout — approximately 700 kW solar + wind + 500 kWh BESS, operated as a near-100% daytime RE grid with diesel backup only for evening and low-irradiation periods — demonstrating that Seychelles's islands can achieve near-100% RE when scaled appropriately.

World's first blue bond (2018): ocean financing innovation with energy implicationsIn 2018, the Republic of Seychelles issued a US$15M "blue bond" — the world's first sovereign bond specifically designed to finance ocean-positive activities (sustainable fishing, marine protected areas, fisheries management) — with the World Bank providing partial guarantees and technical support. The blue bond was listed on the London Stock Exchange and attracted institutional investor demand that exceeded the initial offering. The blue bond's relevance to energy: it established that Seychelles can access international capital markets for environmental finance, and the "blue economy" framework it represents — where ocean-based sustainable development is explicitly financed — logically extends to clean maritime energy (offshore wind, tidal, wave) and to reducing the emissions from diesel generation that threaten the coral reef ecosystems that underpin Seychelles's tourism and fisheries economy. PUC and the Seychelles government have not yet issued an energy-specific sustainability bond, but the blue bond precedent makes this straightforward.
La Digue: proof-of-concept for 100% daytime RE on Seychelles islandsLa Digue (population ~3,000, 10 km²) is Seychelles's third-most-populated island — famous for its giant granite boulders, bicycle-based transport (no cars), and Anse Source d'Argent beach. PUC electrified La Digue with a diesel generator until 2012, when a wind turbine (100 kW) was installed as a pilot. Between 2018-2022, GEF and ADB financed solar panels (600 kW), a 500 kWh BESS, and grid upgrades that together enabled La Digue to operate at near 100% RE during daylight hours (diesel only after dark and in overcast periods). La Digue's energy transition is the most successful outer island RE case in Seychelles and a template for Praslin (next in scale) and for the 30+ inner islands that have small residential communities. The La Digue model works precisely because the island is small enough (2 MW peak demand) that a 600 kW solar + 100 kW wind + 500 kWh BESS system can supply all daytime demand with no grid stability concerns — a scale advantage that Mahé (~85 MW) does not have without substantially larger BESS.
Tourism premium and the electricity quality paradoxSeychelles receives approximately 450,000 tourists annually — primarily European ultra-premium visitors to resorts on Mahé, Praslin, and the outer islands (North Island, Fregate, Denis, Bird). These resorts charge US$1,000-5,000/night; their guests expect absolute reliability, immaculate environments, and increasingly, demonstrable sustainability credentials. The electricity paradox: guests paying US$3,000/night for a "nature luxury" experience at a resort claiming environmental leadership are often unknowingly powered by HFO-fired diesel generators (PUC grid) or resort-owned diesel gensets. The reputational gap between "Seychelles as blue economy champion" and "Seychelles as 83% diesel grid" is increasingly noted by sustainability-conscious travel journalists and ESG-focused tour operators. This reputational pressure — not just climate policy — is a driver of Seychelles RE investment: resorts increasingly want to be able to certify "100% RE electricity" to their guests, which requires either rooftop solar or RE certificates from PUC that PUC cannot currently issue.
Debt-for-nature swap (2021) and climate finance innovationIn 2021, Seychelles completed a debt-for-nature swap: The Nature Conservancy (TNC) raised philanthropic capital to purchase Seychelles's external debt at a discount, and in exchange Seychelles committed to expanding its marine protected area (MPA) from 0.04% to 30% of its Exclusive Economic Zone (1.37 million km², one of the largest MPA networks in the world). The debt-for-nature swap freed budget resources that were previously committed to debt service — some of which has been directed to conservation and climate resilience. This structure — philanthropic capital reducing sovereign debt in exchange for conservation commitments — is a template that could be extended to energy: a "debt-for-energy" swap where Seychelles's remaining debt is partially purchased in exchange for commitments to accelerate the RE transition, closing the gap between Seychelles's blue economy narrative and its diesel-dependent electricity reality.

The 15% RE Target and Its Inadequacy

Seychelles's NDC (2022) commits to 15% RE by 2030 — a target that has already been nearly met (17% in 2024, though the NDC baseline was 2019 when RE was ~8%). A 15% RE target for a high-income island state with excellent wind and solar resources, a demonstrated La Digue success case, strong international capital market access, and a reputation as a global ocean and climate leader is widely viewed as inadequate — by Seychellois civil society, international donors, and comparative analysis against SIDS peers. PUC has internally modelled a pathway to 50% RE by 2030 (with 40 MW additional solar + 20 MW BESS); the financial modelling is positive (BESS + solar delivers lower-cost electricity than diesel at current fuel prices). The gap is not economic or technical but political: transitioning away from HFO diesel at the rate required for 50% RE means accelerating retirement of PUC's diesel plant capital before end of technical life — a balance sheet impairment that PUC and the government are reluctant to formally recognise.

Seychelles has every ingredient for a rapid and complete RE transition — high income, strong solar and wind resources, demonstrated outer island RE success at La Digue, access to international climate finance, and the world's most powerful branding case ("blue bond," debt-for-nature, 30% MPA) for green finance. The 15% NDC target is not a ceiling — it is a floor that political economy considerations have kept artificially low. The case for Seychelles raising its NDC ambition to 50%+ is essentially risk-free: the economics are positive, the technology is proven, and the reputational benefit to Seychelles's global ocean leadership positioning would be significant.

Five Strategic Takeaways

1
Seychelles should raise its NDC RE target from 15% to at least 40-50% by 2030 — based on PUC's own internal modelling showing positive economics for 40 MW solar + 20 MW BESS — because maintaining a 15% target that was essentially achieved before 2025 is inconsistent with Seychelles's global leadership positioning on ocean sustainability and climate finance, and the gap between Seychelles's "blue economy champion" narrative and its 83% diesel electricity reality is increasingly visible and damaging to that positioning in international forums. AOSIS, where Seychelles has been an active voice, now includes SIDS that have achieved 50-100% RE on modest incomes. Seychelles's high income, strong resource, and blue finance track record make a 15% target indefensible in comparative SIDS context. The political economy barrier (PUC diesel asset write-downs) should be addressed through a structured early retirement package (WB or EIB balance sheet support for PUC stranded asset write-down) rather than by maintaining an artificially low RE target.
2
The La Digue model — 600 kW solar + 100 kW wind + 500 kWh BESS, near 100% daytime RE on a 2 MW demand island — should be immediately replicated on Praslin (~7 MW) with appropriately scaled RE and BESS, which PUC's engineering team has the capacity to execute and which ADB can finance under the existing Pacific (Indian Ocean) energy programme, with Praslin's transition demonstrably achievable within 2-3 years and providing the precedent for scaling the model to Mahé. Praslin (population ~8,000, ~7 MW peak demand) needs approximately 3.5 MW solar + 3 MW BESS to achieve 60-70% daytime RE. Cost: approximately US$12-15M. Praslin's RE transition would eliminate approximately 1,000 tonnes of CO₂/year, reduce PUC's diesel logistics cost to Praslin by 40%, and create a second demonstrated success case that politically enables the Mahé RE scale-up. ADB's current Seychelles programme scope includes a Praslin component — it should be accelerated to commissioning by 2027.
3
Seychelles's luxury resort sector — which generates the foreign exchange and tourism income that funds the economy — has a direct financial incentive to drive PUC's RE transition faster than PUC's own capital planning would generate, because resorts charging US$3,000+/night can credibly market "100% solar electricity" as a premium amenity that justifies price premiums, and PUC should develop a voluntary green tariff mechanism (resorts pay a premium over standard commercial tariff in exchange for RE certificates and PUC RE investment commitment) that monetises this commercial demand for green electricity without requiring government subsidy. A voluntary green tariff at 10-15% premium above standard commercial rate, offered to any resort that commits to 3-year offtake, would fund PUC solar investment at a rate that makes RE expansion commercially self-financing. The resort sector's demonstrated willingness to pay for environmental credentials (they already pay Six Senses/Four Seasons-level sustainability certifications) makes this instrument viable. ADB or IFC could structure the first round as a green bond issued by PUC, with resort offtake agreements as the security.
4
Seychelles's debt-for-nature swap model (2021, TNC, US$21M) should be extended to an energy-specific "debt-for-energy" swap structure where international climate philanthropists and DFIs purchase a tranche of Seychelles's remaining sovereign debt in exchange for Seychelles committing to specified electricity sector RE milestones (e.g., 40% RE by 2028, 70% RE by 2030), with the freed debt service funds directed to PUC's RE capital programme — creating a self-reinforcing feedback loop between climate finance and energy transition on the model already demonstrated in Seychelles's own debt swap history. The 2021 debt-for-nature swap reduced Seychelles's debt service by approximately US$2M/year (redirected to conservation). A "debt-for-energy" swap of comparable scale (US$20-30M debt purchase, US$2-3M/year freed service) directed to PUC's solar+BESS capital budget would contribute materially to the Mahé RE scale-up. The structure is familiar to Seychelles's Ministry of Finance (they executed the TNC swap), to WB (which guaranteed it), and to the international impact investor community. ESMAP should facilitate the structuring conversation between WB, TNC/Conservation Finance Network, and Seychelles.
5
PUC's aging HFO diesel plant at Roche Caiman (~70 MW, 1990s-2000s vintage) faces a capital maintenance decision within 5-8 years — major overhaul vs. replacement — and this decision is the most consequential investment choice in Seychelles's electricity future: a diesel overhaul extends HFO dependency to 2035-2040, while an RE+BESS replacement commits Seychelles to 70-80% RE by 2032 at lower long-run cost, and ADB should pre-position the financing for the RE replacement option before PUC's internal engineering team recommends a diesel overhaul on least-disruptive grounds. Capital asset decisions in utilities are often made by the engineering team on operational grounds (a diesel overhaul is "known," predictable, and procurable without new regulatory approval) rather than on financial or climate grounds. PUC's Roche Caiman overhaul vs. replacement decision will be made within the next 5-8 years — possibly as soon as 2027-2028 if plant condition deteriorates. ADB should now prepare a "Roche Caiman replacement with RE+BESS" feasibility study, with a committed financing term sheet, so that PUC management has a credible RE alternative to evaluate against the diesel overhaul — not just an aspiration.
